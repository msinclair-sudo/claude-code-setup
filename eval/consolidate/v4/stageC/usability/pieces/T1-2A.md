# Predicting microbial communities at places the model has never seen

Suppose you measure temperature, pH and nutrients at a patch of soil or a wastewater plant and want to predict the microbes living there. A community can be described two ways. A taxonomic profile lists who is present and in what proportions, at some level of the tree of life, from individual sequence variants up to phyla. A functional profile lists what the community can do: the genes, pathways or traits it carries. A recurring idea, functional redundancy, holds that many different microbes can do the same job. If so, environment might select for the job more reliably than for whoever does it, and the functional profile should be easier to predict.

The test that counts is prediction at sites, habitats, studies or times that played no part in building the model. Scoring a model on the data it was fitted to tells you how much environment explains, which is an easier question.

## The direct comparison has not been made

A systematic search of the literature found no study that predicts a measured functional profile (genes or pathways read from a metagenome, the sequenced DNA of the whole community) alongside a taxonomic profile from the same samples, and compares the two at held-out units.

What exists are three studies in which the functional profile was derived from taxonomy: each detected genus is assigned functions from a lookup of what that genus is known to do. It is a regrouping of the taxonomic data, not an independent measurement of function. The three studies disagree.

Averill et al. (2021) fitted models of soil bacterial and fungal groups to global surveys and tested them on an independent survey spanning the continental USA (NEON), a test of transfer between studies. They concluded that functional groups and high-level taxonomic groups were "equally predictable". Their supplementary data, compared with phyla at the scale of whole sites, splits by kingdom. Bacterial functional groups had a median R² of 0.15 against 0.27 for bacterial phyla; fungal functional groups had 0.23 against 0.12 for fungal phyla. A 2026 reanalysis of the Averill and NEON data (Aouabed et al.; abstract only) reports that bacterial functional groups failed under every machine-learning model on Averill's data while fungal functional groups reached R² 0.45 on NEON.

Werbin et al. (2024, not yet peer reviewed) forecast 170 soil groups at NEON sites and found functional groups more predictable than most taxonomic groups, but scored that comparison on later dates at the same sites used to fit the models, where a group's own recent history contributes to the forecast. Bacterial functional groups gained skill this way, through persistence, while being less sensitive to environment than bacterial taxa; fungal functional groups were more sensitive than fungal taxa. At 13 new sites, bacteria lost far more skill than fungi (a probabilistic error score worsened by 65% against 8%), a result reported by kingdom rather than profile type. Seasonality alone matched or beat six environmental predictors for most groups.

Liu et al. (2023) predicted composition in 777 activated-sludge samples from 269 plants in 23 countries. Five derived functional groups, such as nitrifiers, reached R² of 33 to 57%; taxonomic levels reached 33 to 42%. The test set was a random 20% of samples, so one plant could sit on both sides of the split.

Derived function thus sits below bacterial phyla in a cross-study test, above most taxa at familiar sites, and level or higher in a random split. Every hold-out design differs, so the disagreement cannot be pinned on one cause.

The only held-out study of measured genes found was Faure et al. (2021): 228,914 protein clusters across 93 Tara Oceans metagenomes, mean test R² 0.28, with ocean region (Longhurst province) the top predictor in 43% of models. It had no taxonomic comparison, no baseline and random test samples.

Outside microbes the evidence is mostly explanatory. In streams, trait composition matched environment more closely than species composition for Danish stream plants, invertebrates and fish (Göthe et al. 2017) and for diatoms (Soininen et al. 2016). That pattern rests on abstracts, centres on stream invertebrates studied by a linked group of authors, and finds little echo on land: across more than a million vegetation plots, community trait axes were only weakly associated with climate and soil (Bruelheide et al. 2018). Explanatory microbial studies split. Some find functional structure bound to environment while taxonomy within functional groups is not (Louca et al. 2016; Nelson et al. 2016); others find function tracking taxonomy closely (Fierer et al. 2012; Galand et al. 2018).

## Resolution

For taxonomic profiles, the held-out studies examined show no consistent shape with rank. In Averill's data bacterial skill peaked at order (median R² 0.51) with phyla lowest, and fungal medians wandered from phylum to genus (0.12, 0.01, 0.11, 0.13, 0.08), although the authors state that fungal predictability rises with rank, perhaps from a summary not consulted here. Werbin found bacteria most predictable at class. Liu saw no clear trend. Mod et al. (2021), at 255 Swiss Alpine soil sites, found that how finely sequences were grouped made no difference. Two 2026 reports, the Aouabed reanalysis and a study predicting from airborne canopy reflectance, lean towards broader groups predicting better.

Rank is tangled with abundance. Broader groups are more abundant, and in Werbin's data abundance was the strongest correlate of predictability. None of these studies separates the two.

On the functional side, no held-out study compared single genes with pathways or broad categories, and none compared the two profile types at matched levels.

## What makes prediction work or fail

Abundance is the most consistent factor. Commoner, more widespread taxa were predicted better in Swiss soils, activated sludge and NEON soils, and rare taxa worse, as in plant and animal models. In activated sludge, taxa with high immigration rates under a neutral community model were predicted worse.

How the test is built can matter as much as the biology. Benchmarking 33 models on five plant and animal datasets, Norberg et al. (2019) found extrapolation much harder than interpolation; the prediction task explained 29% of variation in performance, model choice 33% and data properties 38%. Flexible models overfit. In the Swiss soils a boosted-tree model correlated about 0.97 with its fitting data and 0.21 at held-out sites; a simpler model, 0.53 and 0.26. Most microbial studies split data at random, which flatters transfer.

The skill measure matters too. An ordinary R² rewards predictions that rise and fall with the observations even when they are consistently too high or too low; R² against the 1:1 line asks whether predicted equals observed. In Averill's test the two diverge sharply. The lignin-degrading bacterial group scored 0.96 on the first and 0 on the second. Median 1:1 R² at site scale was zero for every category except fungal functional groups (0.11), although about 21 of 130 individual groups beat zero. No microbial study scored both profile types against the plainest baseline, predicting the average profile everywhere.

Derived profiles add their own variable: groups defined from literature plus genomic evidence were predicted better than those from literature alone (Werbin). And derived function is not measured function: in agricultural soils, 16S-derived function matched measured enzyme patterns qualitatively but not quantitatively (Breitkreuz et al. 2021).

## Where that leaves the question

Whether function is easier to predict than taxonomy at new places remains open. Settling it needs a study that predicts measured metagenomes and taxonomy from the same samples, holds out whole sites or regions, scores both against a mean-profile baseline, and compares them at matched levels of resolution. For freshwater, sediments and engineered systems beyond activated sludge, no held-out microbial study of either profile type turned up.
