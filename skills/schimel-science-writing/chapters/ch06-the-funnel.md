# Chapter 6: The Funnel: Connecting O and C

## Core Idea
The body of the Introduction is the funnel of the hourglass: it connects the broad Opening to the specific Challenge by narrowing from general to specific and framing a concrete knowledge gap, convincing readers that to make progress on the big problem they must answer your narrow question.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Funnel (O → C)**: The main body of the Introduction narrows from the large problem (Opening) to the specific question (Challenge), building the argument that the two are logically connected.
  - When to use: every Introduction; the central structural move between O and C.
  - How: (1) state the large problem (O); (2) add background that flows from it, introducing key characters; (3) at each step narrow scale while naming what we need to know; (4) define a concrete knowledge gap; (5) close with the specific question (C). Include only material that has a concrete role in moving the story forward.
  - Why it works: taps the SUCCES formula's U and E — unexpectedness ("I didn't realize we didn't know that!") and emotion/curiosity ("what's the answer?"). Failure mode: dumping everything you know instead of developing logical connections step by step.
- **Framing the Knowledge Gap**: Unmask a specific hole in the wall of knowledge rather than reviewing what's known.
  - When to use: throughout the funnel, to set up the Challenge.
  - How: use existing literature to define the *edges* of knowledge; state precisely what is *not* known in concrete language; show why filling that gap matters.
  - Why it works: concreteness creates curiosity; "sources are not clearly defined" beats "little is known about X."
- **The Bizzwidget Problem**: Don't sell a solution before defining the problem.
  - When to use: diagnostic for proposals/papers that lead with their method or product.
  - How: open with a concern the audience shares, show why the problem needs solving, *then* introduce your specific solution (the Challenge) — "have you experienced problem X? You have no solution? Let me show you one."

## Key Concepts
- **The funnel**: the narrowing body of the Introduction that connects Opening to Challenge.
- **Knowledge gap**: the specific, concrete hole in current understanding the paper will fill.
- **OCAR vs. ABDCE flow**: OCAR papers use a smooth O → C funnel with background flowing from the opening; ABDCE (and most proposals) open with action, then back up to fill background (B) before development.
- **"Little is known about X"**: a fuzzy, unconvincing gap statement — false scientifically, self-contradicting logically, non-concrete linguistically.
- **Data dump**: piling up facts without synthesizing them into a story or gap; a sign of an unfinished draft.
- **Literature review vs. Introduction**: a review builds a solid wall (describes knowledge); an Introduction describes the hole in the wall (describes ignorance).
- **Citation placement**: "Smith (2003) found X" (researcher as subject) signals a data dump; "X occurs (Smith 2003)" (nature as subject) tells the story.
- **Bizzwidget community**: the small in-group who already value your solution; selling only to them loses the wider audience.

## Mental Models
- Use the **funnel** when you need to justify a narrow study from a broad problem (e.g. global climate → rate constant of one reaction).
- Use the **hole-in-the-wall test** when deciding what literature to cite: cite papers that define the *edges* of knowledge, not the core.
- Use the **citation-placement test** when revising: if sentences start "Smith found…," ask whether the researcher or the research is the point; usually rewrite to make nature the subject.
- Use the **bizzwidget check** when your draft introduces a method early: have you first convinced the reader they have a problem?

## Anti-patterns
- **"Little is known about X"**: false (hundreds of studies usually exist), self-contradicting (you then list much that *is* known), and fuzzy (how little is little?).
- **Offering a solution before a problem**: like a door-to-door salesman pushing a Bizzwidget — only the in-group buys; everyone else closes the door.
- **Introduction as literature review**: synopsizing what's known builds a wall instead of revealing the gap; fails on curiosity.
- **Telling everything you know**: background without a concrete role in the story is a data dump.

## Worked Example
Schimel dissects the Introduction of Vaghjiani & Ravishankara's 1991 *Nature* paper (Example 6.1) — an extreme funnel from global warming down to the rate constant k₁ of a single reaction (OH + CH₄). He numbers the moves: {1} opening frames methane and the greenhouse effect (wide audience); {2}–{3} introduce the second character, the OH radical, then back up to fill background — making it an ABDCE line (the first sentences are A, this is B); {4} lays the gap base: methane "sources are not clearly defined"; {5} establishes importance (we need this to manage CH₄); {6} since CH₄ sinks (reaction with OH) are measurable, flux can be estimated from losses, bringing OH back; {7} narrows to the molecular scale — we need the rate constant k₁; {8} closes the gap: only three studies measured k₁ at realistic (low) temperatures, only one near the right range. {9} The Challenge — "We studied reaction 1…" — Schimel calls a dud: it should have been concrete ("We measured k₁ down to 230 K…"). His verdict: the paper succeeded anyway — "you don't need to be perfect to be successful."

## Key Takeaways
1. Treat the Introduction body as a funnel: narrow step by step from O to C, naming what we need to know at each step.
2. Define the gap concretely — write "X is not clearly defined," never "little is known about X."
3. Sell the problem before the solution; open with a shared concern, then offer your Bizzwidget.
4. Write an Introduction (the hole), not a literature review (the wall); cite papers that bound knowledge.
5. Flip "Smith found X" to "X occurs (Smith 2003)" so nature, not the researcher, is the subject — except for live debates or a lone researcher challenging dogma.
6. Cut any background fact that has no concrete role in moving the story forward.
7. By the Challenge, readers should feel your question is the obvious one — even if they'd never thought of it.
