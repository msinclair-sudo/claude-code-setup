# The nitrogen bill: what fertiliser costs to make, and what soil microbes can pay

Synthetic nitrogen fertiliser starts as ammonia, made by the Haber-Bosch process. Atmospheric nitrogen (N₂) is combined with hydrogen at high temperature and pressure, and the product is ammonia, which carries 82% nitrogen by mass. Most of the energy and almost all of the carbon enter before the synthesis, when the hydrogen is stripped from natural gas or coal by steam reforming, so the feedstock largely decides a plant's footprint.

## Energy and carbon at the factory gate

A modern natural-gas plant needs roughly 28 GJ per tonne of ammonia. The global average is higher, near 37 GJ, because older and coal-fed plants are still running; coal-based production in China runs to about 59 GJ per tonne. In the 1950s plants needed about 55 GJ.

Fertiliser is accounted per tonne of nitrogen, and one tonne of nitrogen requires about 1.21 tonnes of ammonia. That puts the energy cost of a tonne of fertiliser nitrogen at roughly 34 GJ on the best gas route and roughly 45 GJ at the global average.

Carbon dioxide follows the feedstock. Efficient gas plants emit close to 1.6 t CO₂ per tonne of ammonia. A process-optimisation study using a global mix that includes coal reports about 2.8 t. The ecoinvent life-cycle inventory, a standard reference database, sits in between at 1.92 t CO₂-equivalent per tonne of liquid ammonia. Fitting carbon capture to a gas plant can bring the figure down to about 0.79 t. Converted to nitrogen, manufacture emits about 1.9 to 3.4 t CO₂-equivalent per tonne of N, the low end for efficient gas plants and the high end for coal-heavy production. Independent industrial-ecology and biogeochemistry sources arrive at these ranges (Tock et al. 2015; Demirhan et al. 2019; Razon 2014; Tunå et al. 2014), and they are the firmest numbers in this piece.

These are cradle-to-gate figures. They leave out the nitrous oxide (N₂O) released from soil after the fertiliser is spread, which is the larger term over the life cycle.

## Three shares, three questions

How much of the world's greenhouse-gas emissions nitrogen fertiliser accounts for depends on where the system boundary is drawn. Three figures circulate, each answering a different question, and quoting one in place of another is the commonest error in this literature.

Manufacturing alone is the smallest slice. The International Fertilizer Association attributed 0.47 Gt CO₂-equivalent to nitrogen fertiliser production in 2007, about 0.93% of anthropogenic emissions, and independent process studies put ammonia manufacture at roughly 1% to 1.8%.

Widen the boundary to the whole synthetic-nitrogen supply chain, adding transport and field emissions, and the total for 2018 comes to 1.13 Gt CO₂-equivalent: 2.1% of global emissions and 10.6% of agricultural emissions (Menegat et al. 2022). Within that total, manufacture contributes 38.8%, transport 2.6%, and emissions from fields after application 58.6%.

Widen it again to the full life cycle of nitrogen fertiliser, manure included, and the share rises to about 5% of global emissions, with roughly two-thirds arising after the fertiliser is deployed (Gao and Cabrera Serrenho 2023). The same study puts manure at about 23 t CO₂-equivalent per tonne of nitrogen, some 1.9 times an average synthetic fertiliser, because so much of manure's nitrogen is lost.

Once the boundary takes in the field, the field outweighs the factory. Gao and Cabrera Serrenho name nitrogen-use efficiency, the fraction of applied nitrogen that ends up in the crop, as the single most effective lever for cutting fertiliser emissions.

## Can soil microbes stand in for the factory?

Could soil microbes reduce how much synthetic nitrogen farmers need to apply? The evidence splits cleanly, depending on which microbes are meant.

**Legumes and their rhizobia.** When farmers deliberately add a nitrogen-fixing symbiosis, by growing legumes whose root nodules host rhizobia, the reduction in synthetic input is real and direct. The fixed nitrogen substitutes for applied nitrogen in the legume itself and partly enriches the crop that follows. In one organic, legume-based rotation in Europe, fixation supplied 87% of all nitrogen inputs, and the rotation matched conventional productivity while leaving a nitrogen surplus of only 21 kg per hectare per year, against 40 to 100 kg for fertilised systems (Anglade et al. 2015).

Two things bound the saving. The share of a legume's nitrogen that comes from fixation varies from a few percent to nearly all, depending on species, the mineral nitrogen already in the soil, and competition. And fixation is suppressed by the very input it might replace: applying 125 to 250 kg of fertiliser nitrogen per hectare cut fixation rates by 81% to 86% (Ladha et al. 2022). Fertiliser and fixation partly cancel each other, so a heavily fertilised legume does little fixing.

**The resident community.** The more ambitious claim is that the microbes already living in a soil, if they cycled nitrogen more efficiently, would lower the synthetic requirement. Here the support is weak and indirect: three lines of evidence bear on it, and none tests it head-on.

Free-living and associative nitrogen fixers, bacteria that fix nitrogen outside a nodule, are everywhere, but their measured contribution in cereal systems is modest, rarely large enough to displace a defined fertiliser rate at the same yield. Engineering cereals to fix their own nitrogen has not been achieved.

Microbial inoculants, sold as biofertilisers, do raise yields on average. The largest meta-analysis, covering 171 field studies, found gains of 8.5% in continental climates, 10.0% in oceanic, 14.9% in tropical and 20.0% in dry climates, with effects often inconsistent and dependent on soil phosphorus and climate (Schütz et al. 2018). But these trials measure extra yield at a given fertiliser rate, whereas the substitution question needs the other experiment: how much fertiliser can be withheld at constant yield.

Mineralisation, the microbial release of nitrogen from soil organic matter, does feed crops, but only about 1% to 4% of soil organic nitrogen is mineralised in a season, and that alone is seldom enough for modern cultivars to reach their maximum yield (St. Luce et al. 2011). Measuring a field's nitrogen supply can trim fertiliser rates, but what does the work there is the measurement, not any demonstrated efficiency of the microbes.

Underneath all three sits an attribution problem. A lower fertiliser need co-varies with soil organic matter, moisture and rotation, and so does the microbial community. No study in the review separated microbial efficiency from those soil and management variables as the cause of a lower requirement at held yield.

## The question the review did not ask

The strongest evidence for microbes reducing synthetic nitrogen concerns legumes, a management choice; claims for the resident community remain unproven. That verdict covers substitution only. It says nothing about a different and arguably more consequential question: whether the composition and state of the soil community govern how much of the nitrogen that is applied gets taken up by the crop and how much is lost to leaching, denitrification and N₂O. Field emissions are the largest part of the footprint, so that is where microbes could matter most. It was not researched, and the weak result on substitution is no answer to it.

A caution on provenance: these findings come from one literature review whose reading of its sources was not checked by a second reader. The manufacturing figures rest on several agreeing studies; the microbial findings mostly rest on one source each.
