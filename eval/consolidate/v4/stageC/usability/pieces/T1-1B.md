# Predicting microbial communities at sites the model has never seen

Ecologists increasingly want to forecast what lives somewhere from the conditions there: fit a model linking community make-up to climate, soil or water chemistry at surveyed sites, then predict sites, years or surveys the model never saw. That last step is the real test, since a model can describe its own data well and still fail somewhere new.

For microbes, community make-up can mean two things. A taxonomic profile records which organisms are present and in what proportions, usually by sequencing a marker gene (16S rRNA for bacteria, the ITS region for fungi). A functional profile records what the community can do: the genes it carries, or traits such as nitrogen fixation. Functional profiles come in two kinds. A measured profile counts genes directly in metagenomes, the sequenced DNA of the whole community. A derived profile is built from the taxonomic one, by looking up which genera are known to perform a function and summing them. It is taxonomy regrouped, so it can never be fully independent of it.

Many expect function to be the easier target. Unrelated microbes often do the same job, so species can turn over while the community's capabilities stay put and follow the environment more faithfully. For prediction at new sites, the evidence is thin, and what exists disagrees.

## The direct comparisons

No study I could draw on compares a measured gene profile with a taxonomic profile from the same samples at held-out sites. Three compare derived functional groups with taxa, all microbial.

Averill and colleagues (2021) calibrated models of soil bacterial and fungal groups on global surveys and tested them on an independent survey of US soils, the National Ecological Observatory Network (NEON). They concluded that functional groups and high-level taxonomic groups were "equally predictable". Medians recomputed from their supplementary data, at site scale, split by kingdom. Bacterial functional groups scored lowest of any bacterial category (median R² 0.15, against 0.23 to 0.51 across ranks from phylum to genus). Fungal functional groups scored highest among fungi (0.23, against 0.01 to 0.13).

Werbin and colleagues (2024, a preprint not yet peer reviewed) forecast 170 soil groups at NEON sites, testing on a later period and on 13 new sites. Functional groups beat most taxonomic groups, but only at the calibration sites on later dates. There, bacterial functional groups gained from temporal autocorrelation (a group abundant last season tends to stay abundant) and were less sensitive to environment than bacterial taxa. At new sites, bacteria lost far more skill than fungi: forecast error worsened by 65%, against 8%.

Liu and colleagues (2023) predicted activated-sludge communities in 269 wastewater plants worldwide. Functional groups such as nitrifiers reached 33 to 57% of variance explained, against 33 to 42% across taxonomic levels. The test set was a random fifth of samples, though, so the same plant could sit in both training and test data.

A 2026 machine-learning reanalysis of the Averill and NEON data (Aouabed and colleagues, seen only as an abstract) reports bacterial functional groups failing under every model and fungal ones reaching R² 0.45. Read together, derived function gives bacteria no clear advantage and may transfer worse; fungi may gain somewhat. The test designs all differ, so the disagreement has no single explanation.

The one held-out study of measured genes has no taxonomic counterpart. Faure and colleagues (2021) predicted about 229,000 marine protein families across 93 Tara Oceans samples; mean test R² was 0.28. Swiss soil bacteria, predicted taxonomically at 255 sites, reached a median correlation of about 0.26 at held-out sites (Mod et al. 2021). Different measures, so the two can't be ranked.

## Why the expectation persists

Most support for function comes from explanatory studies, which ask how much of each profile environment accounts for within one dataset, with no held-out test. In streams, trait composition often matches environment more closely and varies less than species composition, though that rests mainly on invertebrates studied by one linked group of researchers. Across more than a million vegetation plots, plant traits were only weakly tied to climate and soil (Bruelheide et al. 2018). Microbial results split. In the global ocean, environment strongly structured derived functional groups but barely structured which taxa filled them (Louca et al. 2016), and bacteria on seaweed surfaces shared about 70% of their functions but only 15% of their species (Burke et al. 2011). Yet across 16 soils, functional and taxonomic differences between sites moved together (Fierer et al. 2012), and at a Mediterranean coastal site function tracked taxonomy (Galand et al. 2018).

Low variability also cuts both ways. A profile that barely changes between sites is easy to predict, but the average profile predicts it almost as well, so a model may add little. In large European rivers, models of invertebrate trait profiles reportedly gained negligibly over the average profile at test reaches (Statzner et al. 2005, as described in a later review). No microbial study has scored both profile types against that kind of baseline.

## Resolution

Taxonomic rank shows no consistent shape. In Averill's recomputed medians, bacteria peak at order and score lowest at phylum, while fungal medians bounce between ranks. Werbin found fungi best at phylum and class and bacteria best at class. Two 2026 studies, the Aouabed reanalysis and one predicting NEON soil microbes from aircraft-measured canopy reflectance, lean towards broad groups predicting best. In Swiss soils, changing how finely sequences were clustered made no difference. Rank is also tangled with abundance: broader groups are more abundant, and abundance was the strongest correlate of predictability in Werbin's data. An apparent gain from coarsening may simply be a gain from commonness.

Functional resolution has no held-out evidence at all. Nobody has tested whether single genes, pathways or broad categories predict better. The nearest hint is explanatory: in soil metagenomes, narrow functions carried by few taxa are less redundant than broad ones (Chen et al. 2022), which suggests narrow functions behave more like taxa.

## What makes prediction succeed or fail

**Commonness.** Abundant, widespread taxa were predicted better in Swiss soils, activated sludge and NEON; rare ones poorly.

**How the test set is drawn.** Random splits let close neighbours of training samples into the test set, which flatters the score; blocked or extrapolating splits are harder. In a benchmark of 33 community models on plant and animal data, the type of prediction task explained 29% of the variation in performance (Norberg et al. 2019). Most microbial studies used random splits. Flexible models make it worse: a boosted-tree model of Swiss soil bacteria correlated about 0.97 with its training data and 0.21 at new sites.

**The skill measure.** Averill reported two kinds of R². The best-fit version rewards predictions that rise and fall with the observations even when the values are off; R² against the 1:1 line requires the values themselves to be right. Under the second, median skill was zero for every group except fungal functional groups; lignolytic bacteria scored 0.96 on the first and 0 on the second. The authors link the bias partly to compositional data, where proportions must sum to one. The ordering of functional against taxonomic groups changed with the measure.

**Time.** Forecasting next season at a known site borrows from autocorrelation, which does not travel to new sites.

**Derived versus measured.** Werbin's functional groups predicted better when defined from genomic pathway evidence as well as the literature. In agricultural soils, derived functions matched measured enzyme activity in direction but not in magnitude (Breitkreuz et al. 2021), so results on derived profiles need not hold for measured genes.

The study that would settle the question has not been done: measured genes and taxa from the same samples, tested at spatially separate sites, scored on an absolute measure against an average-profile baseline. Until it is, the case for function rests on fit within datasets.
