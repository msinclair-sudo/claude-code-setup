## What the research found, by question

### C-PREDICT

**D1. Where taxonomic and gene or trait profiles were both predicted at held-out sites, habitats, studies or times, how did their predictive skill compare, in either direction, and how does the comparison depend on the hold-out design? Report same-study comparisons separately from comparisons assembled across studies.**

*Answer, as corrected by a second reader.*

The body supports the headline: no same-study held-out comparison of a measured metagenomic gene or pathway profile against a measured taxonomic profile was found by the review's routes. The three same-study held-out comparisons all use functional profiles derived from taxonomy, and they disagree. Averill et al. 2021 must be stated more exactly. The authors' 'equally predictable' claim (abstract, verified verbatim by the critic) compares functional groups with HIGH-LEVEL taxonomic groups, not with all ranks. Against phylum at site scale, the supplement (recomputed by the critic, matching the review's numbers) gives a split by kingdom: bacterial functional median best-fit R2 is 0.15 against 0.27 for phyla, fungal functional is 0.23 against 0.12 for phyla. So the reviewer's reading departs from the authors for bacteria only, not for both kingdoms. Averill's Extended Data Fig. 1, a within-NEON 70/30 random cross-validation of functional and taxonomic groups under the 1:1 R2, and Extended Data Fig. 2, coefficients of variation across groupings, went unreported. Werbin et al. 2024 (preprint) and Liu et al. 2023 stand as described, with their designs as the review labels them. A 2026 reanalysis the review missed (Aouabed et al., Scientific Reports, abstract read by the critic only) fits several ML models to the Averill and NEON data. It reports that bacterial functional groups failed under every model on Averill's data, and that fungal functional groups reached R2 0.45 on NEON. This is further derived-profile, same-data evidence, and its hold-out design is unverified. The explanatory freshwater pattern (trait composition more aligned with environment and less variable than taxonomic composition) rests entirely on abstracts. It is concentrated in stream macroinvertebrates, from a closely linked set of authors (Statzner, Dolédec, Usseglio-Polatera, Bonada), and Bruelheide et al. 2018 bounds it in plants. It is moderate at best and should not be called replicated across organism groups. Dolédec 2011 and Hoeinghaus 2007 are about how consistently responses hold across regions, not how closely a profile fits environment. Statzner et al. 2005 is a trait-only result, with no taxonomic counterpart in the same study. It is not a trait-versus-taxon comparison, and its held-out status comes from the secondary account in Hawkins 2010.

**D2. How does held-out predictability change with the level of resolution on each side: from strain or sequence variant to phylum for taxonomic profiles, and from gene to broad functional category (or from individual trait to trait syndrome) for gene or trait profiles? Where a study compares the two profile types at several levels, how does the comparison change across levels?**

*Answer, as corrected by a second reader.*

For microbial taxonomic rank, the body supports 'no consistent monotonic shape across the studies reviewed', with the caveats as stated (single studies, confounding with abundance). The review missed two reports. Aouabed et al. 2026 (abstract only) say predictability rises with taxonomic scale across several ML models fitted to the Averill and NEON data. A 2026 New Phytologist study predicting soil microbes at NEON from airborne canopy reflectance (abstract only; its predictors are remote sensing, not direct environmental measures) found broad microbial groups predicted best. Both lean towards broader ranks predicting better and weaken the review's 'no direction' reading. Neither separates rank from abundance. For bioassessment, the Hawkins et al. 2000 abstract compares species and family models on 'how well models predicted the taxa found at unimpaired sites', and the comparison is confounded with the Pc threshold choice. That is not confirmed as held-out, so it is not a held-out species-versus-family result. The Moss et al. 1999 abstract gives no comparison between levels. For the functional hierarchy (gene to category), no held-out evidence was found, and the body supports that. No study compared the two profile types at matched levels.

**D3. Which properties of either profile type have been shown to raise or lower its held-out skill, how does the choice of skill measure and baseline shift the comparison, and how have studies accounted for these properties? Report each property with the profile type it was shown to affect and the direction of the effect.**

*Answer, as corrected by a second reader.*

Supported: higher abundance and occurrence frequency go with higher held-out skill for taxonomic profiles in three microbial studies (Mod 2021 verified by the critic in full text: GAMnb median correlation about 0.53 in calibration and 0.26 in prediction, GBM about 0.97 and 0.21). Random splits flatter transfer relative to blocked or extrapolative splits (SDM literature). Measure dependence rests on Averill alone: the critic confirmed that median site-scale 1:1 R2 is 0 for every group except fungal functional groups, but about 21 of 130 groups have a positive 1:1 R2 (for example Bacteroidetes 0.50), so 'almost every group had no skill' is a median-level statement. Corrections: Van Sickle et al. 2005's SDs (0.18 against 0.20; 0.13 against 0.28) are O/E precision across the reference sites used to build the models (verified in the abstract). They are not held-out skill over a null, and should be labelled calibration-site evidence. Statzner et al. 2005's negligible gain over a mean model is verified in the abstract, but whether that comparison was made at held-out test reaches is not stated there, so it rests on the secondary account in Hawkins 2010. The claim that trait profiles 'vary little across reference sites, so a constant predicts them' is the reviewer's inference. The Statzner abstract itself reports that many trait categories changed along 13 rivers. That claim should be removed or labelled as the reviewer's reading. The properties shown on derived functional profiles (the rule used to define groups, autocorrelation) remain single source (the Werbin preprint). None have been shown on measured gene profiles.

### taxonomic, measured gene and derived functional profiles

- The comparison the research asked for predicts both a taxonomic profile and a gene or trait profile from environment and scores both at units not used in fitting; a measured gene profile is metagenomic genes, pathways or categories, while a derived functional profile is obtained from taxonomy by assigning genera to functions (genus-to-function lookup). (the review's definitions)
  Holds for: the review's terms.

### hold-out design and transferability

- Hold-out design changes apparent skill: Norberg et al., benchmarking 33 stacked and joint SDMs on five plant and animal datasets, found extrapolation much harder than interpolation, and the prediction task explained 29% of variance in performance, model variant 33% and data properties 38%. (Norberg et al. 2019)
  Holds for: five plant and animal datasets, 33 stacked and joint species distribution models.
- Wenger and Olden found machine-learning models with excellent in-sample fit transferred poorly under blocked cross-validation. (Wenger and Olden 2012)
  Holds for: machine-learning ecological models under blocked cross-validation.
- Random splits overstate transfer relative to blocked or extrapolative splits; in Norberg et al. the prediction task explained 29% of variance in performance. (Norberg et al. 2019; Wenger and Olden 2012)
  Holds for: the species distribution model literature.
  Evidence: replicated; rated "Moderate" by the research.
- Maguire et al. 2016 found both species and community models failed across distant, climatically novel periods; its abstract also reports that community-level models generally outperformed SDMs in novel climates (transferring better than species models) and did not overfit. (Maguire et al. 2016)
  Holds for: distant, climatically novel periods; taxonomic profiles only.
- Zurell et al. 2020 found stacking choices mattered more than model type. (Zurell and others 2020)
  Holds for: stacking choices; taxonomic profiles only.
- Microbial held-out studies mostly used random splits: random, not site-blocked, splits in Liu 2023, Faure 2021 and Frémont 2022, and random splits of whole sites, not spatially blocked, in Mod 2021; Fuhrman et al. 2006 evaluated in-sample; and Werbin's functional-versus-taxonomic contrast is a within-site temporal test. (Werbin et al. 2024; Liu et al. 2023; Faure et al. 2021; Fremont et al. 2022; Mod et al. 2021; Fuhrman et al. 2006)
  Holds for: microbial held-out prediction studies.
- The clearest microbial failure bounds found are the one spatial transfer result in Werbin (bacteria losing 65% of CRPS skill at new sites) and the zero median site-scale 1:1 R2 in Averill's cross-study test, where that median is 0 for every group except fungal functional groups, though about 21 of 130 groups have a positive 1:1 R2. (Averill et al. 2021; Werbin et al. 2024)
  Holds for: soil microbes, NEON; new-site transfer (Werbin) and cross-study transfer (Averill).
  Evidence: limited by access (a source was not read in full; not the same as weak evidence).

### skill measures and baselines

- The continuous ranked probability score (CRPS) is a proper scoring rule for probabilistic forecasts that take the form of predictive cumulative distribution functions; it generalises the absolute error. (Gneiting and Raftery 2007)
  Background, not from the research.
- Averill et al.'s supplementary table reports two measures of out-of-sample skill: R2 of the best-fit line between predicted and observed, and R2 against the 1:1 line. (Averill et al. 2021)
  Holds for: Averill et al. 2021 Supplementary Data 1.
- Skill measure and baseline can reverse or erase apparent skill: under the two measures in Averill et al. the orderings change, with high correlation alongside zero 1:1 skill; in microbes this measure-reversal finding rests on Averill et al. 2021 alone. (Averill et al. 2021)
  Holds for: soil microbes, cross-study transfer (Averill).
  Evidence: rated "Weak (single study)" by the research.
- No microbial study reported skill over a mean-profile or persistence baseline for both profile types. (the review's search)
  Holds for: microbial held-out prediction.
  Evidence: a search result.
  The research does not say whether this was searched for.

  Holds for: 13 new NEON sites against the 30 calibration sites.
  Evidence: a single source; limited by access (a source was not read in full; not the same as weak evidence).

  Holds for: soil microbes at NEON.
