# Chapter 17: Putting it All Together: Real Editing

## Core Idea
Real writing problems never come one at a time — broken story arcs travel with weak linkages, prepositional phrases with passive verbs, jargon with nominalizations — so editing means making multiple passes with all your tools, working top-down from structure to language via the **SCFL** sequence, cycling back as fixes expose new problems.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **SCFL (Structure → Clarity → Flow → Language)**: The top-down editing sequence. Fix big structural issues first, then work down into finer details of word use and style.
  - When to use: Editing any rough first draft into polished prose.
  - How: (S) Get the story's structure in shape — make sure OCAR elements are in place; (C) ensure ideas are clear and concrete; (F) make ideas flow, linking one thought to the next; (L) make it sound good.
  - Why it works / failure mode: You can't do just four clean passes — the elements overlap. Clarifying language may force you to rethink structure, so you repeat SCFL several times. If the topic is unclear you can't even define structure, and unclear topics usually come from weak language.
- **Multiple-pass cyclic editing**: Each pass solves one or two problems and often unmasks others; so edit again, and again, cycling back to the top of SCFL.
  - When to use: Always — "few of us are good enough editors to see all the way from clunky start to graceful end."
  - How: After fixing language, return to Structure and re-ask the big questions (e.g., "can I kill this sentence now?").
- **Read it aloud (secret weapon)**: Close the door, print a clean copy, stand up, step away, and read it out loud. Your eyes skip over awkward expressions and broken flow; your ears do not.

## Key Concepts
- **SCFL**: Structure–Clarity–Flow–Language editing order, top-down.
- **OCAR**: Opening–Challenge–Action–Resolution arc; even a single sentence can carry distinct OCAR functions across its phrases.
- **Topic–stress linkage**: The stress of one sentence must connect to the topic of the next; broken stress→topic linkage = no flow.
- **Subject–verb connection**: Verb should follow its subject quickly; a 12-word subject feels disconnected even when technically adjacent to the verb.
- **Nominalization**: Action buried in a noun ("adjustments") or adjective ("responsive") instead of an active verb.
- **Pawn push vs. launching a queen**: A general, positioning opening sentence is a pawn push; cutting straight to the strong specific claim launches a queen.
- **Concrete**: Telling the reader not just where something happens but what it is (physiology at the organelle, architecture at the canopy).
- **Compact arcs**: Packaging a sentence into a few little phrases, each doing one OCAR job.

## Mental Models
- Use **SCFL top-down** when a draft has tangled multiple problems — fix structure before polishing words, because structure makes the rest easier.
- Use **cyclic re-passing** when one fix exposes another — don't expect a single right answer; writing is experimentation and revision (Ch 1).
- Use the **topic–stress test** when two sentences feel disconnected — diagram each sentence's topic and stress and check the stress→topic handoff.
- Use **read-aloud** when the prose still feels clunky after silent editing — the ear catches what the eye glides past.

## Anti-patterns
- **Trying to fix everything in one pass**: Problems overlap and hide each other; a single sweep can't reach graceful prose.
- **Leaving the action in a nominalization/adjective**: "Adjustments... exemplify" hides that *plants adjust and respond* — depersonalized, abstract, no "old lady screaming."
- **Stress→topic gap**: Second sentence meant to illustrate the first but sharing no link words — kills flow.
- **Over-trimming into a noun train**: Cutting "changes in architecture" to "canopy-level architecture changes" creates a worse problem; sometimes condense the *change* words instead.

## Worked Example
Schimel revises a 42-word, two-sentence paper opening down to 19 words. Original:
> "Plant behavior in response to dynamic resource availability... has been well studied. Adjustments made at the organelle to canopy level... exemplify how responsive plants are..."

- **Structure**: The passive "has been studied" makes the point ambiguous — history of science or biology? The author picks **B (biology)** with an active verb following its subject: "Plants use a range of approaches to respond to varying resource availability." Drops "such as changing light regimes" to leave the key idea in the stress position. (A pawn push.)
- **Clarity + Flow**: Sentence 2 has no stress→topic link to sentence 1, and its action sits in the nominalization "adjustments" and adjective "responsive." Activate the verb and connect to the prior stress: *"For example, as light varies, plants modify light interception by mechanisms that range from organelle-level physiological shifts to canopy-level changes in architecture."* "For example" + "as light varies" bridges the gap; "plants modify" makes it concrete.
- **Language**: Still clumsy — repeated "light"/"level," prepositional "shifts in," noun-train risk. Condense the change-words and reword: *"For example, as light varies, plants modify its interception by mechanisms working at different scales, from organelle physiology to canopy architecture."* Three compact arcs, each doing an OCAR job (open / action / resolve), with a parallelism in the stress position.
- **Cycle back to Structure**: If the paper is only about varying light, kill the pawn-push first sentence and launch a queen: *"As light varies, plants modify its interception by mechanisms working at different scales, from organelle physiology to canopy architecture."* — 19 words, none wasted.

## Key Takeaways
1. Edit top-down: get the story and OCAR right before touching word choice — SCFL.
2. Expect to loop. Each pass solves one or two problems and exposes others; cycle back to Structure.
3. Diagram topic and stress to test flow; the stress of one sentence must feed the topic of the next.
4. Hunt nominalizations and passives — put the action into an active verb that follows its subject.
5. Make it concrete: say *what* the thing is, not just where it is.
6. After fixing the language, re-ask structural questions — you may now be able to cut a whole sentence (kill the pawn, launch the queen).
7. Read the final draft aloud, standing, on a clean printout — your ears catch what your eyes miss.
