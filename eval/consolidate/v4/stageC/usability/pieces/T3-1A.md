# Where the nitrous oxide goes: denitrification in constraint-based metabolic models

Denitrification is a chain of four reductions. Bacteria that respire nitrate in place of oxygen strip it down one step at a time: nitrate (NO₃⁻) to nitrite (NO₂⁻), nitrite to nitric oxide (NO), nitric oxide to nitrous oxide (N₂O), and nitrous oxide to dinitrogen gas (N₂). Each step has its own enzyme. Nitrate reductase (Nar or Nap) does the first, nitrite reductase (NirK or NirS) the second, nitric oxide reductase (Nor, encoded by *norBC*) the third, and nitrous oxide reductase (NosZ) the last. Taking two nitrate ions all the way to one molecule of N₂ consumes ten electrons.

The last step decides how much N₂O escapes. If NosZ is missing or held back, the chain stops one rung short and N₂O leaves the cell instead of passing through. So a model of denitrification can be asked two things: does it keep the four steps apart, and does it report N₂O as an output in its own right? For constraint-based models the answer, as of mid-2026, is partly, and only in a neighbouring corner of the nitrogen cycle.

## How a constraint-based model would hold the steps

A constraint-based model, usually run by flux balance analysis (FBA), describes metabolism as a network of reactions with fixed stoichiometry and no enzyme rates. It asks which combination of reaction rates (fluxes) keeps every metabolite in balance within a few bounds, and then picks one by linear optimisation, most often the combination that maximises biomass production. Kinetic models work the other way: they write a rate equation for each reaction and integrate those equations through time.

Nothing stops FBA from carrying denitrification as four separate reactions. Each reductase becomes its own reaction, each draws electrons from a shared donor pool (NADH or quinones) and each can be coupled to proton pumping. The nitrogen oxides become internal metabolites, and an "exchange" reaction lets any of them leave the cell. In principle, N₂O output is then just the flux through its exchange reaction.

## What has been built: nitrifiers, not denitrifiers

The models that do this are of ammonia-oxidising bacteria such as *Nitrosomonas europaea*. These organisms also carry part of the denitrification chain, reducing nitrite to NO with NirK and NO to N₂O with NorB, a route called nitrifier denitrification. *N. europaea* has no *nosZ* gene, so for this organism N₂O is the end of the line.

Perez-Garcia and colleagues (2014) built a stoichiometric network of *N. europaea* energy metabolism, 51 reactions and 44 metabolites, and used FBA to calculate NO and N₂O production through two routes: nitrifier denitrification, and a second route through hydroxylamine oxidation. N₂O comes out as a distinct, quantified output, and predicted yields were on average within 15% of 17 measured yield ratios.

The mechanism they settled on was electron imbalance. In their words, "N2O is produced due to electron flow imbalances in the cell." Nitrifier denitrification switches on "when the availability of electron donors exceeds that of electron sinks". When oxygen runs short, the cell sheds surplus reducing power onto nitrogen oxides. A 2016 follow-up widened the network to eight species of ammonia and nitrite oxidisers; there, simulated oxidation of NO back to nitrite reduced N₂O formation by 60% without changing how much nitrite the culture made.

Mellbye and colleagues (2018) took the idea to a two-species community, joining genome-scale reconstructions of *N. europaea* and the nitrite oxidiser *Nitrobacter winogradskyi* with abiotic reactions that oxidise NO. The model tracks NO, NO₂ and N₂O separately and ties N₂O to gene content: the trace N₂O that *N. winogradskyi* makes was left out because its genome has no known nitric oxide reductase gene. The coculture emitted more nitrogen oxides because together the two organisms drew oxygen down. It is the only community-scale constraint-based model found that reports N₂O.

So these models do separate steps and report N₂O. But they cover only the nitrite-to-N₂O half of the pathway, in organisms that cannot finish it, and N₂O is an overflow valve for excess electrons rather than the result of a throttled four-step chain.

## What the complete denitrifiers' models leave out

Genome-scale models exist for several organisms that do run the whole pathway: *Paracoccus denitrificans* (the textbook complete denitrifier, with Nap, NirS, Nor and NosZ), *Thauera* sp. MZ1T, *Pseudomonas stutzeri* and *Pseudomonas aeruginosa*. The genes for every step are there, yet none of these models reports N₂O as an output. They were built and tested for growth yields, polymer production, pollutant breakdown and anaerobic growth, with denitrification treated as a route to N₂ and energy. One automatically generated draft model of *P. stutzeri* lacked a working anaerobic nitrate respiration chain altogether.

Community models fare no better. They estimate fluxes and the metabolites organisms swap, and no case was found that resolved the four steps with N₂O as a community output. Gut community reconstructions include nitrate and nitrite reductases and stop there.

Part of the reason is the method. Balancing electrons and charge in the cytochrome and nitrogen-oxide reactions is error-prone; a heavily re-curated *N. europaea* model from 2022 shows how much hand-correction it took. And a model that maximises biomass has no built-in reason to waste nitrogen as a by-product; predicting overflow of any by-product usually needs extra constraints. Getting N₂O out of a complete denitrifier would mean adding a separate exit for each intermediate, coupling each reductase explicitly to its electron donor, and capping NosZ flux to mimic, say, copper limitation or slow enzyme induction. Nobody has published that.

## Kinetic and trait-based models already do it

Stepwise N₂O accumulation lives in other modelling families. The Activated Sludge Model for Nitrogen, from wastewater engineering, runs all four steps with separate rate parameters for each. Electron-competition models let the four steps compete for electrons and use that competition to predict when N₂O accumulates. Cybernetic models add dynamic regulation of the enzymes, and one has offered a possible mechanism for the delayed enzyme response seen in denitrification. A kinetic model of *Agrobacterium tumefaciens* includes Nap, NirK and NorB with transcriptional and kinetic regulation, and reproduces NO and N₂O release when the pathway is only partly expressed during a shift from oxic to anoxic conditions.

Trait-based ecosystem models step back from single cells. Work by Zakem and colleagues (2024) on ocean oxygen-minimum zones treats denitrification as modules (NO₃⁻→NO₂⁻, then on to N₂O, then N₂, leaving NO aside) carried by different microbial types, each defined by the energy it gets from its step and the biomass it can build. N₂O cycling falls out of competition between types: one reduces nitrate to nitrite, another reduces N₂O to N₂. Ocean biogeochemical models have traditionally collapsed denitrification into one step. This work splits it apart and is the clearest example of N₂O arising from pathway gaps spread across a community.

These approaches trade one thing for another. Kinetic and cybernetic models need rate parameters and regulatory rules for each step; they deliver time courses and accumulation. FBA needs a genome and a stoichiometric network but no rates, and it gives a distribution of fluxes. So far it delivers N₂O only where electron overflow forces it out.

## Where that leaves things

A constraint-based model would count as having closed the gap if three things held at once: nitrate, nitrite, nitric oxide and nitrous oxide reductases sit as separate, mass- and charge-balanced reactions; N₂O leaves as its own product; and the stated cause is a missing or capped NosZ, or a division of labour between organisms, rather than electron overflow alone. No such model had been published by June 2026. That rests on a thorough but finite literature search, some of whose methods sections could not be read in full. For now, stepwise N₂O from a denitrifier means a kinetic, cybernetic or trait-based model.
