## Core Frameworks & Mental Models

Everything in the book reduces to **two roots**: **OCAR** (story structure) and **SUCCES** (stickiness). When any rule feels arbitrary, trace it back to one of these.

### OCAR — the master story structure (Ch 4)
Every unit — paper, section, paragraph, sentence — should carry four elements in sequence:
- **Opening** — the driving problem + characters + target audience
- **Challenge** — the specific question / knowledge gap
- **Action** — the work done or proposed (Methods, Results, most of Discussion)
- **Resolution** — how understanding changed; *close the loop* back to the opening (a spiral, not a circle)

A story missing any element is "slippery, not sticky." A paper with no resolution "falls into the reject bin." **IMRaD is a rule; OCAR is a principle** — honor OCAR inside the IMRaD layout.

### Choose structure by audience patience (Ch 4)
- **OCAR** → specialist journals (patient; front-loading looks like you forced the data)
- **LDR** (lead → develop → resolve) → generalist journals (Nature/Science); editor triages
- **ABDCE** (action-first) → proposals & mystery/adventure; hook fast
- **LD** (inverted pyramid; whole story in the lead) → the most impatient reader

For proposals and Nature/Science: **if you haven't told them by the end of page 2, you haven't told them.**

### SUCCES — engineer stickiness (Ch 3, from Heath & Heath)
**S**imple (find the *core* — not simplistic, which dodges it) · **U**nexpected (frame a knowledge gap, don't dump data) · **C**oncrete (ground abstractions) · **C**redible (unbroken chain: prior work → methods → data → conclusions) · **E**motional (engage *curiosity*, the one acceptable scientific emotion) · **S**tories (build from modular sub-stories).

### The knowledge gap (Ch 3, 6, 7)
Frame the **unknown inside the known** — "frame a window." Identifying the gap creates curiosity; filling it creates novelty. **Lead with the question, not the answer.** Showing off what you know kills curiosity.

### Topic & Stress — sentence-level OCAR (Ch 12, 13, Joseph Williams)
- **Topic position** (sentence start) → old/familiar info; *never* put a new technical term here.
- **Stress position** (end of main clause) → the new idea, key term, main message.
- **Flow**: the **stress of sentence N feeds the topic of N+1** ("reach back and grab"). Stress→topic = story; topic→topic = a list.
- Map **Subject-Verb-Object onto Opening-Action-Resolution**.

### Energize the verbs (Ch 14)
Hunt the three emasculators: **passive voice, fuzzy verbs (is/makes/performs), and nominalizations** ("adjustment," "the regulation of"). Restore the real action as an active verb following its subject: **actor → action → acted-on**.

### SCFL — the editing sequence (Ch 17)
Edit top-down and cyclically: **S**tory → **C**lear → **F**luent → **L**anguage. Fix the story before the words (polishing sentences you may cut is wasted). Each pass exposes new problems — cycle back to Structure. Read the final draft **aloud, standing, from a clean printout**.

### Dealing with limitations: "but, yes" (Ch 18)
Raise limitations **early**, constrain conclusions to fit, then end on the science. "Yes, but" makes "more research is needed" your lasting message. Often the cheapest fix is **revising the question** to match what the method can answer.

### Premises (Ch 1, 21)
You are a **professional writer**; success = being **cited**, not published. **Writing is rewriting** — draft fast and ugly, invest in revision. **It is the author's job to make the reader's job easy.**
