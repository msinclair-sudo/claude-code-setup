# Denitrification in metabolic models: where nitrous oxide shows up, and where it doesn't

Denitrification is how many bacteria keep respiring when oxygen runs short. They hand electrons to nitrogen oxides instead, in four steps, each run by its own enzyme. Nitrate reductase (Nar or Nap) turns nitrate (NO₃⁻) into nitrite (NO₂⁻). Nitrite reductase (NirK or NirS) turns nitrite into nitric oxide (NO). Nitric oxide reductase (Nor, encoded by *norBC*) turns NO into nitrous oxide (N₂O). Nitrous oxide reductase (NosZ) finishes the job, turning N₂O into dinitrogen gas (N₂). Taking two nitrate ions all the way to N₂ uses ten electrons.

N₂O is the product that appears when the chain stops one step short. If NosZ is missing, inhibited or slow, N₂O leaves the cell instead of becoming N₂. A model can only say anything about that if it keeps the four steps apart and lets N₂O come out as a product in its own right.

## What a constraint-based model is

Flux balance analysis (FBA) belongs to the constraint-based family. These models start from a stoichiometric network: a list of reactions, each with fixed proportions of what goes in and what comes out, usually reconstructed from an organism's genome (a genome-scale model). Instead of integrating rate equations through time, FBA uses linear programming to find the pattern of reaction fluxes through the network that best serves an objective, commonly maximising biomass production or uptake of the energy substrate.

On paper, denitrification fits this format easily. Each reductase becomes a separate reaction balanced against a pool of electron donors (NADH or quinones), and each nitrogen oxide becomes a metabolite that an exchange reaction can drain out of the cell. Whether N₂O then shows up as an output depends on whether anything pushes flux that way.

## Where FBA does report N₂O

Constraint-based models that report N₂O as a distinct output do exist, but they come from ammonia-oxidising bacteria, not classic denitrifiers. *Nitrosomonas europaea* gets its energy by oxidising ammonia, yet it also carries a short denitrification branch: nitrite to NO by NirK, and NO to N₂O by NorB. This is called nitrifier denitrification. The organism has no *nosZ* gene, so N₂O is necessarily where its branch ends.

Perez-Garcia and colleagues (2014) built a network of *N. europaea* energy metabolism, 51 reactions and 44 metabolites, and used FBA to quantify NO and N₂O made by two routes, the hydroxylamine-oxidation pathway and nitrifier denitrification, with each step tied to its enzyme. Its estimates deviated on average by 15% from 17 measured yield ratios. The main finding is about why N₂O appears at all. Nitrifier denitrification switches on "when the availability of electron donors exceeds that of electron sinks": when oxygen is limiting, the cell has more reducing equivalents than oxygen can take, and nitrogen oxides absorb the surplus. In the authors' words, "N2O is produced due to electron flow imbalances in the cell." A 2016 follow-up widened the network to the nitrogen pathways of eight ammonia- and nitrite-oxidising species, and found that oxidising NO back to nitrite cut N₂O formation by 60% without changing how much nitrite the culture produced.

Mellbye and colleagues (2018) took this to community scale, joining genome-scale reconstructions of *N. europaea* and the nitrite oxidiser *Nitrobacter winogradskyi* and tracking NO, NO₂ and N₂O as separate species. Emissions rose in coculture because the nitrite oxidiser's oxygen demand, added to that of ammonia oxidation, left the environment microaerobic. N₂O production follows gene content: *N. winogradskyi* is observed to make a little N₂O, but its genome has no known nitric oxide reductase gene, so the model gives it none.

The steps are separate, then, and N₂O is a distinct readout. The cause, though, is electron overflow under oxygen limitation, with N₂O as a fixed end point. It is not a full four-step chain stopping short.

## Complete denitrifiers: steps present, N₂O not read out

Genome-scale models exist for several bacteria that carry all four steps: *Paracoccus denitrificans* Pd1222 (972 genes, 1,371 reactions), *Thauera* sp. MZ1T, two models of *Pseudomonas stutzeri* A1501 and two of *Pseudomonas aeruginosa*. The denitrification genes are in them, but none was built or tested to report N₂O. The *Paracoccus* model, for a textbook complete denitrifier, was validated on biomass yields from carbon sources, including with nitrate as the electron acceptor. The *Thauera* model acknowledges that the organism makes nitric and nitrous oxide, but was used to predict polymer production and pollutant breakdown. The *Pseudomonas* models treat denitrification as a route to N₂ and energy for growth without oxygen.

Two difficulties sit behind this. Balancing the cytochrome and nitrogen-oxide reactions for mass and charge is error-prone; a 2022 re-curation of the *N. europaea* model suggests how much hand work that lineage needed. And an FBA model that maximises biomass does not naturally predict that a cell will spill a by-product such as N₂O; that takes extra constraints.

Community constraint-based models have not filled the gap either. They infer fluxes and the metabolites members exchange, and none was found that resolves the four steps with N₂O as an output.

## Kinetic and trait-based models

Models that resolve all four steps, with N₂O as an intermediate that can accumulate, are mostly kinetic: a rate equation for each reaction, integrated through time. The Activated Sludge Model for Nitrogen (Hiatt and Grady 2008), from wastewater engineering, gives each step its own parameters. Electron-competition models make the four steps compete for a shared electron supply and predict N₂O accumulation from that contest. Cybernetic models (Song and Liu 2015; Song et al. 2017) add dynamic enzyme regulation, which offers a possible mechanism for delayed enzyme responses. Kampschreur and colleagues (2012) modelled *Agrobacterium tumefaciens* with transcriptional and kinetic regulation, and reproduced NO and N₂O emission from unbalanced expression of the pathway as cells moved between oxic and anoxic conditions.

Trait-based ecosystem models come at the question from the community side. Ocean biogeochemical models have traditionally collapsed denitrification into one step, though many microbes carry only part of it. Zakem and colleagues (2024) split it into modules (nitrate to nitrite, nitrite to N₂O, N₂O to N₂, leaving NO out) and assigned them to functional types using redox thermodynamics, biomass-yield traits and resource competition. N₂O cycling then emerges from who carries what: one type reduces nitrate to nitrite, another reduces N₂O to N₂.

## How they compare

Constraint-based models are built from genomes and enforce mass and electron balance, so they say which steps an organism can carry out and what its electron budget forces. Where they emit N₂O, electrons are overflowing in an organism with no route past it. N₂O building up in a complete denitrifier has other causes: NosZ missing or inhibited, steps competing for electrons, regulation lagging, or the pathway split across organisms. Kinetic models carry the rates and regulation; trait-based models carry the division of labour.

What a literature search up to June 2026 did not find is a balanced genome-scale or community FBA model with each of the four reductases as a separate reaction, N₂O as an exchange or accumulating product, and a pathway gap as the cause (NosZ absent or capped, or steps split between organisms) rather than electron overflow alone. Building one would mean taking a complete-denitrifier model such as *Paracoccus* or *Thauera*, adding an exchange reaction and explicit electron-donor coupling for each step, then capping NosZ flux to mimic copper limitation or regulatory lag.

That absence comes from a thorough but finite search, so a very recent or unpublished model could exist. The line between constraint-based and kinetic also blurs in hybrids such as dynamic FBA and cybernetic models; here models are sorted by their core engine, optimisation over a stoichiometric network or integration of rate equations.
