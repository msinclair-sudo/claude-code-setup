# Where the nitrous oxide goes: denitrification in flux balance models

When oxygen runs short, many bacteria pass their spare electrons to nitrate instead. Denitrification does this in four steps, each run by its own enzyme: nitrate (NO₃⁻) to nitrite (NO₂⁻) by nitrate reductase (Nar or Nap), nitrite to nitric oxide (NO) by nitrite reductase (NirK or NirS), nitric oxide to nitrous oxide (N₂O) by nitric oxide reductase (Nor, from the *norBC* genes), and nitrous oxide to dinitrogen gas (N₂) by nitrous oxide reductase (NosZ). N₂O is the step that matters here: a cell or community that stops at the third step releases N₂O instead of N₂.

Kinetic, trait-based and constraint-based metabolic models all represent this chain, but only the first two have so far been used to show N₂O building up when the chain is cut short.

## What a flux balance model is

Flux balance analysis (FBA) treats a cell's metabolism as a network of reactions with known stoichiometry. At steady state, every internal metabolite must be produced as fast as it is consumed. Those mass balances constrain the possible reaction rates (fluxes), and linear programming then picks the flux pattern that best serves a stated objective, usually maximum growth (Orth et al. 2010). The model has no rate equations. It answers "what can this network do, given its parts and a goal?" and leaves timing and regulation aside.

In principle the four denitrification steps fit this frame easily. Each becomes its own reaction, drawing electrons from the cell's donor pool (NADH and quinones) and coupled to proton pumping. But the steady-state rule has a consequence for N₂O. An internal intermediate cannot pile up, so for N₂O to leave the cell as a product the model needs an explicit exchange reaction to drain it, and some reason for flux to take that route. Maximising growth rarely supplies one: FBA with a biomass objective does not naturally predict by-product spillover without extra constraints.

## The one place FBA reports N₂O: nitrifier denitrification

A recent literature review found constraint-based models that report N₂O as a distinct output in one setting only. That setting is not classic denitrification. It is nitrifier denitrification in ammonia-oxidising bacteria, which use part of the pathway: NirK takes nitrite to NO, and NorB takes NO to N₂O. *Nitrosomonas europaea* has no *nosZ* gene, so in these organisms N₂O is necessarily a dead end.

Perez-Garcia et al. (2014) built a 51-reaction network of *N. europaea* energy metabolism and used FBA to track NO and N₂O made by two routes, hydroxylamine oxidation and nitrifier denitrification. Each conversion was a separate reaction tied to its enzyme. The model's estimates deviated on average by 15% from 17 reported yield ratios under non-limiting oxygen and ammonium. Its central finding was about electrons. Nitrifier denitrification switches on "when the availability of electron donors exceeds that of electron sinks", and NO and N₂O are emitted when their combined production outruns half the rate at which cytochrome P460 oxidises NO. In other words, when oxygen is scarce the cell has more reducing power than places to put it, and N-oxides act as an overflow valve.

A 2016 extension covered the nitrogen pathways inferred from the genomes of eight ammonia- and nitrite-oxidising species. In its simulations, oxidising NO back to nitrite cut N₂O formation by 60% without changing nitrite output (Perez-Garcia et al. 2016).

Mellbye et al. (2018) took the approach to community scale. They joined two genome-scale reconstructions, one for *N. europaea* and one for the nitrite oxidiser *Nitrobacter winogradskyi*, added abiotic NO oxidation in gas and liquid, and ran the coculture as a dynamic FBA. NO, NO₂ and N₂O are tracked as separate species. Gene content decides who emits: the model gives *N. winogradskyi* no N₂O production because its genome has no known Nor gene. Higher emission in coculture was traced to a low-oxygen environment created by the coupled metabolism, as the nitrite oxidiser's activity adds to the oxygen demand of ammonia oxidation.

These models genuinely resolve N-oxide reactions and output N₂O. But their N₂O comes from nitrifiers and is driven by electron overflow. It is not N₂O left over when a denitrifier's four-step chain is cut short.

## Denitrifiers with all four genes, and no N₂O readout

Genome-scale models of complete denitrifiers do exist. *Paracoccus denitrificans* Pd1222 (972 genes, 1,371 reactions; Bordel et al. 2024) and *Thauera* sp. MZ1T (iThauera861; Tec-Campos et al. 2025) both carry genes for all four steps. Models of *Pseudomonas stutzeri* and *P. aeruginosa* include denitrification too. None was built or validated to report N₂O. The *Paracoccus* model was checked against biomass yields on carbon sources, including with nitrate as electron acceptor. The *Thauera* model notes that the organism makes NO and N₂O, but it was applied to polymer production and pollutant breakdown. The *Pseudomonas* models treat it as a route to N₂ and energy for anaerobic growth.

Community constraint-based models report exchanges between members and aggregate capability, not resolved sub-steps. The research found none that resolved the four steps with N₂O emerging at community level.

Closing the gap would mean adding a per-step exchange reaction for each N-oxide, explicit electron-donor and quinone coupling for each reductase, and constraints that make truncation possible, such as capping NosZ flux to mimic copper limitation or slow regulation. The review's test is a published, mass- and charge-balanced model of that kind in which N₂O leaves or accumulates because of a pathway gap (absent or capped NosZ, or labour divided between organisms) rather than electron overflow alone. None turned up in the published literature it could locate as of June 2026, though a very recent or unpublished one could exist.

## How kinetic and trait-based models do it

The stepwise picture, with N₂O accumulating, is well established elsewhere. Kinetic models integrate rate equations over time rather than solving an optimisation, so each step can run at its own speed and N₂O builds up whenever its production outpaces its reduction. The Activated Sludge Model for Nitrogen includes all four steps "using individual, reaction-specific parameters" (Hiatt and Grady 2008). Electron-competition models (Pan et al.) make the four steps compete for electrons to predict N₂O accumulation. A model of *Agrobacterium tumefaciens* resolves Nap, NirK and NorB with transcriptional regulation and reproduces NO and N₂O release from unbalanced enzyme expression as cells shift from oxic to anoxic conditions (Kampschreur et al. 2012). Cybernetic models (Song and Liu 2015; Song et al. 2017) add dynamic regulation of the sequential steps. Cybernetic models and dynamic FBA blur the line between families, so the review sorted each model by its core engine: optimisation over stoichiometry, or integration of rate equations.

Trait-based models move the question from the cell to the community. Zakem and colleagues (2024) note that ocean biogeochemical models traditionally collapse denitrification into a single step, while real microbes often carry only some steps, as "modules". Their ocean model runs nitrate to nitrite to N₂O to N₂, leaving NO out, and builds functional types from redox thermodynamics, growth-yield traits and resource competition. N₂O cycling then emerges from niche partitioning: one type reduces nitrate to nitrite, another reduces N₂O to N₂. This is the community pathway-gap mechanism in working form, but built from traits rather than a stoichiometric network.

## Reading the comparison

The three families answer different questions. FBA says what a network can do under its stoichiometry and a goal, and it surfaces N₂O when the electron budget forces it out. Kinetic models capture timing and regulation, and produce N₂O accumulation from steps running out of balance. Trait-based models ask which organisms hold which steps. A constraint-based model in which N₂O comes from a missing or throttled final step has not been found in print; the *Paracoccus* and *Thauera* models, which already carry all four steps, are the obvious place to start one.

Two cautions apply. The review's findings were not checked by a second reader. The detailed electron coupling of the N-oxide reactions in the *Nitrosomonas* models could not be confirmed, because their full methods and reaction tables were only partly accessible.
