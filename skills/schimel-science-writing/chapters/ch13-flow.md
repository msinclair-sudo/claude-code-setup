# Chapter 13: Flow

## Core Idea
Flow is what turns a paper into a "page turner" instead of a "re-turner": you achieve it by linking each sentence's topic to the previous sentence's stress (and each paragraph's opening to the previous paragraph's resolution), weaving old and new information into an unbroken chain so readers never derail at transitions.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Janus Function**: The topic (opening) of each sentence or story arc must face both directions at once, like the two-faced Roman god — looking *backward* to information familiar from earlier text, and *forward* to signal what this sentence is about.
  - When to use: At every sentence opening and every arc/paragraph transition.
  - How: Build the topic from something already in the reader's mind, then let the stress introduce the new idea that the *next* topic will grab.
  - Why it works: It sockets new information into old, building a framework (schema) so the reader never has to figure out why a fact matters or how it connects.

- **Stress-to-topic linkage = story; topic-to-topic linkage = list**: Tie each sentence's topic to the *stress* of the sentence before it to create a flowing story; repeat the *same topic* across sentences and you get a list of facts about one thing.
  - When to use: Stress-to-topic for narrative advancement; topic-to-topic (with an LD lead) when you deliberately want a short coherent list under one theme.
  - How: Identify the topic and stress of each sentence; check that sentence N's topic reaches back to sentence N-1's stress.
  - Failure mode: **Stress-to-stress linkage makes "confused readers"** — neither a list nor a story.

- **Reach back and grab**: To bridge two disconnected arcs, pull an idea (not necessarily an exact word) out of the prior arc and make it the topic of the next sentence.
  - When to use: When sentences are individually well-written but arcs don't connect.
  - How: Repeat a single word, or more often transform a theme ("bonded atoms" → "bond strength"); the two faces of Janus need not be identical.

- **Linking paragraphs (resolution-to-opening)**: Apply the same stress-topic strategy across paragraph breaks — open paragraph 2 with the idea that resolved paragraph 1.

## Key Concepts
- **Flow**: Seamless linkage of ideas that carries readers from sentence to sentence and arc to arc.
- **Topic**: The grammatical/conceptual subject opening a sentence; performs the Janus function.
- **Stress**: The new, emphasized information at the end of a sentence.
- **Story arc**: A unit of narrative; a paragraph may hold several, and transitions between them are the danger zones.
- **Thematic coherence**: Every sentence's topic fits the theme set by the opening/lead, so the paragraph "hangs together."
- **LD (lead) structure**: A lead that defines a theme so following sentences can link back to it — needed to make a list of points work.
- **Schema / socketing new into old**: Building a framework where each new fact slots into established context.
- **Top-down revision**: Diagnosing flow strategically (restructuring arcs) rather than tactically (forcing local word links).

## Mental Models
- Use **Janus** when checking any sentence opening: does the topic look backward *and* forward?
- Use **"stress-to-topic = story, topic-to-topic = list, stress-to-stress = confusion"** when diagnosing why a passage feels choppy.
- Use **top-down strategic diagnosis** when a quick local fix creates "local flow but global disruption" — the real problem may be that the prior arc points the wrong direction; fix *that* arc instead.
- Use **break the rule for the principle** when flow demands a long subject: violate the short-subject rule (Ch 12) to make ideas connect.

## Anti-patterns
- **Forcing a local connection tactically (e.g., passive voice to grab the prior stress)**: It just pushes the connection problem down the paragraph and can steer the story the wrong way.
- **Squashing an unrelated character in to "introduce" it**: The industrial-landscapes sentence broke flow; the fix was to delete it and give that idea its own arc elsewhere.
- **Stress-to-stress linkage**: Two paragraphs sharing only their stress produces confused readers, not a story.

## Worked Example
**Example 13.1 vs 13.2 (molecules).** In 13.1 every sentence opens with "Molecules" — topic-to-topic, producing a flat *list* of facts. In 13.2 the topic of each sentence ties to the previous stress: "...covalently bonded atoms. Bond strength controls a molecule's reactions. Sometimes those reactions are slower than bond strength would predict." Stress "bonded atoms" → topic "Bond strength"; stress "reactions" → topic "those reactions." This stress-to-topic chain fulfills the Janus function and turns the list into a flowing *story* — same content, but coherent.

**Example 13.3 (salvage logging).** Sentence 3 abruptly introduced "cavities in dead trees / cavity-nesting birds," a new arc unconnected to the logging arc. Fix by reaching back and grabbing "harvesting": "The dead trees that are harvested, however, can provide cavities that are nesting sites for birds." This lengthened the subject (normally bad) but served the higher principle of making the reader's job easy, and revealed a clean OCAR paragraph that opens and resolves on "salvage logging" — a circle pulled into a spiral.

## Key Takeaways
1. Diagram topic and stress for any choppy passage; link each topic to the prior stress to convert a list into a story.
2. At every transition, make the opening topic do the Janus job — context plus launching point.
3. To bridge arcs, grab an *idea* from the previous arc, transforming the wording rather than parroting an exact word.
4. Link paragraphs by tying the next opening to the previous resolution, using the same stress-topic strategy.
5. When a local fix creates global disruption, go top-down: the first arc may point the wrong way — restructure it, or delete a mixed-in sentence.
6. Break the short-subject rule when connecting ideas requires it; the principle (connection) outranks the rule.
