# Blind comparison: NFERT piece A vs piece B

The two pieces have the same length (body about 1,230 words each; B adds a 10-entry reference list) and almost the same skeleton: Haber-Bosch and feedstock; energy per tonne of ammonia and per tonne of N; CO₂ per tonne; three emission shares at three system boundaries; legumes as a real substitute with limits; the resident community (free-living fixers, inoculants, mineralisation) as unproven; then a closing turn to losses and nitrogen-use efficiency. Most numbers are identical. They differ in a handful of added points, in how they attribute sources, and in how they speak to the reader.

## 1. Coverage within the two questions

**Manufacturing energy and emissions, and emission share**

Both cover: 28 / 37 / 59 / 55 GJ per t NH₃; the 1.21 conversion and 34–45 GJ per t N; 1.6 / 1.92 / 2.8 / 0.79 t CO₂ per t NH₃; 1.9–3.4 t CO₂e per t N; cradle-to-gate excludes field N₂O; IFA 0.47 Gt (0.93%) and process studies at 1–1.8%; Menegat 1.13 Gt, 2.1% global, 10.6% of agriculture, 38.8 / 2.6 / 58.6 split; Gao about 5% with two-thirds after application; manure at 23 t CO₂e per t N (1.9 times synthetic); NUE as the main lever.

Only in A:
- A claim about where confidence lies: the manufacturing figures are "the firmest numbers in this piece", backed by several agreeing sources, while the microbial findings mostly rest on one source each. It's a useful steer, though see section 3 on how it is framed.

Only in B:
- The ecoinvent figure comes with its primary energy (41 GJ per t NH₃), a third energy number between the best-plant and global-average figures.
- It says outright that the per-nitrogen numbers are arithmetic on the per-ammonia figures, not separate measurements.
- Gao's split is given in both directions (two-thirds after application, one-third in production).
- A derived life-cycle figure for average synthetic fertiliser, "something like 12 t CO₂e per tonne of N", set against the factory figure. This is the single most useful addition for the reader: it turns the boundary argument into one number per tonne. But it creates a tension (section 3).
- It names the IFA figure as secondhand ("reported in Menegat et al. 2022") and says which process study each number comes from.

**Does microbial nitrogen cycling reduce synthetic input?**

Both cover: Anglade's legume rotation (87% of inputs from fixation, conventional productivity, 21 vs 40–100 kg N/ha/yr surplus); fixation shares ranging from a few percent to nearly all; fertiliser suppressing fixation (81–86% at 125–250 kg N/ha); free-living fixers being modest; no N-fixing cereals; Schütz's inoculant meta-analysis, with the point that it measures extra yield, not fertiliser withheld at constant yield; mineralisation at 1–4% a season, with soil-supply testing as the lever rather than microbial efficiency; the attribution problem; and the losses and NUE question as the bigger, untested one.

Only in A:
- It names associative fixers alongside free-living ones.
- It frames the uptake/loss question explicitly as the more consequential microbial question (leaching, denitrification, N₂O), and says the weak substitution result is "no answer to it". B makes the same move, but A names the loss pathways.

Only in B:
- Legumes are "the largest biological nitrogen input to cropping systems" (Ladha).
- The 81–86% suppression is scoped to "one system reviewed by Ladha et al.", where A states it as a general result.
- A one-line verdict: the resident community "supplements fertiliser and has not been shown to replace it". That's a clearer bottom line than A's "remain unproven", because it credits the real (mineralisation) supply while denying substitution.

On coverage the pieces are close to equal. B has slightly more, and it attributes better.

## 2. Clarity for a scientifically literate outsider

**A**
- It refers to a process the reader never sees. "No study in the review separated...", the heading "The question the review did not ask", "It was not researched", and the closing note that "these findings come from one literature review whose reading of its sources was not checked by a second reader." A standalone piece has no "review" the reader knows about. These lines read like notes from the people who produced it, and the last one undercuts the piece without telling the reader what to discount.
- The opening paragraph is dense. Process, product, 82% N, steam reforming and the feedstock conclusion all arrive in three sentences, and the 82% figure is stated well before it gets used (the 1.21 conversion, two paragraphs later).
- Citations come in one cluster at the end of the CO₂ paragraph, so the reader can't tell which number came from which study, and there is no reference list to follow them up. It also calls these sources "industrial-ecology and biogeochemistry", which doesn't fit what are mostly process-engineering studies.
- The heading "Three shares, three questions" is clever, but it gives the reader nothing to scan by. The three figures only emerge one paragraph at a time.

**B**
- Its intro is easier to follow: "The nitrogen is free. The hydrogen is not." That puts the feedstock argument in two short sentences.
- The bold lead-ins ("Manufacture only: about 0.9% to 1.8%", "The synthetic-nitrogen supply chain: about 2.1%", "...manure included: about 5%") let the reader see the three boundaries at once, which is the section's whole point.
- Splitting energy and CO₂ under separate headings, with a citation on each number, makes the manufacturing figures easier to check.
- The weak spot is the "something like 12 t" sentence. Its derivation (23 ÷ 1.9) is left implicit behind "therefore", and a reader may stop to work out where it came from.
- "None of the studies here" is a milder form of A's problem, but it reads naturally as "the studies cited here".

Overall B is easier to read, mainly because A keeps pointing at a review the reader can't see.

## 3. Missing, wrong, inconsistent or overclaimed

Both:
- **Ammonia is treated as fertiliser.** Both convert per-tonne-ammonia figures to "a tonne of fertiliser nitrogen" as if farmers spread ammonia. Neither mentions urea, ammonium nitrate or nitric-acid N₂O, which add energy and emissions downstream of the ammonia plant. That probably explains a hidden inconsistency in both pieces. The cradle-to-gate range (1.9–3.4 t CO₂e per t N) looks low next to the share figures they also quote. For example, manufacture at 38.8% of Menegat's 1.13 Gt works out to several tonnes per tonne of N at any plausible global N use. An outsider wouldn't spot this, but it is a real gap in the manufacturing answer.
- **CO₂ and CO₂e are mixed.** The 1.9 low end comes from 1.6 t CO₂ (not CO₂e) times 1.21, yet the range is labelled CO₂e. It's minor, but it's in both.
- **Carbon capture is left unconverted.** The 0.79 t figure is never put per tonne of N, and neither piece says what share of plants have it. Low-carbon (electrolytic) hydrogen isn't mentioned at all, though one cited source (Tunå et al.) is about non-fossil ammonia. That matters for how fixed the manufacturing footprint is.
- **Thin evidence for the legume claim.** The "real" substitution rests on one European organic rotation. Both say "one", which is fair, but "real and direct" (A) and "real and measured" (B) lean on it heavily.
- **Microbes and losses are absent.** Nitrification inhibitors, the most obvious microbial-cycling intervention that does change fertiliser need, appear in neither. Both acknowledge the losses question as open, so this is a scope choice rather than an error.

A only:
- **The Ladha suppression result is overgeneralised.** "Applying 125 to 250 kg ... cut fixation rates by 81% to 86%", followed by "a heavily fertilised legume does little fixing", states one system's result as a rule. B scopes it correctly.
- **The confidence note doesn't hold together.** The closing caveat says the reading of sources "was not checked by a second reader". That casts doubt on the whole piece, yet the same piece calls the manufacturing figures "the firmest numbers in this piece". The reader is told to distrust it and given no way to act on that.
- **Citations can't be followed.** Names and years appear with no reference list.

B only:
- **The 12 t figure sits awkwardly with the split.** If an average synthetic fertiliser carries about 12 t CO₂e per t N over its life, and one-third of life-cycle emissions are in production (as B also says), production would be about 4 t per t N. That's above B's own 1.9–3.4 factory range. The likely reasons are that Gao's production stage includes more than the ammonia plant, and that the one-third share is computed with manure included. B doesn't say either, so the numbers don't add up on the page. The figure is also derived by B rather than reported, and "something like" only partly signals that.
- **"Supplements fertiliser" is a light overreach.** Mineralisation supplies N, so it's defensible. But the piece has just said the inoculant evidence is inconsistent and that the effect of an "efficient" community isn't isolated, so "supplements" is doing slightly more work than the evidence it cites.
- **"Without a host plant" is slightly too narrow** for the free-living/associative category it stands for. That's minor.

## 4. Which I would give the reader

I'd give the reader B. The substance is nearly identical, so the difference is modest, but B is easier to follow. It sets out the three emission boundaries in a form you can scan, attaches each number to its source, and comes with a reference list. It also avoids A's habit of talking about "the review" and a missing "second reader", which a standalone reader can't place and which weakens A's ending. A's real advantages are small: it names the loss pathways more concretely, and it says honestly where the evidence is thin. A careless overgeneralisation of the Ladha result offsets them. B's derived 12 t figure is its one real liability. I'd keep it but fix it: show the arithmetic and explain why it doesn't reconcile with the factory range. Neither piece should go out without noting that the ammonia-plant figures understate the footprint of the fertiliser products farmers actually buy.
