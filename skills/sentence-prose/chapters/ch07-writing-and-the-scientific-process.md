# Chapter 7: Writing and the Scientific Process

## Core Idea
The seven reader-expectation principles are **principles, not rules** — there is no algorithm for good writing. But applied together they don't just clarify prose; they re-engage the thinking behind the science, because "the structure of the prose becomes the structure of the scientific argument."

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The seven structural principles** (the article's closing synthesis):
  1. Follow a grammatical subject as soon as possible with its verb.
  2. Place in the **stress position** the new information you want emphasized.
  3. Place the person/thing whose **story** the sentence tells at the beginning, in the **topic position**.
  4. Place appropriate **old information** in the topic position for linkage backward and context forward.
  5. Articulate the **action** of every clause or sentence in its verb.
  6. In general, provide **context before** asking the reader to consider anything new.
  7. In general, make the relative emphases of the **substance** coincide with the relative emphases raised by the **structure**.
  - When to use: As a final revision checklist on any dense passage.
  - How: Pass through the passage once per principle, or use the cheatsheet's combined diagnostic.

## Key Concepts
- **Principles, not rules**: Slavish adherence fails as surely as "always avoid split infinitives." Too many expectations operate at once for a fixed algorithm, and any expectation can be violated to good effect.
- **Skillful violation**: The best stylists violate expectations deliberately — but only because they satisfy them most of the time, so the violation registers as a marked, meaningful moment.
- **Style as accumulated structural choices**: A writer's style is the sum of the structural decisions they habitually make — which is why habits, once identified, can be permanently changed.
- **Substance ↔ expression intertwined**: Changing one changes the other; revising structure repeatedly exposed conceptual gaps that had to be filled with new content.

## Mental Models
- Think of the seven principles as a lens stack, not a gate: each reveals problems, none alone defines "correct."
- "Improving either the prose or the science improves the other" — treat a stubborn sentence as possibly a sign of unfinished thinking.

## Anti-patterns
- **Treating the principles as rules**: Mechanical application produces stilted prose and misses the cases where violation is the right move.
- **Believing writers can "merely" record data**: Every word sits in a structural location that shapes interpretation; neutral recording is impossible.

## Worked Example
Across the article's four examples, only the first (URF's) could be fixed by structural rearrangement alone to yield a near-finished passage. The other three — earthquakes, hydrogen bonds, 5S RNA — each *required adding material*: the structural pass reached a point where the writer "could proceed no further without either supplying connections between ideas or eliminating some existing material altogether." In every case, analyzing the prose forced a return to the substance of the science. That is the chapter's proof that structure and thought are inseparable.

## Key Takeaways
1. Use the seven principles as a revision checklist, applied as principles — never as rigid rules.
2. Provide context before new material; align structural emphasis with substantive emphasis.
3. Violate expectations only deliberately, against a background of mostly satisfying them.
4. Your style is your habitual structural choices — identifiable, and therefore improvable.
5. Structural revision is a thinking tool: it surfaces conceptual gaps and reconnects you to the science.
