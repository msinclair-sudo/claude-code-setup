# Chapter 5: The Opening

## Core Idea
The opening is the paper's "power position": the first sentence (and usually the first paragraph) must identify the driving problem, introduce the characters, and target an audience — setting direction, momentum, and tone before the reader can drift. Frame the opening to promise exactly the story you will deliver, no wider and no narrower.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **The Three Goals of the Opening**: every opening must (1) identify the problem that drives the research, (2) introduce the characters, and (3) target an audience. Bonus: foreshadow the challenge and even the conclusions.
  - When to use: writing/diagnosing any first paragraph of a paper or proposal.
  - How: ask of your draft opening — Is it clear what the paper is about? Does it frame the problem? Does it introduce the critical characters? Whom do you want to read this and how should they think about it?
  - Why it works / failure mode: initial impressions are strong and lasting, so the leverage is high. Fail by giving misdirection (start one way, switch) or no direction (textbook schema, no signal).

- **The Two-Step Opening**: open with a broad issue that engages your target audience, then modulate/narrow to the specific focus you actually work with.
  - When to use: targeting a broader audience or a proposal review panel whose members don't share your sub-discipline's schemas.
  - How: step 1 — engage with a topic the audience already cares about; step 2 — transition down in scale to your specific question. Named "two-step" because, like the dance, it must be quick: more than two steps and you stumble.
  - Why it works / failure mode: builds the bridge from reviewers' interests to your work (Weintraub opened on the Arctic carbon store, narrowed to C–N interactions, won a best-paper award). Failure: too many steps, or never narrowing (no direction).

- **Matching Opening to Resolution (the hourglass)**: set the top of the hourglass as wide as you can credibly deliver — the bottom (resolution) must be the same width as the top.
  - When to use: deciding how broadly to cast the opening, since the goal is citation, not just publication.
  - How: if opening is wider than resolution you overpromise and readers feel cheated (bait-and-switch); if narrower you underpromise and lose interested readers. If you must err, err slightly wide — you can filter down fast; framing too narrow loses readers immediately and you can't get them back.

- **Positioning Sentence**: a broad "positioning" or "launch" first sentence that frames the focus, from which you launch to the specific point in the next sentence.
  - When to use: when the specific point can't fit in one sentence (Example 5.3: topography of mountains → river incision).

- **Targeting by Audience Schema**: choose language, characters, and breadth to match what your intended readers already know and value.
  - When to use: specialist vs. generalist journals; NSF vs. mission agency proposals.
  - How: specialists get a technical, schema-rich opening (the "secret password," e.g., "Larry Pomeroy's seminal paper"); generalists get an accessible story with widely known characters (van Leeuwenhoek, the Viking Lander).

## Key Concepts
- **Power position**: the opening, where your first words carry maximum leverage over impression and direction.
- **Mental whiplash**: reader disorientation when you start one direction then abruptly switch.
- **Schema**: a reader's pre-existing mental framework of a field; openings must match audiences who hold different schemas than you.
- **Misdirection**: an opening that develops one story, then redirects to a different mechanism (Example 5.5).
- **No direction**: an opening of textbook/widely-held-schema material that gives no signal where the story goes (Example 5.6).
- **Positioning statement**: a broad opening sentence that sets up the specific point — meaningful in a good paper, a platitude in a poor one.
- **The compact**: the opening establishes a promise with readers about what the story is about; you must deliver on it.
- **SUCCES elements**: simple, unexpected, concrete, credible, emotional, story — engaged to make openings stick (Azam & Worden's Mars opening).

## Mental Models
- Use the **hourglass** when sizing the opening: make the top as wide as the bottom can deliver.
- Use the **two-step dance** when your audience's schema differs from yours: engage broad, then narrow — fast.
- Use the **"secret password" test** when choosing audience: does your first sentence court a wide readership (van Leeuwenhoek) or constrain to insiders (Pomeroy)?
- Use the **delete-to-the-real-start** rule when revising: if you explained schema "for yourself" in the draft, find where the real story begins and cut everything before it.

## Anti-patterns
- **Misdirection**: developing one mechanism in paragraph 1, then introducing a different one in paragraph 2 — cuts readers adrift.
- **No direction / explaining a widely held schema**: laying out textbook material insiders already know; common in inexperienced writers still ingraining their own schema.
- **Overpromising (top wider than bottom)**: a wide opening with a narrow resolution — readers feel cheated.
- **Underpromising (top narrower than bottom)**: a narrow opening hiding a broad story — interested readers never discover it and tune out.
- **Platitude openings**: imitating opening lines that say little instead of building a positioning statement.

## Worked Example
Schimel dissects his own methane paper (Example 5.5) as misdirection. The opening paragraph casts plants as controlling CH4 by *feeding carbon to methanogens* — so a reader assumes that's the whole paper. Then paragraph 2 opens: "Vascular plants, however, also transport CH4 out of soil...," a new mechanism. Readers are left wondering: false lead, contradiction, or additional mechanism? The near-trivial fix is a broader positioning first sentence: "Plants control CH4 dynamics in wetland ecosystems by two mechanisms. The first is to supply C to the soil methanogenic community..." Now the carbon-supply paragraph reads as *completing* a stated direction rather than switching it, and the transport paragraph feels like the promised second half.

## Key Takeaways
1. Make the first sentence do real work — identify the problem, introduce characters, target an audience; treat it as a power position.
2. Diagnose drafts for the two failure modes: misdirection (you switched) and no direction (textbook schema). Cut everything before the real story starts.
3. If one sentence can't carry the point, write a broad positioning sentence, then launch to specifics.
4. For broad audiences and proposal panels, use the two-step opening — engage wide, narrow fast (no more than two steps).
5. Size the opening to the resolution (hourglass); if forced, err slightly wide because you can filter down, but never frame too narrow.
6. Pick the opening's characters and language by your readers' schema — Pomeroy's paper for insiders, van Leeuwenhoek for everyone.
