# Chapter 9: The Resolution

## Core Idea
The resolution is a power position — readers remember the last thing you say, so it must carry your strongest, most concrete take-home message. A good resolution steps backward through OCAR to widen the hourglass and close the circle back to the opening; anything weak, distracting, or self-undermining placed there destroys the whole paper.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Stepping backward through OCAR**: A good resolution achieves the story's spiral by reversing OCAR — it reiterates the **action**, answers the questions raised in the **challenge**, and demonstrates how those answers contribute to the larger problem of the **opening**.
  - When to use: writing the closing paragraph of any paper.
  - How: (1) flag the resolution ("In conclusion," or a framing sentence); (2) state the key result that answers the challenge question; (3) interpret/synthesize it into a simple model or message — start "widening the hourglass"; (4) finish opening the hourglass by tying back to the big issue of the opening, ideally echoing the opening sentence's key phrase to "close the circle."
  - Why it works: last words are accepted as the take-home message; closing the circle completes the story and makes the paper findable. Failure mode: putting any extraneous thought in the closing position makes it read as your most important point — "don't blow the punchline."

- **The three resolution objectives (synopsize–synthesize–contribute)**: First pare away dead tissue (fluff, distractions, new ideas — move important ones elsewhere). Then condense the resolution to do three things: (1) **synopsize** the key results, (2) **synthesize** them — show how they answer your question, turning information into knowledge, (3) **show what this contributes** to the larger problem.
  - When to use: diagnosing and fixing any weak/bad resolution.
  - How: check each version against SUCCES, especially **C (concrete)** — most failures lack concreteness. Make a strong statement without overselling (use constrained "may offer").

- **Concluding with a Question**: When your most important discovery is a new, unanticipated question, resolve by posing it — but make it concrete and show how it grew from your work (you identified a new gap, you did not fail to fill one).
  - When to use: when the work's chief contribution is opening a well-defined new direction.
  - How: state findings/conclusions in strong language first, then frame concrete questions that reinforce and extend the conclusions and point the way forward.

- **Resolutions in Proposals**: Don't end a proposal in pure **LD** structure after the last experiment. Add a resolution paragraph that reiterates the big issue and explains how the components work together to solve it — the final pitch for funding.
  - When to use: every proposal; reviewers' opinions often solidify only at the end.
  - How: restate problem, challenge, research plan, and how it solves the problem; "give them the words" to write "excellent" / "must fund."

## Key Concepts
- **Power position**: the closing slot; readers remember and weight the last thing you say.
- **Take-home message**: the strongest, most memorable conclusion you want readers to carry.
- **The story's spiral**: closing back to the original topic while showing how the starting point has moved.
- **Widening / opening the hourglass**: moving from specific results outward to broader significance.
- **Closing the circle**: returning the final sentence to the issue (and phrasing) of the opening.
- **Flag / road sign**: words like "In conclusion" or "In summary" signaling you're at the resolution.
- **"More research is needed"**: the most common, most damaging closing — it weaponizes the power position against your own conclusions.
- **Concluding with a question vs. "more research is needed"**: a concrete question that grew from the work resolves; a vague question about the work undermines it.

## Mental Models
- Use **step backward through OCAR** when you need a reliable skeleton for the closing paragraph (action → challenge answer → opening's big problem).
- Use **echo the opening sentence's key phrase at the very end** when you want the reader to feel the circle close.
- Use **constrained strong language ("may offer a route")** when the conclusion is real but uncertain — strength without overselling.
- Use **concluding with a concrete question** when the biggest contribution is a new, well-framed direction, not a tidy answer.

## Anti-patterns
- **Weak**: synopsizes results then asserts importance without saying how — "may provide new insights" but never names them; overreaches and underdelivers, simultaneously unconvincing and obvious.
- **Distracting**: ends with truisms/textbook material that belong in the Introduction, or introduces a brand-new idea (the "whodunit" plot twist) never set up in the opening — fails to close the circle and won't surface in literature searches.
- **Undermining your conclusions**: "more research is needed," "importance has yet to be assessed," "remains to be established in future trials" — focuses on what you didn't accomplish, using the power position to weaken your science.

## Worked Example
Schimel dissects a weak hummingbird resolution (Ex. 9.5): "A proteomic evaluation… revealed… protein degradation… These results suggest that changes in the hummingbird proteome may provide new insights into the complex physiology of avian systems biology." Good synopsis, but it skips synthesis — it claims relevance to wider bird physiology without saying what the insight is. His fix keeps the result sentence, then synthesizes concretely: "While hummingbirds migrate long distances over water without feeding or resting, it is physiologically stressful, and the birds' ability to manage this stress may limit the distance they can migrate." To widen the hourglass to all migratory birds, he edits the last sentence — adding "such as hummingbirds" and condensing "the birds'" to "birds'" — shifting from specific birds to birds in general (only valid if the Introduction established stress in other birds).

## Key Takeaways
1. Treat the last sentence as the punchline — put only your strongest, most concrete take-home message there.
2. Build the resolution by stepping backward through OCAR; close the circle by echoing the opening's key phrase.
3. Always do three things: synopsize the key results, synthesize them into an answer, show the contribution to the big problem.
4. Never end with "more research is needed" — it uses the power position to undermine your own work.
5. If a strong new idea emerged while writing, weave it into the opening/Introduction — don't spring it as a surprise resolution.
6. Make strong claims but constrain them ("may offer") rather than overselling.
7. Give proposals a real resolution paragraph — restate the big issue and how the pieces solve it; end strong because reviewers decide at the end.

- **Ch (the opening / the funnel-hourglass)**: the resolution must close back to the opening's big problem, reopening the hourglass.
