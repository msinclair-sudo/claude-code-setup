### NFERT

**D1. Manufacturing energy and emissions, and emission share**

*Answer to D1, as the review gave it; no critic checked it.* **Question 1 — Manufacturing energy and emissions, and emission share: WELL-ESTABLISHED WITH QUANTIFIED FIGURES**

Independent industrial-ecology and biogeochemistry sources converge. Manufacture costs about 28 to 37 GJ and emits about 1.6 to 2.8 t CO₂ per tonne of ammonia, equivalently about 1.9 to 3.4 t CO₂-equivalent per tonne of N (Tock et al. 2015; Demirhan et al. 2019; Razon 2014; Tunå et al. 2014). **D2. Does microbial nitrogen cycling reduce synthetic input?**

*Answer to D2, as the review gave it; no critic checked it.* **Question 2 — Microbial cycling reduces synthetic input: SPLIT**

For deliberate legume-based biological fixation, the reduction is ESTABLISHED BUT QUALIFIED, with strong direct evidence bounded by wide variability in fixation fraction and by fertiliser suppression of fixation (Anglade et al. 2015; Ladha et al. 2022). For the resident microbial community cycling nutrients more efficiently, as the question is literally posed, the link is WEAKLY / ONLY INDIRECTLY SUPPORTED. The inoculant evidence measures yield gain rather than input reduction at constant yield (INDIRECT; Schütz et al. 2018), free-living fixation is modest (INDIRECT; Ladha et al. 2022), and mineralisation rarely meets high-yield demand (St. Luce et al. 2011). No reviewed source isolates microbial-community efficiency from confounding soil and management variables as the cause of a reduced synthetic requirement at held yield.

## Concepts

In an order where each concept comes after the ones it builds on; the order means nothing more.

### ammonia synthesis by the Haber-Bosch process

- Ammonia synthesis runs through the Haber-Bosch process, which fixes atmospheric N₂ with hydrogen at high temperature and pressure; most of the energy and almost all of the carbon enter upstream, when hydrogen is stripped from natural gas or coal by steam reforming.

### energy per unit of ammonia and nitrogen

Builds on: ammonia synthesis by the Haber-Bosch process.

- Modern natural-gas plants need roughly 28 GJ per tonne of ammonia.
- The global average sits higher, near 37 GJ per tonne of ammonia, because older and coal-fed plants remain in service; coal-based production in China runs to about 59 GJ per tonne.
- Energy requirements fell from about 55 GJ per tonne of ammonia in the 1950s to the high-20s today.
- Ammonia carries 82% nitrogen by mass, so one tonne of N requires about 1.21 tonnes of ammonia; manufacturing one tonne of fertiliser N therefore costs roughly 34 GJ on the best natural-gas route and roughly 45 GJ at the global average.

### carbon dioxide per unit of ammonia and nitrogen

Builds on: ammonia synthesis by the Haber-Bosch process, energy per unit of ammonia and nitrogen.

- Production CO₂ tracks the feedstock: modern natural-gas plants emit close to 1.6 t CO₂ per tonne of ammonia.
- The ecoinvent inventory gives 1.92 t CO₂-equivalent per tonne of liquid ammonia alongside 41 GJ of primary energy.
- A process-optimisation study citing a global mix that includes coal reports about 2.8 t CO₂ per tonne of ammonia.
- Carbon capture on a natural-gas plant can bring the gate figure down to about 0.79 t CO₂ per tonne of ammonia.
- Per unit nitrogen, the manufacturing footprint is about 1.9 to 3.4 t CO₂-equivalent per tonne of N, the lower bound for efficient gas plants and the upper bound for coal-heavy mixes.
- Manufacture costs about 28 to 37 GJ and emits about 1.6 to 2.8 t CO₂ per tonne of ammonia, equivalently about 1.9 to 3.4 t CO₂-equivalent per tonne of N. *(repl)*
- These are cradle-to-gate manufacturing figures: they exclude the field N₂O released after application, which is the larger life-cycle term and belongs to use, not manufacture.

### share of agricultural and global emissions

Builds on: carbon dioxide per unit of ammonia and nitrogen.

- The share depends entirely on where the system boundary is drawn, and the three commonly cited figures answer three different questions.
- Manufacturing alone is the smallest slice: the International Fertilizer Association attributed 0.47 Gt CO₂-equivalent, about 0.93% of total anthropogenic emissions, to N fertiliser production in 2007.
- Independent process studies place ammonia manufacture at roughly 1% to 1.8% of global greenhouse-gas emissions.
- The full synthetic-N supply chain is larger because it adds field and transport emissions: Menegat et al. (2022) estimate 1.13 Gt CO₂-equivalent for 2018, equal to 10.6% of agricultural emissions and 2.1% of global emissions.
- Within the supply-chain total, manufacture accounts for 38.8%, field emissions for 58.6% and transport for 2.6%: the use phase, not the factory, dominates.
- The full nitrogen-fertiliser life cycle including manure is larger still: Gao and Cabrera Serrenho (2023) put production and use at about 5% of global greenhouse-gas emissions, roughly two-thirds occurring after deployment and one-third during production.
- Gao and Cabrera Serrenho report that manure emits about 23 t CO₂-equivalent per tonne of N, some 1.9 times an average synthetic fertiliser, because much of its nitrogen is lost.
- Manufacturing is about 0.9% to 1.8% of global emissions; the synthetic-N supply chain about 2.1% of global and 10.6% of agricultural emissions; the full life cycle including manure about 5% of global emissions. *(repl)*

### nitrogen-use efficiency

Builds on: share of agricultural and global emissions.

- Gao and Cabrera Serrenho identify nitrogen-use efficiency as the single most effective mitigation lever for nitrogen-fertiliser emissions.

### symbiotic biological nitrogen fixation (legumes and rhizobia)

- Legumes in symbiosis with rhizobia are the largest biological nitrogen input to cropping systems, and their effect on fertiliser demand is documented.
- In an organic legume-based rotation in Europe, 87% of total nitrogen inputs came from fixation, and the rotation matched conventional productivity at a surplus of only 21 kg N per hectare per year against 40 to 100 for fertilised systems.
- Where humans deliberately add a nitrogen-fixing symbiosis, the reduction in synthetic input is real and direct: fixed nitrogen substitutes for applied nitrogen in the legume and partially enriches the following crop.
- The fraction of legume nitrogen derived from fixation varies enormously, from a few percent to nearly all, depending on species, soil mineral nitrogen and competition.
- Fixation is suppressed by the very input it might replace: applying 125 to 250 kg fertiliser N per hectare cut fixation rates by 81% to 86%.
- Symbiotic fixation and fertiliser are therefore partly substitutive, not additive, which caps the achievable saving.
- For deliberate legume-based biological fixation, the reduction in synthetic input has strong direct evidence, bounded by wide variability in fixation fraction and by fertiliser suppression of fixation.

### free-living and associative diazotrophs

- Free-living and associative diazotrophs are ubiquitous and do fix nitrogen, but their measured contribution in cereal systems is modest, and engineering cereals to fix their own nitrogen remains unrealised.
- Their support for a reduced fertiliser requirement is indirect: the fixed quantities are rarely large enough to displace a defined fertiliser rate at constant yield.

### microbial inoculants (biofertilisers)

- Microbial inoculants raise yield inconsistently: the largest meta-analysis, spanning 171 field studies, found yield responses of +8.5% in continental climates, +10.0% in oceanic, +14.9% in tropical and +20.0% in dry climates, with effects described as often inconsistent and dependent on soil phosphorus and climate.
- The inoculant evidence is indirect for the substitution question, because it measures yield gain at a given input rather than input reduction at a held yield.

### nitrogen mineralisation by the resident community

- Mineralisation by the resident community supplies nitrogen, but rarely enough: only about 1% to 4% of soil organic nitrogen is mineralised in a season, and mineralisation alone is seldom sufficient to reach the maximum yield of modern cultivars, so additional fertiliser or organic nitrogen is needed.
- Quantifying soil nitrogen supply can improve fertiliser-use efficiency and trim rates, but the lever is the measured soil supply, not a demonstrated property of microbial-community efficiency.

### whether microbial nitrogen cycling reduces synthetic input at constant yield

Builds on: symbiotic biological nitrogen fixation (legumes and rhizobia), free-living and associative diazotrophs, microbial inoculants (biofertilisers), nitrogen mineralisation by the resident community.

- Whether microbial nitrogen cycling reduces synthetic input splits along a clear line: the reduction is real and direct where humans deliberately add a nitrogen-fixing symbiosis, and the support is mostly indirect where the claim rests on the resident free-living community cycling nitrogen more efficiently.
- For the resident microbial community cycling nutrients more efficiently, as the question is literally posed, the link to reduced synthetic input is weakly or only indirectly supported: three lines of evidence (free-living fixation, inoculants, mineralisation) bound it, and none isolates the microbial term cleanly.
- No reviewed source isolates microbial-community efficiency from confounding soil and management variables as the cause of a reduced synthetic requirement at held yield.
- Attribution is the first failure mode: reduced fertiliser need co-varies with organic matter, water and rotation (soil organic matter, moisture and management), so the microbial contribution is hard to separate.
- Suppression is the second failure mode: adding fertiliser depresses fixation, bounding the saving.
- Magnitude is the third failure mode: free-living fixation and mineralisation seldom meet high-yield demand, so the resident community supplements rather than replaces synthetic nitrogen.
- The strongest evidence for reduced synthetic input is agronomic and concerns legume inclusion, a deliberate management choice, not the spontaneous efficiency of a resident community.
