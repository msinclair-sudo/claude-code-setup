# Chapter 4: Story Structure

## Core Idea
All stories — including science papers — are built from four elements (Opening, Challenge, Action, Resolution = **OCAR**), and you choose among four standard structures for assembling them based on how patient your audience is. OCAR is a principle that must be honored at every scale; IMRaD is merely one rule-bound physical layout that maps onto it.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **OCAR**: The four universal story elements taken in sequence — **Opening** (who/where/what larger problem), **Challenge** (what question must be answered), **Action** (the work done or proposed), **Resolution** (how understanding has changed).
  - When to use: Specialist-journal papers, where readers are patient and want to watch ideas develop before conclusions.
  - How: Place O as the first short section, C at the end of the introduction, A through Methods/Results, R as the last section of the Discussion.
  - Why it works / failure mode: A story missing any element is "slippery, rather than sticky." A paper with no resolution "falls into the reject bin." In specialist journals a lead-based paper looks suspect — like you forced data to prove a predetermined story rather than test it.
- **ABDCE**: **Action, Background, Development, Climax, Ending** — front-loads the story by collapsing Challenge into the Opening to make an exciting "action" start. A+B together comprise O+C; Ending = Resolution.
  - When to use: Step-less-patient audiences — modern fiction, mystery/adventure, and scientific proposal reviewers.
  - How: Open with dramatic action to hook, then back up to fill in background, develop to climax, resolve.
  - Why it works / failure mode: Hooks impatient readers fast; the trade-off is inefficiency — after the opening action you must double back for background. Worth it if it commits the reader.
- **LDR (Lead/Development/Resolution)**: Magazine-journalist structure — an engaging lead, then development, then a satisfying resolution.
  - When to use: Generalist journals (Nature, Science) where the editor is the hurdle and performs triage; moderately impatient audiences who will still read to the end.
- **LD (Lead/Development)**: Newspaper "inverted pyramid" — the entire opening, challenge, and resolution collapse into the lead (possibly one sentence); the rest just develops it.
  - When to use: The most impatient audience; readers who may never reach the end (or whose ending an editor may cut for space).

## Key Concepts
- **Opening (O)**: Introduces characters, setting, and the larger problem being addressed.
- **Challenge (C)**: The specific question or hypothesis the work answers.
- **Action (A)**: The work itself — what you did (paper) or hope to do (proposal).
- **Resolution (R)**: The conclusion — how the characters/world (our understanding) have changed.
- **The lead**: The first sentence(s) that must hook the reader and carry the core of the story up front.
- **Closing the loop**: A good story is circular — the resolution returns to the opening's problem.
- **Spiral, not circle**: The story returns to its starting point, but that point has moved because things changed (figure 4.1).
- **Hourglass structure**: A paper starts wide (opening), narrows to specific questions (challenge/action), and widens back out (resolution).
- **IMRaD**: Introduction, Methods, Results, and Discussion — the common physical layout. "IMRaD is a rule, OCAR is a principle."

## Mental Models
- Use the **patience continuum** to pick structure: OCAR (slowest, most patient) → ABDCE (faster, get into action) → LDR (faster yet, reads to end) → LD (fastest, whole story up front).
- Use **key-location reading** when drafting: readers accept whatever you put at O, C, and R as your opening, challenge, and resolution — put the wrong info there and they get the wrong message.
- Use the **O/C/R skim test**: a reader should grasp your key points from O, C, and R alone, and know whether to read the full paper.
- Use **spiral thinking** to judge a draft: if the resolution doesn't return to (and resolve) the opening's wide problem, the loop isn't closed.

## Anti-patterns
- **No resolution**: Falls into "Theater of the Absurd" (fiction) or the reject bin (science) — readers need to see how the world changed.
- **Lead-based structure in a specialist journal**: Front-loading conclusions looks like you forced data to fit a predetermined story — proving rather than testing.
- **Burying the point at the end for an impatient audience**: Proposal reviewers and Nature/Science editors triage early; "If you haven't told them in the first two pages, you haven't told them."
- **Treating IMRaD as the goal**: Following the physical sections while neglecting the OCAR conceptual story.

## Worked Example
Schimel dissects Watson and Crick's 1953 DNA paper as lead-based (LDR/LD) science. The opening leads with the claim: "We wish to suggest a structure for the salt of deoxyribose nucleic acid... This structure has novel features which are of considerable biological interest." The body develops the structure; the resolution lands the famous understated punch: "It has not escaped our notice that the specific pairing we have postulated immediately suggests a possible copying mechanism for the genetic material." They didn't elaborate the mechanism — it was obvious. The lead states the result up front; the resolution maps back to the opening's "biological interest," closing the loop. He contrasts this with Sue Grafton's *C Is for Corpse* and *The Odyssey*'s *in medias res* opening as pure ABDCE.

## Key Takeaways
1. Build every piece on OCAR — make sure Opening, Challenge, Action, and Resolution are all present and clear.
2. Choose your structure by audience patience: OCAR for specialist papers, LDR for generalist journals, ABDCE or LDR for proposals.
3. Always close the loop — your resolution must return to the wide problem named in the opening, with the point moved (a spiral).
4. Put the right information at O, C, and R; readers read those locations as your message.
5. For impatient audiences (proposal reviewers, Nature/Science editors), front-load — win the no/maybe cut by the end of the introduction.
6. Honor OCAR as a principle even inside IMRaD's rule; let the conceptual story drive the physical sections.
7. Shape papers as an hourglass: open wide, narrow to specific questions, widen back to general conclusions.
