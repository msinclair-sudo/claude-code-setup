# Chapter 19: Writing Global Science

## Core Idea
For scientists writing in English as a second language or from developing scientific cultures, the real barrier to publishing in top journals is not language but doing novel science and structuring a strong story. Story structure transcends language; OCAR isn't about English.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Do good science first — information vs. knowledge**: A paper must deliver new knowledge and understanding, not just add to the information base.
  - When to use: Deciding whether a study is publishable in a high-impact journal, especially when tempted to "answer an old question in a new system."
  - How: Test the work against three failure cases — answering an old question (a) in a new system, (b) with new technology, or (c) both — none of which create novelty. Instead, find what questions the prior work left open: does the pattern break in your system? what is the mechanism? how does it interact with other factors? Ask those new questions.
  - Why it works / failure mode: Repeating "Prof. Genelle found X, I'll see if X holds in my system" reinforces her conclusions but isn't novel — once she did it, it's an old story. Leading journals reject papers that merely flesh out the information base regardless of region (India or Indiana).

- **Target the right audience — scope over status**: Choose a journal by its scope and intended audience, not its prestige.
  - When to use: Before submitting, when torn between being locally relevant and publishing in prestigious venues.
  - How: Read the journal's description and analyze the papers it publishes; email the editor if unsure. Ask whether your story serves local managers/farmers or gives basic scientists new transferable insight, then match the venue. Don't chase impact factor.
  - Why it works / failure mode: This is not basic vs. applied — first-rate applied work also offers broader, transferable insight. Submitting a misfit paper to a high-impact journal wastes everyone's time; good papers get found and cited anywhere via search engines and open access.

- **Story before language (SCFL ordering)**: Structure comes before language; develop and tell the story first, then ensure correct English.
  - When to use: Allocating effort and deciding what matters most when ESL writers fear grammar.
  - How: Follow the SCFL formula from chapter 17 (Story, then Clear, Fluent, Language). Nail the story and structure (OCAR); only afterward fix words, spelling, grammar, and usage.

## Key Concepts
- **Information vs. knowledge**: Information fills the data base; knowledge provides transferable understanding — journals want the latter.
- **Novelty**: The genuinely new finding in a result, distinct from incidental qualifying detail (the "French grassland" is incidental; "fungi more drought-tolerant than bacteria" is the novelty).
- **Confidence and humility**: Doing science demands both — confidence to advance and expose your ideas, humility to admit their limits; the balance is a core challenge.
- **Academic adolescence**: The phase (Schimel's own, mid-PhD) of asking "can I do this?" while learning to present one's own ideas.
- **Scientific culture / culture of inquiry**: An environment that trains researchers to take risks, challenge ideas, and question authority — sometimes harder to acquire than language.
- **SCFL formula**: Story, Clear, Fluent, Language — the priority ordering from chapter 17.
- **Coauthor responsibility**: Native-English-speaking coauthors are responsible for the paper's language; reviewers are "appropriately brutal" when they fail.
- **Scope vs. status**: Match journal by fit and audience, not by impact factor.

## Mental Models
- Use **information vs. knowledge** when judging novelty: ask "does this give understanding, or just one more data point?"
- Use **scope over status** when picking a journal: read its papers and aims, not its impact factor.
- Use **SCFL ordering** when anxious about English: fix the story first — "you can hire an editor to help with the language, but you can't hire a scientist to help with the science."
- Use the **Prof. Genelle test** when a study feels derivative: identify the questions the original left on the table and ask those instead.

## Anti-patterns
- **Answering an old question in a new system/with new technology**: Merely fills the information base; not novel.
- **Submitting misfit papers to high-impact journals**: Pressure to succeed pushes purely-informational work to venues it doesn't fit, wasting reviewers' time and earning rejection.
- **Relying on a friend down the hall for language**: Most friends lack the time or skill for a careful word-by-word edit; use a professional service unless the friend is both a good friend and a good editor.
- **Expecting reviewers/editors/publishers to fix your English**: They won't — papers below standard get bounced before review.
- **Blaming rejection on regional discrimination**: Editors want to broaden the international base; rejection usually means the paper offered information, not knowledge.

## Worked Example
Schimel dissects the hypothetical paper "Fungi are more drought tolerant than bacteria in a French grassland." The novelty is the fungi-vs-bacteria drought finding; "French grassland" is incidental qualifying information. Showing the same pattern in another grassland only reinforces her conclusion — not publishable in a high-profile journal. To create a new story you must find what her work opened up: show the pattern does *not* hold elsewhere (raising why), analyze the *mechanism* of fungal drought tolerance, or test how drought tolerance interacts with other stresses. Those are new questions; the repeat is an old story.

## Key Takeaways
1. Do good science first — push from producing information to producing understanding.
2. Find and highlight the novelty: few data sets are too dull to yield novel insight, few so novel they can't be made dull.
3. Pick the journal by scope and audience, not prestige; email the editor if unsure.
4. Get the story down before the grammar — vote story every time.
5. Own your language: keep a short style reference (Strunk and White), use spell/grammar checkers cautiously, and pay for a professional edit when needed.
6. Make sure native-English coauthors actually do the language editing, and allow time for it.
7. Distinguish basic vs. applied from information vs. knowledge — strong applied work still offers transferable insight.
