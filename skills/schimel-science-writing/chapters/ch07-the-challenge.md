# Chapter 7: The Challenge

## Core Idea
The challenge is the C in OCAR: the moment you state the specific knowledge you hope to gain, anchored by a clear question (optionally framed as a hypothesis and objectives). A challenge that names data-collection objectives but hides the underlying question is weak science and weak storytelling.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **"To learn X, we did Y"**: The conceptual condensation of a good challenge for a specialist journal — state the question/knowledge gap (X), then briefly lay out the approach (Y).
  - When to use: every OCAR/IMRaD challenge in a specialist paper.
  - How: (1) remind the reader of the larger issue; (2) pose the specific question, ideally framed as a falsifiable hypothesis; (3) briefly name the approach/objectives that will answer it.
  - Why it works / failure mode: it satisfies principle 1 (make the reader's job easy) and engages SUCCES curiosity. The dominant failure is dropping the "to learn X" and writing only "we did Y," leaving the reader to reverse-engineer your question — or to suspect you had none.

- **Question before objectives**: The question (knowledge gap) is the core; hypothesis and objectives are optional refinements layered on top.
  - When to use: deciding what to lead the challenge with.
  - How: state the question first; only then transform it into a hypothesis (if your field's culture demands one) and list objectives. Know your field — NSF Biology demands hypotheses; Geosciences is baffled by the obsession.
  - Why it works / failure mode: a hypothesis merely makes the question a falsifiable prediction; questions like "what is the rate constant?" are valid science with no hypothesis. Leading with "Our objectives were" focuses on information collected, not knowledge gained.

- **Telegraphed OCAR**: After a full Introduction, briefly preview the Conclusions ("In this paper we show...").
  - When to use: impatient audiences (common in biomedical literature) who still want to watch arguments develop.
  - How: end the challenge with a short Conclusions overview. More front-loaded than normal OCAR, less than ABDCE or LDR.

- **Intellectual order over chronological order**: When a challenge has multiple goals, lead with the most important (broadest) one, not the first one done in time.
  - When to use: multi-objective studies (e.g., method-validation plus a science question).
  - How: put the core question first; let following objectives elaborate/refine it. Reserve strong verbs (constrain, evaluate) for the main goal, not the subordinate methods step.

## Key Concepts
- **Challenge**: the C of OCAR — where you state the specific knowledge you hope to gain.
- **Question**: the statement of the knowledge gap; the core of the challenge, even when no hypothesis exists.
- **Hypothesis**: a question turned into a falsifiable prediction (Popper); a tool for organizing thought, not a requirement for all science.
- **Objectives**: the specific information/data you will generate; useful as a map but not a substitute for the question.
- **Research approach**: the brief sketch of methods (e.g., culture vs. DNA sequencing) that follows the question.
- **Knowledge gap**: what is not yet known — what the question defines and the challenge must make explicit.
- **SUCCES**: the stickiness checklist; a good challenge taps Simple, Unexpected, curiosity, and Emotion.
- **Telegraphing**: previewing Conclusions inside the challenge to help readers screen papers quickly.

## Mental Models
- Use **"to learn X, we did Y"** when writing any specialist challenge — if you can't fill in X, you don't have a question.
- Use a **hypothesis** when you are testing whether a relationship exists; use a bare **question** when you are quantifying a known relationship (e.g., a rate constant).
- Use **intellectual order** (not chronological) when ranking multiple objectives, so the broadest question reads as the study's thrust.
- Use a **strongly worded question at the end of the lead** when writing for a broad-audience journal (LDR, Science) to ensure the challenge isn't missed.

## Anti-patterns
- **"Our objectives were..." instead of "Our question was..."**: focuses on information, not knowledge; forces the reader to infer your purpose and invites the criticism "why are you wasting my time?"
- **Assuming the question is obvious**: you know your work too well; no reader does. The challenge is too important to leave to assumption, hope, or chance.
- **Chronological ordering of goals**: leading with method-validation makes a climate-change paper look like a narrow measurement-comparison study.
- **An unclear question**: even if you state objectives, a vague question (multiple buried implied hypotheses) leaves readers adrift with no way to know if the paper arrived.

## Worked Example
Schimel dissects Example 7.7 (grassland / global change). The original opens: "The study had two goals. First, we aimed to constrain our estimates of grassland plant production by comparing measurements based on two techniques...; Second, we examined the response of grass growth to a combination of elevated CO2 and increased temperatures." The fault is ordering: chronological, not intellectual. The methods-validation goal leads and gets strong verbs (constrain, compare), while the genuinely interesting climate question is subordinated to second place with the weak verb examine. His fix flips the order and elevates the question: "The primary goal of this study was to evaluate how grass growth responds to a combination of elevated CO2 and increased temperatures, conditions that are expected to occur with climate warming. To validate our plant growth measures, we used two approaches to estimate plant production..." Now the broadly interesting question reads as the study's thrust and the method step is plainly supporting.

## Key Takeaways
1. Always make the question explicit — write "to learn X, we did Y," never just "we did Y."
2. Lead with the question; add a hypothesis only if your field's culture demands one, and know that culture.
3. A question with no hypothesis (e.g., measuring a rate constant) is still real science.
4. Use objectives as a map of your approach, not as a replacement for the knowledge gap.
5. Order multiple goals by intellectual importance, and reserve strong verbs for the core goal.
6. For broad audiences, sharpen the challenge into a charged, confronting question ("completely immaterial?").
7. In a proposal, an unclear challenge is likely fatal — the critical part is "to learn X," not "we did Y."
