# When most of the graph has nothing to say

One way to map a research literature is to take its citation graph, give every paper a vector describing its content, and let those vectors diffuse along the citations, so that linked papers drift toward one another before a clustering step sorts them into topics. A literature-mapping tool called biblion works this way. Each paper's vector is a SPECTER2 embedding, 768 dense numbers encoding what the paper is about. After diffusion, UMAP, a dimension-reduction method that preserves local neighbourhoods, squeezes the result down, and HDBSCAN groups it into clusters. Nothing is labelled; the clusters are the product.

Most of the graph, though, has no vectors. In one example, keeping only nodes with at least two links leaves 1,405 papers with embeddings and 3,392 without, about 70% of the graph. Call these ghosts. Other papers cite them, so they are in the graph, but they bring no content of their own, and they are missing whole, not a few entries at a time.

What follows draws on a single literature review whose findings were not checked by a second reader. Its answers are at best partial and in places simply open.

## What diffusion asks of a node

biblion's diffusion is APPNP, which amounts to a random walk with restart. At each step a node takes the average of its neighbours' current vectors and mixes back in a fixed share of its own original vector, its anchor. In biblion that share is 0.3. The restart matters, because repeated averaging on its own drives every vector toward the same constant, a failure known as oversmoothing (Cai and Wang 2020; Oono and Suzuki 2020).

A ghost has no original vector to return to. It can be given a fabricated anchor, either an imputed vector such as its neighbours' diffusion average or plain zeros, or it can go without one and only pass messages along.

## Does filling in a ghost keep the link it carries?

The case that matters is two real papers, A and B, that both cite the same ghost. Co-citation analysis treats such papers as similar by construction, and network-embedding theory formalises the same intuition as second-order proximity: nodes with shared neighbours should sit close together (Tang et al. 2015). In APPNP the path through the ghost does add to A and B's similarity. That much is built in.

Whether it survives to the clusters is another matter. Three documented mechanisms work against it. Um et al. (2025) proved that diffusion-based imputation under heavy missingness produces channels whose values are nearly identical, and such low-variance channels carry little information. A ghost anchored to a diffusion average is exactly that kind of signal, so the similarity it passes between A and B may be close to a constant. Oversmoothing erodes differences further. And downstream, UMAP can absorb a weak pull between two points, and HDBSCAN can treat a thin bridge as noise.

A zero anchor fails differently. In an averaging operator, zeros shrink the averages of the ghost's neighbours, pulling real papers toward the origin and distorting their geometry (Rossi et al. 2022). Yi et al. (2020) proved, for non-graph models, that zero-filling makes output scale with the fraction of input observed. The review rates this origin-pull argument well supported.

No study shows whether the bridge survives in a pipeline like this, so it has to be measured stage by stage, with care: a ghost with two links is a far weaker conduit than one with fifty, and if A and B end up close because other paths connect them, the ghost has not been shown to do anything.

## How much missingness is too much?

The most quoted number in this literature is that feature propagation, a diffusion method for filling in missing features, lost only 4.12% relative classification accuracy with 99% of features missing (Rossi et al. 2022). It does not transfer. That result hid individual entries at random, so every node kept some observed values; the features were sparse; and the benchmarks were homophilous, meaning linked nodes tended to share labels. Even there, it sank toward a zero-filling baseline as homophily fell. Most methods did well up to about half the features missing, and the gap between methods opened from around 60%.

Each difference from biblion's setting pushes the wrong way. Yun et al. (2024) found that performance fell more steeply when whole nodes were missing. Ferrini et al. (2026) found that methods which looked robust on sparse word-count features failed on dense, low-dimensional ones, and proved why sparse benchmarks look forgiving: when features are mostly empty already, removing more barely changes what they reveal about the labels until over 90% are gone. At 70% ghosts, biblion sits past the point where only the strongest methods held up, and those were tested under friendlier conditions. Published thresholds are optimistic bounds.

The obvious lever is to demand more links per node. That shrinks the ghost fraction but discards nodes and edges, including the bridges at issue, and changes the degree distribution and homophily of what remains. Nobody has quantified that trade-off for citation graphs, and no theorem gives a clean threshold for dense features with whole-node missingness. The review's judgement, pending a sweep nobody has run, is that if requiring three links instead of two pushed the ghost fraction well below half while keeping most bridges, that would probably buy more than any change to the operator.

## A ghost as a conduit

The alternative to fabricating an anchor is to drop the ghost from the anchor term while it still relays its neighbours' averages. There is precedent. Message-passing networks routinely use virtual nodes that route information without contributing features of their own (Gilmer et al. 2017; Cai et al. 2024). Feature propagation effectively does the same: it resets known features every step and lets the unknowns be set by their surroundings, so its end state ignores how the gaps were first filled, which is part of why it holds up where zero-filling followed by a graph network does not. An anchored diffusion with a fabricated anchor turns the ghost into something else, a persistent injector of whatever it was given.

That is the strongest theoretical case for the conduit design, but it is still a design argument: the review searched for a head-to-head test of imputed, zero and conduit ghosts in a pipeline like this and found none. Details remain open: counting ghosts in their neighbours' degree changes the averaging weights, an unanchored ghost with few links may drift loose and be scattered by UMAP, and strengthening the bridge can work against reducing the origin pull.

## The denoising step

Before diffusion, biblion compresses the real vectors with principal component analysis (PCA) to 100 dimensions, fitting it to real papers only, since ghosts have nothing to denoise. How this interacts with ghost handling has not been studied at this missingness and feature density. Reducing dimensions before a graph network preserved accuracy on a complete, sparse benchmark (Morehead et al. 2022). In classical, non-graph PCA, treating missing entries as zeros biases the spectrum downward, and imputing before decomposing or decomposing only observed values introduce different biases. Any rescaling, such as centring or whitening, interacts with the magnitude bias of zero-filling.

Even the direction of the effect is unclear. After denoising, a zero-anchored ghost pulls on centred, reduced-variance vectors, so its distortion could be relatively larger, because each kept dimension weighs more, or smaller, because magnitudes are smaller. It might also push energy back into directions PCA had discarded. The review infers that denoising first is probably the safer order, since diffusion itself smooths and denoising afterwards would smooth twice. That is an inference, and a test of the order says little unless the space ghosts are injected into, 768 or 100 dimensions, varies too.
