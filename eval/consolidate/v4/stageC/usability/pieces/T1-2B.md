# Predicting microbial communities at sites a model has never seen

Suppose you sample soil at a few hundred sites, record climate and soil chemistry, sequence the microbes, and fit a model from environment to community. Then you ask it about a site it has never seen. How close does it get? And does the answer depend on how you describe the community?

A taxonomic profile lists who is present and in what proportions, usually read from a marker gene (16S rRNA for bacteria, ITS for fungi), at any rank from sequence variant to phylum. A functional profile lists what the community can do: the genes, pathways or traits it carries, counted finely (single gene families) or coarsely (broad metabolic categories). A long-standing idea in microbial ecology holds that many unrelated microbes do the same jobs, so the mix of jobs should follow environment more faithfully than the cast list does. If that's right, functional profiles ought to be easier to predict at new sites.

## Where the idea comes from

Most of the support comes from studies that explain variation within one dataset rather than predict new sites. In the global ocean, environment strongly structured the distribution of functional groups but only weakly structured which taxa filled each group (Louca et al. 2016). On the surface of a seaweed, bacterial communities shared about 15% of their species but about 70% of their functional make-up (Burke et al. 2011). In streams, the trait composition of invertebrates, fish and diatoms often lines up with environment more closely, and varies less from place to place, than species composition.

The counter-evidence is just as real. Across 16 soils, functional and taxonomic differences between communities were strongly correlated (Fierer et al. 2012). At a Mediterranean coastal site, function tracked taxonomy once all sequencing reads were used rather than only the reads assigned to known genes (Galand et al. 2018). Across more than a million vegetation plots, community trait composition was only weakly tied to climate and soil (Bruelheide et al. 2018). Much of the stream evidence also comes from one closely linked group of invertebrate researchers, so it is less replicated than the paper count suggests.

But explaining data you already have is a different task from predicting data you don't.

## The comparison that hasn't been made

A literature search aimed squarely at this found no study that predicts a measured gene profile (from a metagenome, which sequences all of a community's DNA) and a taxonomic profile from the same samples and scores both at sites left out of fitting. Every head-to-head comparison it found uses functions derived from taxonomy: each genus is assigned a function from a lookup table, so the functional profile is the taxonomic one regrouped. That is weaker than it sounds. Checked against measured enzyme activity in farm soils, derived function agreed in pattern but not in amount (Breitkreuz et al. 2021).

Three such comparisons exist, and they disagree.

Averill et al. (2021) trained models of soil bacterial and fungal groups on global surveys and tested them on an independent survey of the continental United States (NEON), the cleanest hold-out of the three. The authors conclude that functional groups and high-level taxonomic groups were "equally predictable". Medians recomputed from their supplementary data split by kingdom. For bacteria, derived functional groups scored lowest (median R² 0.15 at site scale, against roughly 0.27 for phyla and 0.51 for orders). For fungi they scored highest (0.23, against 0.01 to 0.13 across ranks). A 2026 reanalysis of the same data with several machine-learning models reports that bacterial functional groups failed under every model (Aouabed et al. 2026).

Werbin et al. (2024, not yet peer reviewed) forecast 170 soil groups at NEON sites and found functional groups more predictable than most taxonomic ones. But that comparison was scored at the sites used for fitting, on later dates. Bacterial functional groups owed their edge largely to persistence over time; they were less sensitive to environment than bacterial taxa. At 13 new sites, forecast error rose by 65% for bacteria against 8% for fungi.

Liu et al. (2023) predicted wastewater-treatment communities from 269 plants in 23 countries. Five derived functional groups, nitrifiers among them, reached 33 to 57% on an absolute-agreement R²; taxonomic levels averaged 33 to 42%. Test samples were drawn at random, so the same plant could sit in both training and test data.

Three hold-out designs, three answers. The only study that predicted measured genes at held-out samples (Faure et al. 2021: about 229,000 marine protein clusters, mean test R² 0.28) had no taxonomic profile to compare against.

## How you score it decides the result

One version of R² asks whether predictions and observations fall on some straight line, which rewards ranking sites correctly even when every prediction is off by a constant factor. Another asks whether they fall on the 1:1 line, where predicted equals observed. In Averill's test, lignin-degrading bacteria scored 0.96 on the first and 0 on the second, and on the 1:1 measure the median was zero for every category except fungal functional groups (0.11), though about one group in six did score above zero. By that stricter standard, transfer between surveys mostly failed for both kinds of profile.

Baselines matter as well. A profile that changes little between sites can be predicted closely by guessing its average everywhere, so low error is not the same as skill. In large European rivers, one study found that environmental models of invertebrate trait profiles gained almost nothing over a mean-profile model (Statzner et al. 2005). No microbial study found scored both profile types against such a baseline. In Werbin's forecasts, models using seasonality alone matched or beat six-variable environmental models for most groups.

## Resolution

Does predictability change as groupings get coarser? For taxa, not in any consistent way. In Averill's supplement bacteria peak at order; in Werbin's forecasts they peak at class, and fungi do best at broad ranks. For Swiss alpine soil bacteria, finer or coarser sequence clustering made no difference (Mod et al. 2021). The 2026 reports, including a NEON study that used airborne canopy reflectance as predictors, lean towards broad groups doing best. But broad groups are also more abundant, abundance is the strongest correlate of predictability, and none of these studies separates the two.

For functions there is nothing at held-out sites: no study found compares the predictability of single genes, pathways and broad categories. The nearest evidence is from plants, where modelling a community's average trait directly beat assembling it from species predictions (R² 0.60 against 0.54 for plant height; Deschamps et al. 2023).

## What helps and what hurts

Common taxa are predicted better than rare ones, in three separate microbial studies. Flexible models overfit: one fitted soil bacteria with a correlation of 0.97 and kept 0.21 at new sites (Mod et al. 2021). Random test splits flatter models compared with holding out whole regions; in a benchmark of 33 community models on plant and animal data, the type of prediction task explained nearly as much of the variation in performance as the choice of model (29% against 33%; Norberg et al. 2019). Persistence through time helps forecast the same site next season and does nothing at a new one. For derived functions, the rule used to define groups matters: groups backed by genomic evidence predicted better than groups defined from the literature alone.

What would settle the main question is a study that sequences genes and taxa from the same samples, holds out whole sites or regions, scores both profile types at matched levels of grouping on absolute agreement, and compares each against its own average. Nobody seems to have run it yet.
