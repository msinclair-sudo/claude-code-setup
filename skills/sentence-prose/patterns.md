# Patterns — Reader-Expectation Revision Techniques

Concrete, repeatable moves from Gopen & Swan. Each is a diagnostic + fix.

## Close the subject-verb gap
**When to use**: The verb is delayed by a long phrase, list, or aside (count the words from subject to verb).
**How**: (a) If the intervening material matters, promote it to its own clause with a semicolon or a second sentence; (b) if it's a mere aside, delete it so the prose drives to its point.
**Trade-offs**: Choice (a) vs (b) depends on author intent — only the author knows whether the material is significant. (a) adds a stress position; (b) sacrifices the detail for directness. (Ch 2)

## Place the point in the stress position
**When to use**: The most important new idea isn't landing at a point of closure.
**How**: Identify the single emphasis-worthy idea; rearrange syntax so it sits just before a period, colon, or semicolon.
**Trade-offs**: Only one primary stress position per closure — if you have several ideas to stress, you need several closures (see next). (Ch 3)

## Open extra stress positions with colon/semicolon
**When to use**: A sentence has more stress-worthy candidates than available stress positions (the real definition of "too long").
**How**: Insert a colon or semicolon; ensure the material *before* it stands alone as a complete sentence. Each closure gives one more emphasis slot.
**Trade-offs**: Over-use fragments the sentence; the lead-in must be grammatically complete. (Ch 3)

## Put old information in the topic position
**When to use**: Sentences open with new material; the paragraph's focus shifts every sentence.
**How**: Begin each sentence with the old, backward-linking information and the concept whose story it is; push new/emphatic material to the stress position.
**Trade-offs**: Requires deciding *whose* story the paragraph tells — an interpretive act, not a mechanical one. (Ch 4)

## The topic-string test
**When to use**: A paragraph "feels" disconnected though each sentence seems fine.
**How**: Read only the topic positions in sequence. If they don't form a single coherent story, the focus is drifting — re-thread one consistent old-information strand through them.
**Trade-offs**: None; pure diagnostic. (Ch 4)

## Choose voice by paragraph focus, not by rule
**When to use**: Deciding active vs. passive ("Bees disperse pollen" vs. "Pollen is dispersed by bees").
**How**: Pick whichever puts the paragraph's continuing subject in the topic position. Passive is correct in a paragraph about pollen.
**Trade-offs**: Rejects the blanket "always active" rule; demands awareness of the surrounding story. (Ch 4)

## The stress→topic hand-off (gap detection)
**When to use**: Checking a passage for missing logic.
**How**: Verify that the new information stressed in one sentence reappears as old information in the next. A dropped thread marks an unstated connection — write it in (often new sentences), don't assume it.
**Trade-offs**: Filling real gaps adds length; that's correct, not bloat. (Ch 5)

## The verb test (locate the action)
**When to use**: A passage names many players but you can't tell what's happening.
**How**: List the verbs in order. If they're mostly *is/are/has* and the list doesn't tell the story, find the true actions, convert them to strong verbs, and pair them with a consistent topic-position actor.
**Trade-offs**: May reveal that the key action was never stated — a substance gap, not a style one. (Ch 6)

## The seven-principle revision pass
**When to use**: Final edit of any dense passage.
**How**: Check each principle once: (1) subject→verb close, (2) point in stress position, (3) story-owner in topic position, (4) old info in topic position, (5) action in the verb, (6) context before new, (7) structural emphasis = substantive emphasis.
**Trade-offs**: Apply as principles, not rules — expect to violate some deliberately; expect the pass to surface conceptual gaps. (Ch 7)
