# Chapter 14: Energizing Writing

## Core Idea
Energy in a sentence lives in its verbs: put the action into a strong verb placed early, right after the subject, so the reader can see the actor do something ("show, don't tell"). Three habits steal that energy — passive voice, fuzzy verbs, and nominalizations — and each should be used only deliberately.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Three ways to emasculate your verbs**: The chapter's organizing list of energy thieves — (1) passive voice, (2) fuzzy verbs, (3) nominalizations.
  - When to use: As a revision checklist for any dull, heavy, or unclear sentence.
  - How: Scan each sentence; find the real action; ask whether it sits in a strong, active verb early in the sentence. If the verb is a form of "to be" + action ("was called"), it's passive; if it tells-not-shows ("occur," "facilitate"), it's fuzzy; if the action hides in a noun ("an investigation"), it's a nominalization.
  - Why it works: The active subject-verb-object structure matches **OCAR** (O = who did it, A = what they did, R = what happened), the order readers process most easily. Failure mode: stacking these devices buries the action and lengthens the sentence with no added information.
- **Active versus passive voice**: Active = Actor/Subject + Action/Verb + Acted-on/Object ("John called Jane"); passive makes the acted-on the subject and couples "to be" to the verb ("Jane was called by John").
  - When to use passive: (1) controlling perspective — getting the right term into the topic position so the sentence fits its paragraph (given-before-new flow); (2) honestly hiding the actor (e.g., uncredited technicians, "samples were collected"; or unattributed prior work, "It has been argued").
  - How: Choose the grammatical subject by who/what the story is about, not by who acted. Otherwise follow Strunk & White: "Use the active voice."
  - Why it works: Passive weakens one sentence but can let it lock into the paragraph, strengthening the whole. Failure mode: the old tradition of passive-for-objectivity — objectivity comes from how you treat your data, not your writing voice.
- **Action verbs vs fuzzy verbs**: Fuzzy verbs (occur, affect, facilitate, conduct, perform, implement) say something happened but not what; action verbs (modify, increase, inhibit, invade, disrupt, accelerate) show what physically happened.
  - When to use: Especially in paragraph-opening sentences and hypotheses.
  - How: Ask "what does the actor physically do?" Replace "facilitate the invasion" with "eat native plants, giving exotic grasses a competitive advantage that allows them to invade."
- **Nominalization**: Turning a verb (investigate → an investigation) or adjective (different → difference) into a noun, which forces in a weak filler verb.
  - When to use (deliberately): To name a concept and define a new **schema** ("gene expression," "ecosystem services"); to encapsulate prior text ("the arguments developed above"); for rhetorical mass (Churchill's "the rescue and the liberation").
  - How: As a rule, turn nominalized verbs back into verbs; convert nominalized adjectives back to adjectives, ideally all the way to a verb.

## Key Concepts
- **Show, don't tell**: Bring the actor on stage to act rather than reporting the action from a distance.
- **OCAR**: Opening-Challenge-Action-Resolution; the active sentence mirrors O (actor) → A (action) → R (result).
- **Topic position / stress position**: Front of the sentence (what it's about) and end (emphasis); passive lets you control both.
- **Fuzzy verb**: A verb that names that something happened without showing what.
- **Nominalization**: A verb or adjective converted into a noun, draining action and adding length.
- **Schema**: The mental concept a name evokes; a nominalization works only if the reader holds the schema, otherwise it becomes jargon.
- **Fuzzy hypothesis**: An unfalsifiable, wishy-washy claim ("is controlled by...") that reads as a truism and lands proposals on the "do not fund" list.
- **The last straw**: A single unnecessary nominalization won't kill writing; the accumulation of them does.

## Mental Models
- Use the **active voice** by default; switch to passive only when you need the acted-on as subject or have an honest reason to hide the actor.
- Use the **"what do they physically do?" test** when a verb feels fuzzy — push the concrete action into the verb.
- Use **nominalizations deliberately** when naming a shared concept or adding rhetorical weight, and convert them otherwise.
- Use a **strong, falsifiable verb in hypotheses** ("is the single strongest control") because challenging proposals get funded and boring ones never do.

## Anti-patterns
- **Passive-for-objectivity tradition**: Removing yourself from the prose does not remove your bias — "We did the work, and we wrote the words."
- **Fuzzy hypotheses**: "Microbial community composition is controlled by..." is a truism, not testable; reviewers reject it.
- **Nominalizing the key action**: "a failure to reproduce the experimental data is often observed" buries the action and dumps "is often observed" into the stress position.
- **Timidity**: Using fuzzy verbs to avoid being challenged — it's your job to be thoughtful, not always right; "being interesting is ultimately more important than being right."

## Worked Example
Schimel dissects Example 14.10: "Although models exist to calculate reaction rates as a function of molecular size, a failure to reproduce the experimental data is often observed." This stacks a nominalization ("a failure") with a passive ("is often observed"), giving "the minimum possible punch" — the passive phrase grabs the stress while the real point hides mid-sentence. His revision: "Although models exist to calculate reaction rates as a function of molecular size, they often fail to reproduce the experimental data." Opening the second clause with "they" ties back to the models (given-before-new), restores a tight subject-verb connection, and lands the action "they often fail" at the start of the main clause where it counts.

## Key Takeaways
1. Find the action in each sentence, put it in the verb, and put the verb early.
2. Default to active voice; reach for passive only to fix perspective or to hide an actor honestly.
3. Replace fuzzy verbs (occur, affect, facilitate) with concrete action verbs that show what physically happens.
4. Convert nominalized verbs and adjectives back into verbs — they add length and steal energy without adding information.
5. Write hypotheses with strong, declarative, falsifiable verbs; nebulous-and-timid loses funding.
6. Keep deliberate nominalizations only for naming shared schemas or rhetorical weight; don't accumulate the straws.
