# Chapter 11: Paragraphs

## Core Idea
A paragraph is a unit of composition only when it tells a complete short story with coherent structure that fits the larger work. You can build a paragraph from any of the book's story structures — the central choice is whether to make the point first and develop it, or build to the point last.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Point-first vs. point-last paragraphs** (Joseph Williams): every paragraph either makes its point early and fleshes it out, or assembles threads and resolves to the point at the end.
  - When to use: deciding a paragraph's architecture before drafting it.
  - How: point-first = TS-D or LD; point-last = LDR or OCAR. Each paragraph needs an opening that sets the stage and a resolution that makes a point — but neither must be a single sentence or the first sentence.
  - Why it works: structures form a spectrum from all-power-in-first-sentence (pure TS-D) to all-power-in-last (pure OCAR). Failure mode: a "point-nowhere" paragraph — okay to be point-first or point-last, never point-nowhere.

- **TS-D (topic sentence–development)**: the grade-school topic-sentence-then-development model; a simplified version of LD story structure.
  - When to use: most paragraphs — it is the "bread-and-butter" paragraph and should dominate your writing.
  - How: state the point in the first sentence, develop it in the rest. If a whole paper were TS-D you could read only first sentences and get its essence.
  - Why it works: clean, scannable, low-effort for the reader. It is how Schimel earlier advised writing Methods and Results (ch. 8). Limit: long or critical paragraphs need more.

- **LD (lead/development) paragraph**: like TS-D but the lead spans several sentences, narrowing to a point that the rest expands on.
  - When to use: long openings or funnels where one topic sentence can't carry the setup.
  - How: build a multi-sentence lead that sharpens from general to specific, ending on the point; then develop.

- **LDR paragraph**: opens with an argument (lead), develops it, then wraps up with a synthesis — strong at both opening and resolution.
  - When to use: long paragraphs that need a resolution to tie them together and remind the reader of the point.

- **OCAR paragraph**: opening sentence introduces the issue without framing an argument (sets the stage / poses question + approach = opening and challenge), action develops, last sentence synthesizes the conclusion.
  - When to use: point-last paragraphs at critical story points where you assemble an argument toward a conclusion.

- **Three-step paragraph fix**: (1) identify the real story / who it's about, (2) decide point-first or point-last, (3) pull apart the tangled threads and place critical pieces in the right positions.

## Key Concepts
- **Unit of composition**: Strunk & White's second principle — make the paragraph the unit; a coherent self-contained short story.
- **Paragraph-as-cell**: paragraphs are to writing what cells are to life — internal cohesion plus external connection.
- **Point-first / point-last**: the two macro-modes of paragraph structure.
- **Spectrum of structures**: TS-D → LD → LDR → OCAR is continuous, not discrete; weighting of one sentence can shift the classification.
- **Curse of knowledge**: when authors know too much but write too little, ideas get overcondensed and jumbled into incoherence.
- **Point-nowhere paragraph**: a paragraph with no identifiable point — the cardinal failure.
- **Unlearning simplifications**: advanced training means moving past the schemas (like rigid TS-D) introductory classes taught.

## Mental Models
- Use **point-last (LDR/OCAR)** when you must weave threads into a conclusion; use **point-first (TS-D)** for the routine 70–75% of body paragraphs.
- Use a **resolution sentence** when a paragraph runs long — short paragraphs are usually TS-D, long ones lean LDR/OCAR.
- Use **complex structures at story junctions** — openings, resolutions, transitions — and plain TS-D for the body.
- When structure is unclear, suspect the point is too — clarity of structure signals clarity of thought.

## Anti-patterns
- **Stringing sentences until you need air, then a break**: produces no unit of composition, no story.
- **Forcing everything into rigid TS-D**: you can use any story structure; over-applying the grade-school schema flattens critical paragraphs.
- **Point-nowhere paragraphs**: multiple aimless threads, no identifiable point (example 11.6).
- **Over-condensing from the curse of knowledge**: jamming obvious-to-you connections together so the reader can't reconstruct them.

## Worked Example
Schimel dissects example 11.6 on grassland restoration. The paragraph weaves four unrelated threads — compost boosts phosphatase/P-release; *Bromus carinatus* is a useful native grass; mycorrhizal inoculation aids it; its effects are unknown — so neither point nor structure is identifiable. The real story is "two approaches to restoring degraded grasslands"; *B. carinatus* is incidental and derails the paragraph. Applying the three-step fix (find the story → choose point-first LD → separate the threads), he rewrites it (example 11.7) into two paragraphs: an LD paragraph stating restoration needs adequate P and naming the two approaches (compost, mycorrhizae), and an LDR paragraph framing *B. carinatus* as a useful but secondary species. He added explanation rather than condensing: "If you need to condense, condense elsewhere."

## Key Takeaways
1. Make each paragraph a complete short story with an opening that sets the stage and a resolution that makes a point.
2. Default to TS-D — it should dominate; reserve LD, LDR, OCAR for long paragraphs and story junctions.
3. Decide point-first or point-last deliberately; never write a point-nowhere paragraph.
4. Give long paragraphs an explicit resolution to remind the reader of the point.
5. To fix a bad paragraph: name the real story, choose its structure, then separate and reposition the threads.
6. Fight the curse of knowledge — spell out connections that are obvious to you but not to the reader; take the space critical ideas need.
