# Can the environment tell you what lives somewhere it has never seen?

Measure the environment (climate, soil chemistry, vegetation) and the community at a set of sites, fit a model linking the two, then ask the model what lives at a site it was not trained on. The community can be described as a taxonomic profile, the abundances of species or broader groups, or as a profile of what those organisms can do, their genes or traits. It is tempting to guess the second predicts better: if many microbes can do the same job, the environment might select for the job more reliably than for whichever organism does it.

That guess has barely been tested at sites the model has not seen. For microbes there are two kinds of functional profile, and the difference matters. A measured gene profile comes from sequencing all the DNA in a sample (a metagenome) and counting genes or pathways. A derived functional profile is built from a taxonomic profile by looking up what each genus is known to do and adding up the abundances of, say, nitrogen fixers or chitin degraders. The derived kind is cheap, and not independent of taxonomy.

## The direct comparison has not been made

No study was found that predicts a measured gene profile and a taxonomic profile from the environment and scores both at held-out sites, habitats, studies or periods. The one held-out study of measured gene content, Faure et al. (2021), modelled protein clusters in 93 ocean metagenomes from 52 environmental variables and reached a mean test R² of 0.28. Its test samples were a random quarter of the data, not whole stations or regions, and there was no taxonomic model to compare against.

Three studies do compare a derived functional profile with a taxonomic one at held-out units, and they disagree. Averill et al. (2021) calibrated models of soil bacterial and fungal groups on global surveys and tested them on NEON, an independent survey spanning the continental USA. The authors conclude that functional groups and high-level taxonomic groups were equally predictable. Their supplementary data, at the site scale, split by kingdom. On a correlation-type measure (more on measures below), bacterial functional groups had a median R² of 0.15, lower than phyla (0.27) and every other bacterial rank. Fungal functional groups had 0.23, higher than fungal phyla (0.12) and higher than any fungal rank. Werbin et al. (2024, a preprint) forecast 170 soil groups at NEON sites and found functional groups more predictable than most taxonomic groups, but scored that comparison on later dates at the sites used for fitting, where a group's recent abundance already says a lot about its next value. Liu et al. (2023), predicting bacteria in 269 wastewater treatment plants across 23 countries, found R² of 33 to 42% across taxonomic levels and 33 to 57% for five functional groups, but tested on a random fifth of samples, so the same plant could sit on both sides of the split.

With every hold-out design different, these results cannot be pooled, and the disagreement cannot be pinned on any one cause. A 2026 machine-learning reanalysis of the same data (Aouabed et al.), whose hold-out design is unverified, found bacterial functional groups failed under every model on Averill's data while fungal ones reached R² 0.45 on NEON.

The idea that function tracks environment comes mostly from studies that explain variation within one dataset. Louca et al. (2016) found ocean functional groups strongly structured by environment while the taxa within each group were not; Fierer et al. (2012) and Galand et al. (2018) found function closely tied to taxonomy instead. In freshwater, trait composition has been found closer to environment than species composition (Göthe et al. 2017; Soininen et al. 2016), but that pattern comes largely from stream invertebrates studied by one linked group of researchers, and across more than a million vegetation plots community plant traits were only weakly tied to climate and soil (Bruelheide et al. 2018). Fitting well inside a dataset is not the same as transferring to a new one.

## Resolution: broad or fine?

For microbial taxonomic rank, the held-out studies show no shape that holds across them. In Averill's supplement, bacteria peak at order (median R² 0.51), with phylum lowest. Fungal medians bounce around without a trend (phylum 0.12, class 0.01, order 0.11, family 0.13, genus 0.08), although the authors state that fungal predictability rises with taxonomic scale, perhaps from a different summary. Werbin et al. find bacteria best at class and fungi best at broad ranks. Liu et al. report no clear trend. Mod et al. (2021) found the sequence-similarity threshold defining bacterial units made no difference. Two 2026 reports lean towards broader groups predicting better, one of them using airborne canopy reflectance rather than measured environment as its predictor. The catch throughout is that broader groups are more abundant, and abundance was the strongest correlate of predictability in Werbin's data. No study has separated the two.

On the functional side there is nothing: no held-out comparison of individual genes, pathways and broad categories, and none of the two profile types at matched levels of aggregation. The closest thing to a head-to-head comes from plants, where modelling community plant height directly beat predicting each species and adding them up (R² 0.60 against 0.54; Deschamps et al. 2023).

## What makes prediction work or fail

How the test data are chosen changes the answer. In a benchmark of 33 species distribution models on five plant and animal datasets, the prediction task (interpolating within the sampled conditions or extrapolating beyond them) explained 29% of the variation in performance (Norberg et al. 2019). Machine-learning models that fit well in-sample often transfer poorly when whole blocks of space are held out (Wenger and Olden 2012), yet most microbial studies split at random. Mod et al. show the cost of flexibility: a gradient-boosting model reached a correlation of about 0.97 on the sites it was fitted to and 0.21 at held-out sites, against 0.53 and 0.26 for a simpler additive model. In Werbin's data bacteria had much lower error than fungi at the calibration sites but lost 65% of their forecast skill at new sites, against 8% for fungi. Bacterial functional groups gained their skill from persistence through time, which does not carry to a new place.

The scoring rule matters as much. A best-fit R² asks whether predictions rise and fall with observations. An R² against the one-to-one line asks whether they hit the observed values. Under the second measure in Averill's cross-study test, the median at site scale was zero for every category except fungal functional groups (0.11), though about 21 of 130 individual groups scored above zero. The lignin-degrading bacterial group scored 0.96 on the first measure and zero on the second. The ranking of kingdoms and profile types changes with the measure.

Then there is the baseline. No microbial study scored both profile types against simply predicting the average profile, or last season's. Where baselines appear, they are humbling. In Werbin's data, models using seasonality alone matched or beat six-variable environmental models for most groups. For invertebrate trait profiles in large European rivers, landscape models gained almost nothing over a mean profile (Statzner et al. 2005), though whether that test used held-out reaches is unclear.

Common, widespread taxa are predicted better than rare ones, in soil, in wastewater and in plants and animals. For derived functional groups, the rule assigning genera to functions mattered: groups backed by genomic pathway evidence as well as the literature were more predictable (Werbin et al. 2024).

The study that would settle the question is easy to describe: one dataset with both metagenomes and taxonomic profiles, whole sites or regions held out, scored against the observed values and against a mean-profile baseline, at several matched levels of resolution. It has not been done.
