# Chapter 20: Writing for the Public

## Core Idea
Writing for the public is not a different language from writing for peers — it is the same craft done "more and less": more story and SUCCES, less complexity and jargon. The whole book has secretly been about this all along.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **The Message Box** (after Nancy Baron): A graphical tool for laying out a story's essential elements before drafting, arranged around two central frames (Issue, Audience) with four numbered points read clockwise.
  - When to use: Framing any science story — for the public, peers, reporters, or proposals — when you need to sort and define the SUCCES elements first.
  - How: Set **A. Issue** and **B. Audience** as the overarching themes in the center. Then clockwise: **(1) Problem** = your specific problem (defines opening + challenge, conflated into a clear lead); **(2) So what?** = why this audience should care (integrate into the lead for LDR); **(3) Solution** = the body (the A in OCAR, the D in ABDCE); **(4) Benefits** = what the audience gets (the resolution).
  - Why it works: It forces the OCAR/LDR architecture and the "so what?" before you write a word, and it prepares you for the reporter who calls. Failure mode: skip the audience frame and your "so what?" lands wrong — your aunt wanders off impressed but baffled.

- **Five Rules for Writing Stories for the Public**: (1) **Story** — a simple story is critical; (2) **Focus** — target the "who knew?" element or the application, and talk about the people doing the work; (3) **Story structure** — use a front-loaded structure, most likely **LDR**, to engage SUCCES fast for an impatient audience; (4) **Language** — define or avoid every technical term, never introduce a technical term in the **topic position**, don't be patronizing (assume intelligent but ignorant); (5) **Scientific method** — show science as incremental process, not "Eureka!" product.
  - When to use: Any popular article, blog, op-ed, or radio piece.
  - How: Lead with the hook, conflate opening and challenge into the lead, strip jargon, foreground human characters and application.

## Key Concepts
- **The flow of concern**: The public's progression from Data → Information → Knowledge → Understanding → Application; nonscientists care about the application end.
- **"So what?"**: The audience's reason to care; it differs for colleagues vs. relatives and must be matched to the audience.
- **Schema**: The audience's existing mental frame (e.g., your aunt's schema is "curing cancer"); start the explanation there or lose them.
- **"Who knew?" story**: A curiosity piece that shows what science is and how it works (e.g., frogs firing leg muscles before landing).
- **Process, not product**: Science advances incrementally through many people; not lone breakthroughs.
- **Application pushback**: When fixing a problem (global warming) is feared to make lives worse, resistance cascades back from application all the way to the data.
- **Scholars, not experts**: Scientists live where no one knows the answer; the struggle is to find the question.
- **Dumbing down (what it is NOT)**: Highlighting the simple core and telling it in the audience's language — not patronizing them.

## Mental Models
- Use **"more and less"** when shifting from peer to public writing: more story/SUCCES, less complexity/jargon — change degree, not method.
- Use the **audience's schema as your opening** when the gap is wide: start with what they already care about (curing cancer), then build outward to gene cascades.
- Use **application framing** when asked "how does this affect me?" — the public's payoff is use, not insight.
- Use the **message box** whenever you'd otherwise start drafting blind, including for proposals and peer writing.

## Anti-patterns
- **Introducing a technical term in the topic position**: It reads as jargon and blocks the reader at the very front of the sentence where the schema should sit.
- **Leading with "Eureka!" framing**: Misrepresents how science actually works and sets false expectations.
- **Assuming peers/journalists will relay the message**: They struggle to understand the work too; you must tell the story yourself.
- **Patronizing the audience**: Assume intelligent but ignorant; "dumbing down" insults and loses them.
- **Expecting policy makers to enter your world**: Their decisions are political as much as natural; effecting change means working in their world, not yours.

## Worked Example
Schimel builds a message box for basic research on gene-regulation cascades studied in **zebrafish**. Issue: *curing cancer*. Audience: *your aunt / the public*. Problem: *we don't understand the genetic interactions that cause normal cells to "go bad."* So what?: *that switch is the critical step in developing cancer.* Solution: *understand the switch — which needs a good model system, the zebrafish.* Benefits: *figuring it out may let us develop treatments.* The resulting story ("Gene Cascades in Zebrafish") opens on the aunt's schema — "Cancer is what happens when normal cells start growing and dividing out of control" — then lands the honest, non-grandiose close: "So, yes, I hope that my work will ultimately contribute to curing cancer." It works because it has a clean **OCAR** structure and plays the **SUCCES** elements: Simple (the regulation breakdown), Emotional (a question the aunt cares about), Concrete and Credible (how the work fits the larger search), and simple language. A second box (arctic tundra winter CO2) shows the same tool driving a real **proposal** opening — keeping the "last one out, turn out the lights" line.

## Key Takeaways
1. Don't treat public writing as a separate skill — do everything you already do, just more story and less jargon.
2. Fill out a message box before drafting: nail Problem, So what?, Solution, Benefits against a named Audience.
3. Lead from the audience's schema and conflate opening + challenge into a single clear lead (LDR).
4. Never put a technical term in the topic position; define or avoid it.
5. Foreground people and either the "who knew?" hook or the application.
6. Be accurate and honest — frame contribution modestly ("may eventually contribute") rather than promising a cure.
7. For policy makers, story structure and the closing ask matter most — they listen hardest for "what you are asking them to do."
