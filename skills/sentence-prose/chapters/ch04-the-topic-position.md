# Chapter 4: The Topic Position

## Core Idea
The beginning of a sentence — the **topic position** — sets the reader's perspective ("this is a story about whoever shows up first") and should carry **old information that links backward**. Filling it with new information is the #1 problem in professional writing.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The topic position**: The slot at the start of a sentence; readers expect *perspective and context* there ("First things first").
  - When to use: Deciding what a sentence should *open* with.
  - How: Begin with the old/already-introduced information that links back to prior discourse and names whose story this sentence tells.
  - Why it works: Readers expect a unit of discourse to be about whoever appears first, and they look there for linkage (backward) and context (forward). Satisfy that and the paragraph reads as one continuous story.
- **Old information vs. new information**: Old = already encountered in this discourse (belongs in topic position). New = first appearance (belongs in stress position when emphasis-worthy).
- **The principle (not a rule)**: *"Put in the topic position the old information that links backward; put in the stress position the new information you want the reader to emphasize."* It is explicitly **not** "old in topic, new in stress" — the middle of every sentence is also full of old and new.

## Key Concepts
- **Linkage (backward) + context (forward)**: The two jobs of topic-position material.
- **Whose story is it**: The topic-position occupant declares the sentence's subject of focus.
- **Active vs. passive is a non-issue here**: "Bees disperse pollen" and "Pollen is dispersed by bees" are both fine — choose by *whose story the paragraph is telling*. Passive is superior in a paragraph about pollen.

## Mental Models
- Read only the topic positions of a paragraph in sequence: if they don't tell a coherent, single story, the focus is shifting and the reader is lost.
- Think of old information as a handrail — the reader grips it to climb into each new sentence.

## Anti-patterns
- **New information in the topic position**: Forbids comfort at the start and arrival at the end; misleads the reader about whose story it is. The single most common defect in American professional writing.
- **Choosing voice by ideology** ("always active") instead of by paragraph focus.

## Worked Example
The earthquake paragraph's topic positions, read in sequence, lurch between unrelated new subjects:
> Large earthquakes / The rates / Therefore...one / subsequent mainshocks / great plate boundary ruptures / the southern segment of the San Andreas fault / the smaller the standard deviation...

Almost all are *new* information in the spot reserved for *old* — so no two readers build the same story. The recurring old thread ("recurrence interval") rarely opens a sentence. Revision rule applied to each sentence: (1) backward-linking old info in topic position, (2) the concept whose story it is in topic position, (3) new emphasis-worthy info in stress position. The revision threads "recurrence time / interval" through the topic positions, turning seven disconnected facts into one continuous argument — and in doing so *exposes* missing transitions ("however?", "indeed?", "for example?") the original had hidden.

## Key Takeaways
1. Open sentences with old, backward-linking information — context before new material.
2. Whoever occupies the topic position is who the sentence is "about"; keep the focus consistent across a paragraph.
3. New, emphasis-worthy information belongs in the stress position, not the opening.
4. Misplaced old/new information is the #1 problem in professional writing.
5. Choose active vs. passive by whose story the paragraph tells, not by rule.
