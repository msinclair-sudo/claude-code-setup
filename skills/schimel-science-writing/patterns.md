# Patterns & Techniques — Writing Science (Schimel)

## OCAR (Opening–Challenge–Action–Resolution)
**When to use**: Structuring any unit — whole paper, section, paragraph, or sentence (specialist-journal default).
**How**: Opening names the driving problem + characters; Challenge poses the specific question; Action is the work; Resolution shows how understanding changed and loops back to the opening.
**Trade-offs**: Most patient structure; in specialist journals it reads as honest testing. Too slow for impatient audiences — use a front-loaded variant.

## Choosing a story structure by audience patience
**When to use**: Deciding how front-loaded to be.
**How**: OCAR (specialist papers) → LDR (generalist journals, Nature/Science) → ABDCE (proposals, mystery/adventure) → LD (newspaper, most impatient; whole story in the lead).
**Trade-offs**: Front-loading hooks impatient readers but forces you to double back for background; patient structures build conviction but lose triage-driven readers.

## SUCCES stickiness checklist
**When to use**: Before drafting, to plan what makes the work memorable.
**How**: Simple (find the core), Unexpected (frame a knowledge gap), Concrete (ground abstractions), Credible (unbroken evidence chain), Emotional (engage curiosity), Stories (modular sub-stories).
**Trade-offs**: Simple risks sliding into simplistic — distill, don't dodge.

## The Funnel (connecting O and C)
**When to use**: The introduction — moving from a wide opening to a specific question.
**How**: Start broad, then progressively narrow each paragraph until the knowledge gap is the only thing left to fill. Sell the *problem* before the solution (the "bizzwidget" rule).
**Trade-offs**: Too wide a funnel promises more than you deliver; too narrow loses the broader audience.

## The Challenge: "To learn X, we did Y"
**When to use**: Stating the question/objective at the end of the introduction.
**How**: Lead with the question (learn X), then the approach (did Y) — question before objectives. Telegraph the coming OCAR.
**Trade-offs**: Objectives-first reads as a to-do list and buries the intellectual stake.

## Lead/Development (LD) in the body
**When to use**: Methods and Results — serving both skimmers and replicators.
**How**: One lead sentence states the goal or major pattern; full detail follows. Never dump unframed detail; never give detail then synopsize.
**Trade-offs**: Costs a sentence up front; saves readers from drowning.

## Story over statistics
**When to use**: Reporting any quantitative result.
**How**: Lead with the effect size ("increased 3.4-fold"); append the p-value as supporting credibility.
**Trade-offs**: None — a p-value is not a finding.

## Resolution: synopsize → synthesize → contribute
**When to use**: The final section of the Discussion.
**How**: Step backward through OCAR — restate findings, synthesize them into understanding, state the contribution; optionally end on a forward question. Close the loop to the opening.
**Trade-offs**: No resolution = the reject bin; a limitation in the resolution = "more research is needed" as your lasting message.

## Nested arcs + power positions
**When to use**: Auditing internal structure of long sections.
**How**: Build hierarchical OCAR arcs; put critical content in beginnings and endings (power positions); check each arc has all four elements.
**Trade-offs**: Putting routine material in power positions wastes them.

## Topic / Stress (Joseph Williams)
**When to use**: Every sentence, especially when the same facts could tell different stories.
**How**: Old/familiar info → topic position (start); new info, key terms, the main message → stress position (end of main clause).
**Trade-offs**: New info at the topic signals "this is old," confusing readers.

## Flow: stress-to-topic linkage (Janus / reach back and grab)
**When to use**: Connecting consecutive sentences into a story (not a list).
**How**: The stress of one sentence feeds the topic of the next ("reach back and grab"). Topic-to-topic linkage instead makes a list.
**Trade-offs**: Story linkage builds momentum; list linkage is right only for genuine parallel enumerations.

## Energize the verbs
**When to use**: Any flat, abstract sentence.
**How**: Hunt the three emasculators — passive voice, fuzzy verbs, nominalizations — and restore the real action as an active verb following its subject (actor-action-acted-on).
**Trade-offs**: Passive is occasionally right (when the actor is unknown/irrelevant or needed at the stress).

## Words: jargon vs. technical term
**When to use**: Introducing any specialized vocabulary.
**How**: A term is fine if the audience holds the schema; otherwise define it — and never introduce a new term in the topic position (it belongs at the stress, per 2-3-1). Prefer Anglo-Saxon over Latinate words.
**Trade-offs**: Over-defining patronizes experts; under-defining loses generalists.

## Condensing: prune, then shake
**When to use**: After the story works, to cut length.
**How**: Prune the four delete-key targets — empty amplifiers ("very," "really"), redundancies, throat-clearing, and dead weight — then "shake" the sentence so remaining words settle into a tighter structure.
**Trade-offs**: Over-trimming creates noun trains; sometimes condense the *change-words* instead.

## SCFL editing sequence (real editing)
**When to use**: Every revision pass.
**How**: Fix in order — **S**tory → **C**lear → **F**luent → **L**anguage. Edit top-down and cyclically (each pass exposes new problems; cycle back to Structure). Read the final draft aloud, standing, from a clean printout.
**Trade-offs**: Polishing language before fixing the story wastes effort on sentences you may cut.

## "But, yes" — dealing with limitations
**When to use**: Any real limitation that could become the reader's parting impression.
**How**: Name it early, constrain conclusions to fit, explain how you handled it, drop it, end on the science. Often the cheapest fix is revising the *question* to match the method.
**Trade-offs**: Hiding limitations backfires — reviewers find them and "go negative."

## The Message Box (writing for the public / proposals)
**When to use**: Framing a story before drafting, especially for non-specialist audiences.
**How**: Center on Issue + Audience; clockwise fill Problem, So what?, Solution, Benefits — these map onto OCAR/LDR. Lead from the audience's schema; never put a technical term in the topic position.
**Trade-offs**: Skip the audience frame and the "so what?" lands wrong.
