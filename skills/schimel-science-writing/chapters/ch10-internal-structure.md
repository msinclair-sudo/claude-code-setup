# Chapter 10: Internal Structure

## Core Idea
A paper isn't one story arc but a hierarchy of nested arcs — sections, subsections, paragraphs, and sentences each open, build, and resolve their own OCAR. Coherence comes from making each unit a complete, self-contained arc and linking them in series, never interleaving them.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Nested arcs (hierarchical OCAR)**: Every level of organization repeats the OCAR pattern — opening, challenge/action, resolution. The whole paper is a story arc; so is each section, each subsection module, each paragraph, each sentence. Small arcs nest inside larger ones, like quarks within protons within atoms within molecules.
  - When to use: Whenever structuring or revising at any scale — section, subsection, or paragraph.
  - How: For each unit, give context (opening), develop one point (action), and close it (resolution) before moving on. A method module = what info you wanted + what you did to get it. A result module = overview + specifics + significance.
  - Why it works: We learn by placing new information into an established framework; each completed arc becomes the context for the next, letting readers build complex stories one manageable piece at a time. Failure mode: "spaghetti" writing where ideas reappear in multiple places.
- **Power positions (beginnings and endings)**: The start and end of every arc are emphasis slots that signal "this is important."
  - When to use: When deciding where to place key points and how to mark arc boundaries.
  - How: Put the important information at beginnings and endings; create discrete arcs so those power positions exist and are visible. Linked complete arcs yield four power positions (start, end, two clean junctions); interspersed arcs muddle even the opening and ending.
- **Arc-checking questions (internal structure audit)**: A five-question revision pass run paragraph by paragraph, section by section.
  - When to use: Final structural revision.
  - How: (1) Does each unit make a single, clear point? (2) Are linkages among paragraphs in a section clear? (3) Has every extraneous thought breaking the serial arc been removed? (4) When you introduce a topic, do you resolve it before introducing a new one? (5) Is every major unit defined by a subhead or clear opening text? Anything less than "yes" means the structure isn't finished.

## Key Concepts
- **Story arc**: The flow of opening → development → resolution that builds and then rewards curiosity.
- **Spiral structure**: A story moves forward but returns at the end to where it began (closure to the opening).
- **Module**: A subsection packaging one complete idea — a single method, data set, or argument — as its own arc.
- **Power position**: Beginning or ending of an arc, where information is emphasized.
- **Flag words**: Linking/transition words (however, consequently, in addition) that signal arc boundaries and connections.
- **Thematic coherence**: A unit holds together because all its sentences serve one theme.
- **Arcless / stream-of-consciousness writing**: Putting each thought down as it arises, so the text flows sentence-to-sentence but goes nowhere.
- **Subhead**: A label identifying an arc's theme and marking its boundary.

## Mental Models
- Use the **programming analogy** when modularizing: good writing is built from internally complete subroutines passing specific processed information; bad writing is a plate of spaghetti.
- Use the **dizzy-pattern test** when ideas recur: if interleaving three arcs is painful to look at on the page, it's just as painful to read.
- Use the **sentence start/end scan** when a paragraph flows but drifts: list what each sentence opens and closes with; if they chain into new characters endlessly, there's no real arc.
- Use the **trash-or-own-arc rule** for extraneous thoughts: if interesting and germane, give it its own arc elsewhere; otherwise delete it.

## Anti-patterns
- **Interspersing arcs**: Breaking up and interleaving ideas so each topic appears in multiple places destroys power positions and makes the reader dizzy.
- **Flow without direction**: Sentences that link tightly but keep introducing new characters (California → fish → upwelling → winds → climate → CO2 → acidification → shellfish) create local cohesion but no thematic coherence and no closure.
- **Stream-of-consciousness drafting surviving to final draft**: Experienced writers cut extraneous thoughts; the mark of inexperience is leaving them in.

## Worked Example
Schimel's deliberately incoherent paragraph (Example 10.3): a single California-fisheries paragraph where every sentence builds off the last — fisheries→algae→nitrogen→upwelling→winds→climate→greenhouse/CO2→ocean acidification→shellfish→"CO2 will affect marine ecosystems." Each sentence is a clear declarative and links seamlessly, yet the paragraph "flows nowhere in particular." It keeps adding new characters and never closes back to the opening, so the reader can't tell whether the topic is climate effects on California fisheries or fisheries as an example of climate effects on oceans. The fix (Example 10.4): split into two linked arcs. Arc one resolves the fisheries-via-ocean-circulation story ("Thus, the productivity of California fisheries will likely change as a result of climate warming"). Arc two, joined by "In addition," pulls acidification into its own arc connected through the climate–CO2 link. Result: SUCCES-type simplicity — a series of simple messages adding to one larger simple message. Schimel also dissects Example 10.2 (Vitousek's stoichiometry), showing two paragraphs each forming an independent arc — the first keeping plants in the stress position ("Even the leaf litter..."), the second opening with "Consequently... animals and microbes" to make consumers the characters — linked by "consequently."

## Key Takeaways
1. Write every section, subsection, and paragraph as a complete OCAR arc — open, develop, resolve — before moving to the next.
2. Resolve one topic fully before introducing the next; never let an idea reappear in scattered places.
3. Put important information in power positions: the beginnings and endings of arcs.
4. Link arcs in series with subheads, paragraph breaks, and flag words (however, consequently, in addition).
5. Mark resolutions explicitly — phrases like "We conclude" flag a closing point.
6. Cut extraneous thoughts from the final draft; if worth keeping, give them their own arc elsewhere.
7. Audit structure with the five arc-checking questions; if any answer isn't "yes," you're not done.
