# Glossary — The Science of Scientific Writing (Gopen & Swan)

**Action (in the verb)** — The expectation that what *happens* in a sentence is expressed by its verb, not buried in a noun (Ch 6).

**Context-before-content** — Readers want orientation/perspective before being asked to process new material; principle #6 (Ch 1, Ch 7).

**Flag word** — A signal (e.g., "both") that tells the reader to expect multiple pieces of new information in one stress position (Ch 5).

**Interruptive material** — Words intervening between subject and verb; structurally read as low-importance regardless of actual value (Ch 2).

**Linkage and context** — The two jobs of the topic position: connect backward to prior discourse (linkage) and prepare for what follows (context) (Ch 4).

**Logical gap** — An unstated connection between ideas, obvious to the writer but invisible to the reader; surfaced by old/new analysis (Ch 5).

**Mental breath** — The attention/tension a reader summons at a sentence's start and releases at its close, producing emphasis at the end (Ch 3).

**New information** — Material making its first appearance in the discourse; belongs in the stress position when emphasis-worthy (Ch 4).

**Old information** — Material already encountered in this discourse; belongs in the topic position to link backward (Ch 4).

**Principle (not rule)** — A reader-expectation guideline that informs judgment but can be violated to good effect; there is no algorithm for good writing (Ch 7).

**Reader expectation** — The relatively fixed assumption about where in a structure a kind of information will appear; foundation of the whole methodology (Ch 1).

**Reader-expectation methodology** — Basing structural choices on how readers actually interpret prose (rhetoric, linguistics, cognitive psychology) (Ch 1).

**Shape** — The quality prose has when a writer intuitively satisfies reader expectations (Ch 1).

**Skillful violation** — Deliberately breaking an expectation for effect, effective only against a background of mostly satisfying expectations (Ch 7).

**Stress position** — The location of natural reader emphasis, at the point of syntactic closure (sentence end, or before a colon/semicolon); "save the best for last" (Ch 3).

**Subject-verb separation** — A long gap between a grammatical subject and its verb; reads as an interruption and delays syntactic resolution (Ch 2).

**Substance ↔ structure interplay** — The principle that identical facts in different structures mean different things; structure directs interpretation (Ch 1).

**Syntactic closure** — The moment the reader knows nothing remains in the clause but what is now being read; where the stress position begins (Ch 3).

**Syntactic resolution** — The reader's need to reach the verb to learn what the subject is doing; unmet while subject and verb are separated (Ch 2).

**Topic position** — The beginning of a sentence; readers expect perspective, context, and old/backward-linking information there; "first things first" (Ch 4).

**Unit of discourse** — Anything with a beginning and an end (clause, sentence, paragraph, section, article); each carries reader expectations (Ch 1).

**Verb test** — Reading a paragraph's verbs in sequence to check whether they convey the actual actions (Ch 6).

**Whose story** — The convention that a unit of discourse is "about" whoever occupies its topic position; focus should stay consistent across a paragraph (Ch 4).
