# Chapter 12: Sentences

## Core Idea
A sentence is the shortest possible story, so the same OCAR logic that governs papers and paragraphs governs sentences: map the grammatical subject-verb-object onto opening-action-resolution, then place old information at the topic and new information at the stress so readers can process each piece in turn.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Sentence as OCAR story**: Treat grammatical units as story units carrying OCAR functions.
  - When to use: Every sentence; the default lens for diagnosing weak prose.
  - How: Subject = Opening (who/what the sentence is about); Verb = Challenge/Action (what happened); Object = Resolution (the outcome). Present these elements in the most convenient order, establishing a framework and then dropping new information into it.
  - Why it works / failure mode: English puts the strongest emotional weight on the last word of the main clause, so simple sentences invariably follow OCAR. Give information out of order (hammer before board) and readers must hold pieces aside.

- **Topic and Stress (Joseph Williams)**: The opening position is the "topic," the closing position is the "stress."
  - When to use: Whenever the same facts could tell different stories.
  - How: Put at the topic an old/familiar schema or character (common knowledge or something you introduced earlier); develop it by adding new information. Put the main message, new ideas, and new terms at the stress (end of the main clause; each clause end is a minor stress).
  - Why it works / failure mode: Readers interpret whatever opens a sentence as "what this is about." Put new information first and you signal it is old, confusing the reader. Three elements in one sentence create six potential messages — structure, not facts, picks the story.

- **2-3-1 rule of emphasis (Roy Peter Clark)**: Weighting follows stress (2) at end carries most, topic (1)... actually stress carries greatest, topic next, middle least — order of emphasis stress > topic > middle.
  - When to use: Deciding where to park a qualification you must keep but don't want to feature.
  - How: Bury low-priority but necessary material in the middle (the 3-position); feature the point at the stress.

- **Subject-verb connection**: The verb should immediately follow the subject.
  - How: Never insert a parenthetical between subject and verb. Move set-off phrases out of the middle to the right.

- **Right-opening LD sentence (Clark)**: For long sentences, make the key point in a short initial main clause, then add subordinate clauses for depth.
  - When to use: Long sentences, where straight OCAR fails because readers wait too long for the point.
  - How: Sketch the main story in a tight opening clause (short subject + action verb), then color in the picture — build a schema, then grow it.

## Key Concepts
- **Topic**: The sentence opening; the single character/schema the sentence is about.
- **Stress**: The end of the main clause; the power position carrying greatest emphasis.
- **Schema**: A familiar mental framework readers already hold, onto which new information is hung.
- **2-3-1 rule**: Emphasis order — stress strongest, topic next, middle weakest.
- **Subject-verb connection**: Tight adjacency of actor and action; gaps make sentences dull and confusing.
- **Given-before-new**: Old information at topic, new at stress (the house-building analogy).
- **Buried topic / buried stress**: The real subject or point is hidden mid-sentence by added clauses.
- **Right-opening (LD) structure**: Long-sentence design with the lead point first, elaboration trailing.
- **Dangling words**: Trailing words after the real stress that drain emphasis; delete or relocate them.

## Mental Models
- Use the **house-building image** when ordering information: hand over board, then nail, then hammer — given before new.
- Use the **2-3-1 rule** when you must keep a hedge or qualification but not feature it — park it in the middle.
- Use **right-opening LD** when a sentence must be long — lead with the point, then add nuance to the right.
- Use the **"six possible messages" test** when three facts share a sentence — choose which story, then structure for it.

## Anti-patterns
- **Opening with new/unfamiliar information** (e.g. "Net mineralization..."): signals it's old and makes the sentence opaque.
- **Inserting a comma-set parenthetical between subject and verb** (Ex 12.4): disconnects actor from action.
- **Long incidental clause before the real topic** (Ex 12.6 "In this study, taking advantage of..."): readers skim it lacking a framework.
- **Dangling words after the stress** (Ex 12.7/12.8 "...products on the market", "...within plant tissues"): weaken the punch and add ambiguity.
- **Opening with nameless agents** (Ex 12.5 "It has been predicted that..."): splits actors; make the real subject the topic.
- **Over-explaining / writing into a corner**: trying too hard to be careful buries both topic and stress (Ex 12.10).

## Worked Example
Schimel rescues Ex 12.10, a 21-word-subject monster: "The qualitative agreement between caribou's preference for feeding on young leaves and the trend for protein to decline with leaf age supports the hypothesis that caribou migration is driven by the patterns of leaf-out and maturation spatially and temporally through their home range, rather than by weather." Simple idea, drowned. **Step 1 — fix the topic**: make it caribou's food preference, in under 20 words: "Caribou prefer to feed on young, protein-rich leaves..." (one-word topic, verb immediately following). **Step 2 — fix the stress**: the original stress was "weather"; it should be the argument that caribou follow food. **Step 3 — finish**: package leftover material into the middle: "Caribou prefer to feed on young, protein-rich leaves, supporting the hypothesis that migration is not driven by weather but by the spatial and temporal patterns of leaf-out and maturation." Tighter topic-action-stress, three clean clauses — clearer not merely because shorter.

## Key Takeaways
1. Find the topic, make it the subject, move it toward the beginning of the sentence.
2. Find the action verb and connect it closely to the subject — no parentheticals between them.
3. Find the stress and move it to the end of the main clause; cut or relocate dangling words.
4. Open with old/familiar schema, end with the new term or main message (given-before-new).
5. Remember three facts make six stories — choose the story, then structure for it.
6. For necessary hedges, use the 2-3-1 rule and bury them in the middle.
7. For long sentences use a right-opening LD design: lead point first, then add nuance to the right.
