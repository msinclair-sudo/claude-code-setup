# Notes on writing the piece

## 1. Wanted or needed, not in the material

- **Why anyone wants this prediction.** Nothing on the practical stakes (biogeochemical models, bioassessment of microbes, wastewater management, forecasting under climate change). The opening had to lean on a generic "suppose you measure..." instead of a real use.
- **The hypothesis behind the question.** The material never states why function might be more predictable than taxonomy. I used "functional redundancy", which appears only in a reference title (Galand et al. 2018) and in Chen et al. 2022 on redundancy. No source in the material sets out the argument, and one "Do not write" entry warns against framing evidence against a hypothesis the literature does not state. So the opening motivation is mine, kept as light as I could.
- **What the numbers mean in practice.** No guidance on what counts as good skill: is a held-out R² of 0.26 or 0.28 useful or poor? No comparison with skill in plant and animal community models, which would have given a lay reader a reference point.
- **The CRPS figure.** "Bacteria losing 65% of CRPS skill" and "mean CRPS worse by 65% against 8%" are both given. I could not tell whether this is a 65% rise in error or a 65% loss of skill relative to a baseline, so the piece says only "a probabilistic error score worsened by 65%".
- **Werbin's "91% and 50% accuracy".** It is quoted from the abstract with no indication of what was being measured, so I left it out.
- **Mechanisms.** Nothing on *why* abundance helps (sampling noise, detection, compositional effects), why high immigration lowers skill, or why fungi and bacteria behave differently. I had an obvious gloss for immigration ("arrivals swamp local selection") but cut it because the material does not offer it.
- **Basic orientation for outsiders.** No plain account of 16S/ITS amplicon sequencing versus shotgun metagenomics, what NEON is, what an ASV or OTU is, or how lookup tables such as FAPROTAX assign function. I wrote short glosses from the material's own definitions and kept the jargon out where I could.
- **The canopy-reflectance study.** No authors or title (cited as "Anonymous 2026"), so its reference entry is a placeholder description plus DOI.
- **Aouabed et al. 2026.** No first names or initials in the material, and its hold-out design is unknown, so I could not say how much weight it carries.
- **Averill main text.** The authors' claim that fungal predictability rises with rank, against the supplement's non-monotonic medians, cannot be resolved from the material. The piece has to leave it hanging ("perhaps from a summary not consulted here").
- **Measured gene profiles in other settings.** No held-out microbial studies at all in freshwater, sediments or most engineered systems, and marine work beyond Faure 2021 was not searched. So the piece can only say that nothing turned up, not that nothing exists.

## 2. Hard to use

- **Format.** The view is organised as a list of claims, each with "Holds for" and "Evidence" tags. Every claim is hedged in a slightly different way ("limited by access", "single source", "the research does not say whether this was searched for", "rated Moderate; a second reader disputes this"). Turning those into proportionate plain-language caveats meant choosing which hedges a general reader needs. Most got dropped; some (preprint, abstract only, random split) survived.
- **Corrections layered on corrections.** The D1–D3 answers are "as corrected by a second reader", and then a "Do not say" section corrects further wordings. Working out the final, safe form of a claim (Averill's "equally predictable", Statzner 2005, the freshwater trait pattern, rank direction) meant cross-reading three places. The "Say instead" texts are written in the reviewers' register, not reader-ready prose.
- **Internal pipeline language.** "The critic", "the reviewer's reading", "the review's search", "Case A/B/C", "D1 single-source sensitivity", "stage-2 routing check", "the brief's evidence bullet". None of it means anything to the target reader, and stripping it out without losing the provenance distinctions (author claim vs reanalyst's recomputation) took care.
- **Two kinds of R² with near-identical labels.** "Best-fit R²", "1:1 R²", "correlation-type R²", "median correlation", "mean 1:1 R²", "test-set R²" are used across studies. Which R² Liu's 33–57% and Faure's 0.28 are was clear for Liu (1:1) but not stated for Faure, so cross-study numbers are hard to place side by side (the material itself says they are not commensurable).
- **Averill duplicate-row ranges.** Phylum medians given as "0.23 to 0.30" in one place and "0.27" in the kingdom-split sentence. I used 0.27 for the functional-versus-phylum comparison as the material does, and only said "phyla lowest" in the rank paragraph.
- **Hold-out status unclear for several sources** (Statzner 2005, Hawkins 2000, Ladau 2018, Larsen 2012, Kruk 2011). These were mostly unusable for a piece about held-out skill, which left the bioassessment side, potentially the richest non-microbial evidence, almost empty. I omitted it rather than explain why each item does not count.
- **Explanatory versus held-out mixing.** About a third of the material is explanatory (within-dataset) evidence. It is relevant background but answers a different question; deciding how much room to give it in a 1,200-word piece on held-out prediction was a judgement call.
- **Self-contradiction on access.** The Gaps section notes that the source note lists several sources as "not obtained" while also citing them as read in abstract. It did not change what I wrote, but it undercuts confidence in the access labels.
