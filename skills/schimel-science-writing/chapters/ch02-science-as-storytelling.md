# Chapter 2: Science Writing as Storytelling

## Core Idea
A scientific paper is not a presentation of results; it is a story about nature built from data. The papers that get cited and the proposals that get funded are those that find and tell the most compelling story — objectively, professionally, and without imposing a preconceived plot on the data.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Elaborate-and-slash (bottom-up / top-down) story development**: Develop your story from the bottom up, then tell it from the top down.
  - When to use: Whenever you have a dataset and need to decide what the paper is actually about, before outlining or drafting.
  - How:
    1. Start with the data; think about them; listen for the story they are trying to tell.
    2. Listen not just to loud proclamations but to quiet, uncertain mutterings — the shoulder on the spectrum, the nonsignificant effect that might be real, the outlier.
    3. Overinterpret wildly: explore the farthest fringes and develop the story expansively.
    4. Then take Occam's razor and slash away to find the simple core.
    5. Tell that distilled story top-down in the paper.
  - Why it works / failure mode: If you grab the first simple story that comes to mind, you impose "plot" onto your characters (Lamott's trap). Only by exploring the boundaries of the data do you find the *important* story rather than the obvious one.

- **Data → Information → Knowledge → Understanding (the flow of science)**: A four-stage path that raw measurements travel to become valuable.
  - When to use: To diagnose how far your paper has taken the reader, and to push it further.
  - How: Raw data (e.g., Photo 51) → information (atomic structure of crystallized DNA) → knowledge (the double-helix structure) → understanding (how that structure enables replication and heredity). The further along this path you carry your work, the more people can assimilate and build on it. If you stop short, readers are left searching for the meaning themselves.

- **Key Story Points (Yanai exercise)**: Four short answers (2–3 sentences each) that fix the essential story before drafting.
  - When to use: At the start of any paper or short article, to lock the skeleton.
  - How: (1) Opening — the larger problem you contribute to, the direction, why it matters, engaging the widest practical audience; (2) the specific question or hypothesis; (3) the key results (a short list, 2–3 points max); (4) the main conclusion — use the results (3) to answer the question (2) and address the larger problem (1).

## Key Concepts
- **Characters**: The subjects of a science story — not people but molecules, organisms, ecosystems, genes, or concepts (e.g., nitrogen cycling in arctic tundra). You "listen" to them by analyzing data with an open mind.
- **Listening to your characters**: Drawing the story out of the data rather than imposing it; attending to outliers and faint signals, not just the clean central relationship.
- **Imposing plot**: The failure mode where you know the story before collecting data and jam the data into that frame (Lamott: "PLOT").
- **Understanding (vs. data)**: The ultimate goal of science; data are supporting actors, while the questions and larger issues are the lead actors.
- **Three aspects of storytelling**: Content (what makes a story engage and stick), structure (how content is assembled so readers get it), and language (how to write it compellingly) — the organizing plan of the whole book.
- **Bottom-up / top-down**: Build the story upward from data; deliver it downward from the big idea.

## Mental Models
- Think of your data as **supporting actors** and your questions and larger issues as the **lead actors** — the story grows from the data, but the data are not the story.
- Use the **data-to-understanding flow** as a ruler: ask "how far did I take the reader?" and try to advance one stage further.
- **Overinterpret, then prune**: treat exploration and distillation as two separate phases, not one — never let the first easy story end the inquiry.
- Think of cross-cultural failure as **"speaking louder in 'science'"**: when outsiders don't understand, repeating jargon more forcefully reads as arrogant and gets you dismissed.

## Anti-patterns
- **"Presenting results" instead of telling a story**: Outlining everything you did and saying something about each piece buries the one story trying to come out.
- **Imposing a preconceived plot**: Knowing the story before the data, then forcing data to fit — destroys objectivity and misses the real finding.
- **Ignoring outliers**: Focusing only on the tight central relationship throws away the most novel, important science (Dietrich would have missed the mudflow wedges).
- **Publishing prematurely**: One good paper can launch a career; many mediocre ones build a different kind of career.
- **Stopping at data or information**: Leaving readers to extract knowledge and understanding themselves.

## Worked Example
**Bill Dietrich's PhD on hillslope soil depth.** Dietrich measured how hillslope steepness controlled soil depth in the Pacific Northwest. Most points fit a tight relationship (steeper slope → thinner soil) — a perfectly good, publishable story. But some soils were far deeper than they "should" have been: outliers. He could have ignored them and told the clean story. Instead he listened to those characters and asked what created the deep soils. He found that uneven bedrock forms hollow "wedges" along the slope; over time these fill with debris and soil, become invisible on the landscape, and in heavy rain can flush out as lethal mudflows. The outliers — not the average — became the focus of his early career and produced more novel, exciting, and important science. This is "elaborate and slash" in action: he explored the fringe his clean regression told him to discard.

Schimel pairs this with the DNA case to anchor the data-to-understanding flow: Franklin's Photo 51 (data) was meaningless to most; Watson and Crick's double-helix sketch built from it (knowledge → understanding of heredity) became foundational. Raw data have limited direct value; the model that interprets them carries the power.

## Key Takeaways
1. Find the story buried in your data and give it to us — do not just outline everything you did.
2. Develop bottom-up, tell top-down: explore the data's fringes expansively, then slash to the simple core with Occam's razor.
3. Listen to your characters — including the quiet, uncertain signals and the outliers, which often hold the real story.
4. Never impose plot: if you know the story before the data, you are jamming data into a frame, not doing science.
5. Push your paper as far along Data → Information → Knowledge → Understanding as you can; that is your goal as an author.
6. Before drafting, nail the four Key Story Points (opening, question, key results, conclusion) so the conclusion answers the question and closes the opening.
7. Storytelling is fully professional and objective; the discipline is content, structure, and language.
