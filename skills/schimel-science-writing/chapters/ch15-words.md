# Chapter 15: Words

## Core Idea
Words are to sentences what atoms are to molecules: they control the chemistry and voice of your writing. Choose short, light, active words and necessary technical terms; strip out unnecessary jargon, heavy Latinate vocabulary, and clunky prepositional phrases—being professional without being pedantic.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Jargon vs. Technical Term test**: A word is *jargon* if (A) it refers to a schema the reader does NOT hold, or (B) an adequate plain-language equivalent exists. It is a *technical term* if (A) it refers to a schema the reader DOES hold, and (B) there is no plain equivalent (or the plain word would confuse).
  - When to use: Every time you reach for a specialized word and aren't sure it's earning its place.
  - How: Ask "Does my reader hold this schema?" and "Is there a clear common word?" The boundary is fuzzy and audience-dependent—"net primary production" is a technical term to ecologists but jargon to a public audience; "plant growth" is the reverse.
  - Why it works / failure mode: Forces you to anchor word choice to the reader's knowledge, not your in-crowd status. Failure mode: undefined acronyms (the worst jargon)—e.g., opening a paper with "DCs are APCs that initiate immunity" excludes every new reader.

- **Where-in-the-sentence rule for introducing terms**: The position you place a term signals how well you assume readers know it.
  - When to use: Introducing a term that is borderline—too familiar to define formally, not familiar enough to assume.
  - How: **Beginning (topic position)** = you assume everyone knows it (risk: it reads as jargon). **End (stress position)** = you define it for everyone (risk: looks ignorant if it's an accepted schema). **Middle (the 3-position, set off in a parenthetical clause)** = you assume most readers know it and signal it isn't critical—it feels like a gentle reminder. This is Clark's **2-3-1 rule of emphasis** from ch.12.
  - Why it works / failure mode: The middle-clause reminder educates novices without alienating experts. When in doubt, err toward overdefining—an expert's irritation is slight and short-lived; a novice's confusion can be permanent (they stop reading).

- **Anglo-Saxon over French/Latin**: Prefer short Anglo-Saxon words to their long French/Latin synonyms (eat>consume, start>initiate, show>demonstrate, use>utilize, death>mortality, need>necessary).
  - When to use: Whenever true synonyms exist; default to the shorter, lighter word.
  - How: Anglo-Saxon words feel comfortable/casual, French formal, Latin like jargon. Words ending in *-ate* are Latin (heavy); *-ion* are French. Check origins in the OED. Exception: keep the Latin/French word when connotation differs (a fire "consumes" but does not "eat" fuel).

- **Compound noun vs. prepositional phrase**: Turn prepositional phrases around into compound nouns to condense ("rate of reaction" → "reaction rate"), unless doing so creates a "noun train."
  - When to use: Editing for tightness; prepositional phrases attract nominalizations and passives.
  - How: 2 nouns → compound is almost always better; 4 nouns → break it up with a preposition; 3 is judgment-based (depends on word complexity and whether two words lump into one unit, e.g. "science fiction writer" is fine, "resource allocation modification" is a mess).

## Key Concepts
- **Schema**: The mental model a reader already holds; whether they hold it decides jargon vs. technical term.
- **2-3-1 rule of emphasis**: Clark's ordering—strongest info at end (1/stress), second at start (2/topic), throwaway in middle (3); used to embed term-reminders mid-sentence.
- **Emotional weight of words**: A word's historical origin (Anglo-Saxon/French/Latin) carries feeling—"soil" (French, elegant) vs. "dirt" (Old Norse "drit," excrement, negative).
- **Nominalization**: An action buried as a noun (e.g., "application," "binding"); flipping prepositional phrases can restore the verb.
- **Noun train (noun cluster)**: Three-plus nouns stacked as modifiers ("resource allocation modification"); worse than a prepositional phrase; coined by Ruth Yanai.
- **Overqualified term**: Adding words for false precision—"climate regime" (= climate), "terrestrial autotrophs" (weaker and oddly broader than "plants").
- **Acronym spell-out rule**: Chicago Manual—spell out terms on first occurrence; exception only for universally known ones (DNA, not deoxyribonucleic acid).
- **Topic position / stress position**: Sentence slots (old info / new info) that control whether a term reads as assumed-known or newly-defined.

## Mental Models
- Use the friend test when a big word tempts you: "Would I use this word talking to a friend?" If not ("the therapy was efficacious"), swap in the spoken version ("the treatment worked").
- Use the reader-impression check: recall the last hard-to-read paper—were you impressed, or frustrated? Readers notice awkward language and blame themselves; good words make them notice the ideas.
- Use overdefining when audience knowledge is uncertain: the cost of irritating an expert is far smaller than the cost of losing a novice.
- Use a prepositional phrase deliberately to break a noun train, to disambiguate noun-vs-verb, or to land the right phrase in the stress ("limit the effects of global warming" puts "global warming" in stress).

## Anti-patterns
- **Heavy Latinate showing-off**: "initiate," "utilize," "demonstrate" signal club membership, not communication—Dutton: "Actual communication has nothing to do with it."
- **Undefined acronyms**: APC left unexplained forces readers online; assumes everyone who ever reads the paper already knows.
- **Naming for your convenience**: labeling forests ASP/HBR (sample sites) instead of DEC/CON (deciduous/coniferous) adds to the reader's memory load.
- **False precision**: "climate regime," "causative agent of," "terrestrial autotrophs"—extra words that sound technical but blur meaning.
- **Reflexive nominalization + prepositional phrase**: "binding of Red22 to..." instead of "Red22 binding to..."

## Worked Example
Schimel dissects Example 15.1 (Moskun et al.). The authors place the well-known but not-universal term *linear response theory* in a mid-sentence clause set off by commas: "This idea that excited states relax... commonly called linear response theory, is a critical component...". The topic introduces concepts any chemist knows; the term's name sits in a local stress (end of its own clause) yet the whole clause sits mid-sentence (the 3-position), so it reads as a comfortable reminder, not a fresh definition. Result: a 37-word sentence—longer than readability calculators like—that is *clearer* and reaches the broadest audience without alienating the core. He pairs this with Example 15.2 ("Programmed cell death, or apoptosis,...") showing the same parenthetical-reminder move.

## Key Takeaways
1. Run every specialized word through the schema test: keep it only if readers hold the schema and no clear plain word exists.
2. Apply the friend test—if you wouldn't say it aloud, find the spoken word.
3. Position borderline terms with the 2-3-1 rule: bury the reminder in a mid-sentence clause; err toward overdefining.
4. Always spell out acronyms on first use (except DNA-level universals); name things for the reader's convenience, not yours.
5. Default to short Anglo-Saxon words; treat *-ate* and *-ion* endings as a warning to check for a lighter synonym.
6. Flip prepositional phrases into compound nouns to tighten—but break four-plus-noun "noun trains" back apart with a preposition.
7. Distrust words added for "precision" ("regime," "agent of," "autotrophs"); the common word usually engages a stronger schema and hits harder.
