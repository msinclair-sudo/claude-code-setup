# Glossary — Writing Science (Schimel)

**ABDCE** — Action, Background, Development, Climax, Ending; a front-loaded story structure that opens on action and backs up to fill in background. Used for proposals and impatient audiences (Ch 4).
**Action (A)** — The third OCAR element: the work done or proposed; the main body (Methods, Results, most of the Discussion) (Ch 4, Ch 8).
**Anglo-Saxon vs. French/Latin** — Prefer short, concrete Anglo-Saxon words over longer Latinate ones for force and clarity (Ch 15).
**Arc** — A self-contained story unit (with its own OCAR) nested inside a larger story (Ch 10).
**"But, yes"** — Air limitations early, then end on the positive science; the inverse of the weak "yes, but" that makes the limitation the resolution (Ch 18).
**Challenge (C)** — The second OCAR element: the specific question or hypothesis the work answers; the knowledge gap (Ch 4, Ch 7).
**Closing the loop** — Ending so the resolution returns to the problem named in the opening (a spiral, not a circle) (Ch 4, Ch 9).
**Concrete** — The C in SUCCES: grounding abstractions in data or familiar images; concreteness underpins credibility (Ch 3).
**Credible** — The second C in SUCCES: an unbroken chain from prior work → methods → data → conclusions (Ch 3).
**Data / Inference / Interpretation** — Three kinds of material every paper handles: actual results; robust near-universal readings; and speculative hypotheses (Ch 8).
**Emotional** — The E in SUCCES: engage curiosity, the one acceptable scientific emotion; excitement wins proposals an advocate (Ch 3).
**Energizing** — Putting the action of a sentence into a strong active verb (Ch 14).
**Funnel** — The narrowing from a wide Opening to a specific Challenge, connecting O and C (Ch 6).
**Fuzzy verbs** — Weak verbs (is, makes, performs) that hide the real action; one of three ways verbs get emasculated (Ch 14).
**Given-before-new** — Put old/familiar information at the topic, new information at the stress (Ch 12, Ch 13).
**Hourglass structure** — A paper that opens wide, narrows to specific questions, then widens to general conclusions (Ch 4).
**IMRaD** — Introduction, Methods, Results, Discussion; the physical layout. "IMRaD is a rule, OCAR is a principle" (Ch 4).
**Janus function** — A sentence's topic looks backward (links to prior sentence) while its stress looks forward; named for the two-faced god (Ch 13).
**Jargon** — A middle-rung technical term used where the audience lacks the schema; distinct from a legitimate technical term (Ch 3, Ch 15).
**Knowledge gap** — The specific unknown framed within known territory; identifying it creates curiosity, filling it creates novelty (Ch 3, Ch 6, Ch 7).
**Ladder of abstraction** — Continuum from broad simple ideas (top) to physical data (bottom); the dangerous middle holds expert-only jargon (Ch 3).
**LD (Lead/Development)** — Newspaper "inverted pyramid": the whole story collapses into the lead, the rest develops it; for the most impatient audience (Ch 4, Ch 8, Ch 11).
**LDR (Lead/Development/Resolution)** — Magazine structure: engaging lead, development, satisfying resolution; for generalist journals (Ch 4).
**Lead** — The opening sentence(s) that hook the reader and carry the core of the story up front (Ch 4, Ch 5).
**Message box** — A graphical pre-writing tool (Issue, Audience, Problem, So what?, Solution, Benefits) that lays out the story before drafting (Ch 20).
**Mental models** — see schema.
**Nominalization** — A verb or adjective turned into a noun ("adjustment," "responsiveness"), burying the action; the third verb-emasculator (Ch 14).
**Novelty** — The genuinely new finding; created by filling a knowledge gap, not by answering an old question in a new system (Ch 3, Ch 19).
**OCAR** — Opening, Challenge, Action, Resolution; the four universal story elements and the book's master framework (Ch 4, Ch 21).
**Opening (O)** — The first OCAR element: identifies the driving problem, introduces characters, targets an audience (Ch 4, Ch 5).
**Pawn push vs. launch the queen** — Editing move: cut a weak setup sentence (pawn) so the strong sentence (queen) can lead (Ch 17).
**Point-first vs. point-last paragraph** — A paragraph that opens with its claim vs. one that builds to it; point-first is usually clearer (Ch 11).
**Power positions** — The beginnings and endings (of papers, sections, paragraphs, sentences); reserve them for critical story points, never limitations (Ch 10, Ch 18).
**Resolution (R)** — The final OCAR element: how understanding has changed; synopsize, synthesize, contribute (Ch 4, Ch 9).
**Schema** — A prior mental structure a reader already holds; new ideas stick when attached to one ("tastes like chicken") (Ch 3).
**SCFL** — Story, Clear, Fluent, Language: the priority order for editing — fix the story before the words (Ch 17, Ch 19).
**Simple vs. simplistic** — Simple captures the essence of the problem; simplistic dodges or trivializes it (Ch 3).
**Spiral** — A closed story loop that returns to its start, but the start has moved because things changed (Ch 4).
**Sticky** — Memorable and often-cited; "papers with legs"; engineered via SUCCES (Ch 3).
**Stress position** — The end of a clause/sentence; carries the strongest emphasis — put new ideas and key terms there (Ch 12, Ch 13).
**Story modules** — Smaller self-contained stories threaded into a larger one (Ch 3, Ch 10).
**Subject-Verb-Object** — Map onto Opening-Action-Resolution; the OCAR of a single sentence (Ch 12).
**SUCCES** — Simple, Unexpected, Concrete, Credible, Emotional, Stories; the six stickiness factors (from Heath & Heath) (Ch 3, Ch 21).
**Topic position** — The opening of a sentence; what the reader takes the sentence to be "about" — put old/familiar info here (Ch 12, Ch 13).
**Topic sentence (TS-D)** — Topic sentence followed by development; the point-first paragraph form (Ch 11).
**2-3-1 rule of emphasis** — Roy Peter Clark's ordering: strongest material last (1 = stress), second-strongest first, weakest in the middle (Ch 12, Ch 15).
**Unexpected** — The U in SUCCES: frame a knowledge gap rather than dump data (Ch 3).
**"Who knew?" story** — A curiosity-driven public piece showing what science is and how it works (Ch 20).
**Writing is rewriting** — The book's premise: invest effort in revision, not in a perfect first draft (Ch 1).
