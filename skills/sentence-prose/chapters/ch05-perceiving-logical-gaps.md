# Chapter 5: Perceiving Logical Gaps

## Core Idea
When **old information is missing entirely** from a sentence, readers must build the logical link themselves — usually because the connection was so obvious in the writer's mind it went unstated. Attending to old/new placement *reveals* exactly where the argument has a hole.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Gap detection via old/new analysis**: Use the topic/stress discipline as a diagnostic, not just a fix.
  - When to use: After re-ordering old and new information, when a sentence's stress-position promise isn't paid off by the next sentence.
  - How: Check that each sentence's stressed (new) material reappears as old material — i.e., gets picked up — in a following sentence. A dropped thread marks a missing logical connection that must be *written in*, not assumed.
  - Why it works: Structure makes the gap visible; the reader's frustrated expectation ("I was promised X, where did it go?") pinpoints the unstated reasoning.

## Key Concepts
- **Logical gap**: An unarticulated connection between ideas; clear to the writer, invisible to the reader.
- **Stress→topic hand-off**: New information stressed in one sentence should become old information picked up in the next; a broken hand-off signals a gap.
- **Flag word "both"**: Notifies the reader that two pieces of new information will share a single stress position — a small structural courtesy that prevents one from being lost.

## Mental Models
- Treat an unfulfilled stress position as an IOU: the reader expects payment in the next sentence. If none comes, you owe a connecting idea.
- "Writers who know their material won't have to conjecture" — the gap you can't fill by inference is a gap in the *science*, not just the prose.

## Anti-patterns
- **Assuming the connection is obvious**: The clearer it is in your head, the more likely you skipped stating it.
- **Cramming multiple emphases into one unannounced stress position**: The reader stresses one and loses the rest.

## Worked Example
The hydrogen-bond passage. After a structural pass (invert sentence 1 so subject-verb connect and dG/dC arrive in the stress position; keep dG/dC in the topic position of sentence 2; give "triisopropylsilyl groups" its own stress position; flag the two effects with *both*), the prose reads better — but a gap surfaces:

> ...dG and dC were derivatized...with triisopropylsilyl groups; these groups serve **both** to solubilize the nucleosides in non-aqueous solvents and to prevent the ribose hydroxyls from forming hydrogen bonds. **From isoperibolic titration measurements, the enthalpy of dC:dG base pair formation is -6.65±0.32 kcal/mol.**

The second sentence stresses *two effects*; the third never mentions them — the very point the authors most wanted to make is missing. Filling it requires *adding* two sentences of chemistry:

> ...**Consequently, when the derivatized nucleosides are dissolved in non-aqueous solvents, hydrogen bonds form almost exclusively between the bases. Since the interbase hydrogen bonds are the only bonds to form upon mixing, their enthalpy of formation can be determined directly by measuring the enthalpy of mixing.** From our isoperibolic titration measurements, the enthalpy of dC:dG base pair formation is -6.65±0.32 kcal/mol.

Now each sentence proceeds logically from its predecessor and "measurements" reaches back as old information to "measured directly."

## Key Takeaways
1. Missing old information forces readers to invent the logic — and they may invent it wrong.
2. New information stressed in one sentence should resurface as old information in the next; a dropped thread marks a gap.
3. Structural revision is a *diagnostic*: it surfaces conceptual holes, not just stylistic ones.
4. Filling real gaps often means adding material, not cutting it.
5. Use flag words like "both" to protect multiple emphases sharing one stress position.
