# Chapter 8: Action

## Core Idea
The action is the main body of OCAR — everything between challenge and resolution (Methods, Results, most of the Discussion). The point is not to dump techniques and data but to embed them in a story, so structure each action section as a mini-story with its own OCAR or LD elements that develops the larger argument.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Lead/Development (LD) structure**: open with a short overview/frame, then supply the details for those who need them.
  - When to use: Methods (serve both skimmers and replicators) and most Results presentations.
  - How: (1) State the goal or major pattern in one lead sentence. (2) Follow with full procedural or numerical detail. Don't bury details unframed, and don't dump details then synopsize.
  - Why it works: All readers get what they need at their chosen depth; without a framework, readers drown in detail. Failure mode = Example 8.2 (detail with no opening) and the unsalvageable Example 8.1 (a bare method citation that doesn't stand alone).
- **Data / Inference / Interpretation (three types of material)**: every paper must address all three somewhere.
  - When to use: deciding how to split or combine Results and Discussion.
  - How: **Data** = your actual results; **Inference** = robust readings nearly any practitioner would draw (often treated as data); **Interpretation** = your hypotheses and speculation about the larger problem. Distinguish them with signal words (*demonstrates*, *indicating*) and by presenting data before the claim.
- **OCAR vs LDR for the Discussion**: choose the Discussion's internal story structure.
  - When to use: opening the Discussion section.
  - How: **OCAR** = reopen with the challenge/question, work to resolution (Ex 8.8, 8.9). **LDR** = open by framing the conclusion you showed, then marshal support toward resolution (Ex 8.10, 8.11). LDR is more common, but it's a rule, not a law.
- **Story over statistics**: report the magnitude (the data), with statistics as supporting credibility, not as the story itself.
  - When to use: writing up any quantitative result.
  - How: lead with effect size ("increased by a factor of 3.4"), append the p-value. Never let an interpretation (significant/not) masquerade as the data.

## Key Concepts
- **Action**: OCAR's main body — Methods, Results, and most of the Discussion (or, in a proposal, the plan).
- **Schema**: a named, well-known method (Lowry assay, hot-start PCR) you tap into so a description reads as simpler and more concrete.
- **Inference vs Interpretation**: robust shared readings vs the author's speculation about the larger problem.
- **Murder your darlings**: cut data/passages you love that don't serve the story; archive the cut data electronically.
- **Type II error**: rejecting a real effect by treating "not significant" (e.g., p = 0.07) as "no difference."
- **Difference vs significance**: effect size and statistical significance are independent; report both.
- **Robust but weak relationship**: significant (p low) yet low explanatory power (R² low) — both descriptions are true and both belong.
- **Combined vs separate Results/Discussion**: a field- and data-dependent choice; theoretical/observational fields integrate, straight-data fields separate.

## Mental Models
- Use **LD** when readers need a frame before detail — which is almost always for Methods and Results.
- Use a **combined Results & Discussion** when data and inference are strongly fused, when experiments are sequential/chronological, or in theoretical/modeling fields; use **separate** when the core report is straight data.
- Use the **fiction test** to catch stat-driven writing: "John's affection for Jane was significantly greater than zero" is silly — the story is in the *amount*.
- Use **data-before-claim** ("listen to your characters") rather than claim-before-data ("imposing plot"), even within one sentence.

## Anti-patterns
- **Bare method citation (Ex 8.1)**: "measured by the method of X et al." — the paper can't stand alone; reader must hunt the original.
- **Detail with no lead (Ex 8.2)**: plunging into procedure with no opening to orient the reader.
- **Telling the story through the statistics**: "warming significantly increased emissions (p < 0.05)" steals clarity and meaning — is it 1.2× or 12×?
- **"No significant effect" for p = 0.07**: implies no difference when treatment was 2.3× control; also smuggles interpretation in as data (the threshold is arbitrary).
- **Presenting all data because cutting hurts**: distraction; know the story and trim.

## Worked Example
Schimel dissects three versions of one Methods passage (Ex 8.1–8.3) on enzyme inactivation following 3-HPAA metabolism. Ex 8.1 ("measured by the method of Turman et al. 2008") is "truly miserable" — no information, can't be evaluated. Ex 8.2 gives full detail (incubate PGHS-1/2 with 25 μM 3-HPAA, add arachidonic acid, normalize to DMSO control...) but opens cold with no frame. Ex 8.3 — the version actually published — prepends one lead clause, "To characterize the extent of enzyme inactivation associated with 3-HPAA metabolism," then the identical detail. A handful of extra words turns raw procedure into LD structure that serves every reader.

He pairs this with the Weintraub tundra-N example: months of HPLC amino-acid data collapsed to two words — "they covaried" — and reduced to subordinate panels in one figure (8.1), because the story was seasonal bioavailable N, not amino acids. Cut data still got archived.

## Key Takeaways
1. Frame before you detail: give every Methods and Results passage a one-sentence lead (LD), then the specifics.
2. Name the schema (Lowry, PCR) and report only deviations from the standard protocol.
3. Decide *which* data to present before *how* — cut darlings that don't build the story, but archive them.
4. Always distinguish results from interpretation; you needn't separate them physically — put data first and use words like *demonstrates*/*indicating*.
5. Lead with effect size; make statistics supporting information. Report difference AND significance, never one as a proxy for the other.
6. Don't write "no significant effect" when there's a real difference at p = 0.07 — state the magnitude and the p-value.
7. Pick OCAR or LDR consciously for the Discussion so it stands as a coherent story building to the resolution.
