## What the research found, by question

### CB1-LIMING

**D1. For agricultural liming with carbonate materials, what does the literature establish about (a) typical application rates per hectare and their frequency, (b) global and regional consumption and the abundance of the mineral resource, (c) the energy and transport cost per tonne applied, and (d) the carbon dioxide emitted by the practice, including how national greenhouse gas inventories account for it?**

*Answer, as the research gave it; not checked by a second reader.* **Verdict on the scarcity claim**

**Unsupported.** No source I reviewed describes carbonate lime as a globally scarce or limited material, and the one primary source that speaks directly to the question says the opposite. USGS states that "stone resources are plentiful throughout the world" and classifies crushed-stone reserves as "Adequate, except where special types are needed or where local shortages exist" (Willett 2025). That is a direct negative, from the agency whose job it is to make that assessment. The evidence runs against scarcity from three further directions, none of which was assembled to support a conclusion: - Where liming falls short of agronomic need, the documented causes are demand, price and logistics. UK application fell from 6,000–7,000 kt/yr to about 2,500 kt/yr against an annual loss of 4,250 kt CaCO₃, and Goulding attributes this to farm economics, not availability (Goulding 2016). - In the region most often described as lime-constrained, the constraint is not the rock. Ethiopian ag-lime plants ran at 1–29% of installed capacity for want of demand; "huge core-limestone deposits in all production areas" appears as an opportunity in the authors' own SWOT analysis; and they state that producing large quantities "should not be an issue in Ethiopia" (Oumer et al. 2023). - The delivered cost is dominated by moving the material, not by obtaining it: roughly 5.5 ETB/Qt at the quarry against 60–163 ETB/Qt to transport it to a rural distribution point (Oumer et al. 2023). A scarce mineral does not have that cost structure. **The claim is regionally qualified in one narrow and one broad sense, and both need care.** The narrow one is real but off-target: USGS does say "the supply of high-purity limestone and dolomite suitable for specialty uses is limited in many geographic areas" (Willett 2025). That limitation is scoped to high-purity specialty grades. Agricultural lime is a coarse bulk product for which purity requirements are low, and no source I read extends the specialty-grade limitation to it. Treating that sentence as evidence for ag-lime scarcity would be a misreading. The broad one is *access*, not *resource*. Lime is genuinely hard to obtain in parts of sub-Saharan Africa: Silva et al. state that "lime is not widely available in the region," with crop yield loss to soil acidity on 32.7 Mha, 23% of sub-Saharan Africa's cropland, and a burden of US$6.0 billion (Silva et al. 2025). But the Ethiopian case study of that same problem locates the failure in the value chain, not in the deposits (Oumer et al. 2023). A regional access constraint on a locally abundant mineral is a distribution problem wearing the costume of a scarcity problem, and the two should not be conflated. **Indirect support, labelled as such.** Two observations could be read as *consistent with* scarcity but do not evidence it. USGS notes shortages in urban and industrialised areas driven by zoning and land-development competition, pushing quarries further from demand (Willett 2025) — that is planning and land-use pressure, and USGS presents it as a price driver, not a reserve limitation. And Beerling et al.'s reliance on existing crushed-limestone infrastructure to argue enhanced weathering is deployable (Beerling et al. 2020) implies a mature, large-scale extractive industry, which cuts against scarcity rather than for it. If the wording in circulation is that liming is a crude fix using a globally limited material, the "globally limited" half fails. What the literature does support is that carbonate lime is bulky, that transport dominates its delivered cost, that its supply chains fail badly in some regions, and that it is itself a carbon dioxide source of contested magnitude.

### The IPCC method for CO₂ from liming

Builds on: Agricultural liming.

- In the IPCC inventory guidelines a tier is a level of methodological complexity: Tier 1 is the basic method, designed to use readily available statistics with the default emission factors provided, Tier 2 is intermediate, and Tier 3 is the most demanding in complexity and data requirements. (Rypdal et al. 2006)
  Background, not from the research.
- IPCC (2006) Volume 4 Chapter 11 §11.3 gives Equation 11.12: CO₂–C emission = (M_limestone × EF_limestone) + (M_dolomite × EF_dolomite), with M in tonnes per year and EF in tonnes of C per tonne of material. (De Klein et al. 2006)
  Holds for: the IPCC (2006) Tier 1 method.
  Evidence: rated "Well established" by the research.
- The Tier 1 method applies an overall emission factor of 0.12 for limestone and 0.13 for dolomite, equivalent to the carbonate carbon contents of the materials (12% for CaCO₃, 13% for CaMg(CO₃)₂). (De Klein et al. 2006)
  Holds for: the IPCC (2006) Tier 1 defaults.
  Evidence: derived; rated "Well established" by the research.
- Multiplied by 44/12, the default factors give 0.44 t CO₂ per tonne of limestone and about 0.477 per tonne of dolomite. (De Klein et al. 2006)
  Holds for: the review's conversion of the IPCC (2006) Tier 1 defaults.
  Evidence: derived.
- Oxides and hydroxides of lime are explicitly excluded from the method, since they contain no inorganic carbon: their CO₂ is produced during manufacture, not after soil application. (De Klein et al. 2006)
  Holds for: the IPCC (2006) method.
- The IPCC states a one-sided uncertainty of −50% for the default factors, which are a ceiling: "The uncertainty is -50% based on approximations suggesting emissions may be less than half of the maximum value, which is the current factor value (West and McBride, 2005)"; uncertainties cannot exceed the emission factors because these represent the absolute maximum emissions associated with liming. (De Klein et al. 2006)
  Holds for: the IPCC (2006) Tier 1 defaults.
  Evidence: rated "Well established" by the research.
- The IPCC Tier 2 text repeats that "emissions are likely to be less than assumed using the Tier 1 approach because the amount of CO₂ emitted after liming will depend on site-specific influences and transport of dissolved inorganic C through rivers and lakes to the ocean." (De Klein et al. 2006)
  Holds for: the IPCC (2006) Tier 2 text.
- Under the IPCC Tier 3 text, increases in soil inorganic C or dissolved inorganic C from liming do not constitute a net removal of CO₂ from the atmosphere; carbonate-C from liming that is not returned to the atmosphere is considered a net reduction in the emissions associated with liming. (De Klein et al. 2006)
  Holds for: the IPCC (2006) Tier 3 accounting rule.
- The 2019 Refinement left the liming defaults unrevised: its section 11.3, CO₂ Emissions from Liming, reads in its entirety "No refinement", so as of the most recent IPCC methodological update the 2006 factors and their −50% uncertainty stand. (Hergoualc'h et al. 2019)
  Holds for: the 2019 Refinement to the 2006 IPCC Guidelines.
  Evidence: rated "Well established" by the research.
