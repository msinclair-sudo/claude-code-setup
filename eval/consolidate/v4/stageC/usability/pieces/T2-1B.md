# Nodes with nothing to say: featureless nodes in graph diffusion

Graph diffusion lets a network smooth the data attached to it. Each node starts with a feature vector, a list of numbers describing it, and at every step it moves part of the way toward the average of its neighbours. Give each paper in a citation network a vector computed from its text, diffuse the vectors along the citation links, and papers that cite one another or share references drift together, ready for a clustering algorithm to find groups.

The trouble starts when many nodes have no vector at all. In one pipeline for mapping research literature, each paper with text gets a 768-number embedding from SPECTER2, a language model built for scientific papers. These vectors are dense: nearly every number is non-zero and carries meaning. The pipeline compresses them to 100 dimensions with principal component analysis (PCA) as a denoising step, diffuses them over the citation graph, then hands the result to UMAP, which projects it into two dimensions, and HDBSCAN, which finds clusters. In one test graph, keeping only nodes with at least two links, 3,392 nodes had no features against 1,405 that did. About 70% of the graph is what the pipeline calls ghosts.

What follows draws on a single literature review of this problem, which no second reader has checked. By its own verdict, each of its questions is at best partially answered and at worst open.

## Does filling them in keep the links?

The diffusion here is APPNP, a personalised-PageRank scheme. At each step a node's vector becomes 70% the average of its neighbours and 30% its own starting vector, its anchor. The anchor stops everything melting into one average, the known failure of repeated smoothing (Cai and Wang 2020; Oono and Suzuki 2020).

A ghost matters most as a bridge. If papers A and B both link to the same ghost, it is their shared neighbour, and both network theory (second-order proximity in LINE; Tang et al. 2015) and bibliometrics treat shared neighbours as a sign of similarity. APPNP is mathematically a random walk that periodically restarts at its origin, so a path through a shared intermediary does raise the similarity between A and B. In that narrow sense the link is kept by construction.

Whether it survives to the clusters has not been measured, and the ghost's anchor is the reason for doubt. The pipeline must supply one, and both options misbehave. Fill it by diffusion from the neighbours, and Um et al. (2025) prove that diffusion-based filling under heavy missingness yields channels with nearly identical values, which contribute very little. A bridge built from a near-constant vector says little about which papers belong together. Set it to zero, and the ghost feeds zeros into its neighbours' averages at every step, shrinking their vectors and pulling real papers toward the origin. Rossi et al. (2022) describe this effect for zero-filling, and the review rates it well supported. Downstream, UMAP, which favours preserving local neighbourhoods, and HDBSCAN, which can treat a thin connection as noise, may discard whatever weak pull remains.

The alternative is to let ghosts carry traffic without an anchor: keep them in the network so A's signal can pass through to B, but drop them from the 30% pull-back. There is precedent. "Virtual nodes" in message-passing networks route information while contributing no features of their own (Gilmer et al. 2017; Cai et al. 2024). Feature Propagation, a method that resets known features at every step and lets unknown ones be set entirely by their surroundings, treats missing nodes in much this way, and it holds up where zero-filling followed by a neural network does not (Rossi et al. 2022).

Nobody has tested masked ghosts against filled or zero-anchored ones in a pipeline like this; the review looked and found nothing. The masked design is a recommendation with complications of its own. A ghost with two links is a far weaker conduit than one with fifty, and one with few links and no anchor may drift loose and be scattered by UMAP. A bridge that looks preserved also proves little if A and B were already close through other paths.

## How much does the share of ghosts matter?

The most quoted robustness result is reassuring on its face: Feature Propagation lost only 4.12% of its accuracy, in relative terms, with 99% of features missing (Rossi et al. 2022). The conditions behind that number differ from this case on almost every count. Features were hidden one entry at a time, at random, not a whole node at once, and they were sparse word counts. Their graphs were homophilous, meaning linked nodes tended to be alike, and the method slid toward the zero-filling baseline as that tendency weakened. They measured accuracy at labelling nodes, not the quality of unsupervised clusters. Even on their benchmarks, most methods did well up to about 50% missing, and the gap between methods opened from around 60%.

The two mismatches that have been studied both point the wrong way. Yun et al. (2024) found Feature Propagation's performance fell more steeply when whole nodes were missing, since a ghost has no observed value anywhere to start from. Ferrini et al. (2026) found that methods that look robust on sparse word counts fail on dense, low-dimensional features. They also showed that in sparse settings extra missingness barely changes what the features reveal until more than 90% is gone, so robustness tested there says little about dense embeddings, where the safe range may be much narrower. At 70% ghosts, the pipeline is already past the optimistic 60% figure.

The obvious remedy is to require more links before a node stays in the graph, which lowers the ghost share. It also throws away nodes and edges, including some of the bridges the ghosts provide, and changes the shape and homophily of what remains. Global shares hide local ones, too: a node among real neighbours is in a different position from one surrounded by ghosts. No published result gives a safe threshold for this regime, and no one has quantified, for citation graphs, how much structure is worth giving up for reliability. The review judges that if a minimum of three links pushes the ghost share well below 50% while keeping most bridges, that probably buys more than any change to the diffusion itself. It has not been tried.

## What does the denoising step do?

Here the evidence is thinnest. PCA is fitted on real papers only, before diffusion, so ghosts get their anchors in a space shaped entirely by how real papers vary. The parts are known only separately. Compressing features with PCA before a graph network kept accuracy on a standard benchmark (80.98% against 80.82%), but with nothing missing (Morehead et al. 2022). In ordinary PCA, treating gaps as zeros biases the spread of variance downward (Josse and Husson 2016). In non-graph models, Yi et al. (2020) proved that zero-filling makes output scale with the fraction of inputs observed, so any step that rescales magnitudes, such as centring or whitening, interacts with that shrinkage.

How these combine is open. A zero-anchored ghost pulling on centred, compressed vectors could distort more, because each retained dimension carries more weight, or less, because the magnitudes are smaller. The review infers that denoising before diffusion is the safer order, since diffusion is itself a smoother and denoising first avoids smoothing the filled-in values twice. That is reasoning, not a result.

The link through a ghost exists in the mathematics. The conditions under which diffusion is known to cope with missing features do not include a graph that is 70% featureless with dense embeddings. What happens between those two statements has to be measured, stage by stage, on the pipeline itself.
