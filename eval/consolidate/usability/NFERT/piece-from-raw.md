# What fixed nitrogen costs, and how much soil microbes can take off the bill

Nearly all synthetic nitrogen fertiliser starts as ammonia made by the Haber-Bosch process, which combines nitrogen from the air with hydrogen at high temperature and pressure. The nitrogen is free. The hydrogen is not. It is stripped from natural gas or coal by steam reforming, and that upstream step is where most of the energy and almost all of the carbon enter the product.

## Energy

A modern natural-gas plant needs about 28 GJ to make a tonne of ammonia (Tock et al. 2015; Demirhan et al. 2019). The global average is higher, near 37 GJ, because older and coal-fed plants are still running; coal-based production in China uses about 59 GJ per tonne. In the 1950s even the best plants needed around 55 GJ (Tunå et al. 2014).

Agronomists count fertiliser in tonnes of nitrogen, not ammonia. Ammonia is 82% nitrogen by mass, so a tonne of nitrogen takes about 1.21 tonnes of ammonia. On that conversion, a tonne of fertiliser nitrogen costs roughly 34 GJ from the best gas plants and roughly 45 GJ at the global average. These per-nitrogen numbers are arithmetic on the per-ammonia figures, not separate measurements.

## Carbon dioxide

Emissions follow the feedstock. A modern gas plant emits close to 1.6 t CO₂ per tonne of ammonia (Tock et al. 2015). The ecoinvent life-cycle inventory, a standard reference database, gives 1.92 t CO₂-equivalent per tonne, alongside 41 GJ of primary energy (Razon 2014). A global mix that includes coal comes to about 2.8 t CO₂ per tonne (Demirhan et al. 2019). Fitting carbon capture to a gas plant brings the figure at the factory gate down to about 0.79 t (Tock et al. 2015).

Per tonne of nitrogen, then, manufacture emits about 1.9 to 3.4 t CO₂-equivalent, from efficient gas plants at the low end to coal-heavy supply at the high end. These are cradle-to-gate numbers. They leave out the nitrous oxide (N₂O) released from fields after the fertiliser is spread, which turns out to matter more than anything inside the factory.

## How big a share of emissions?

Three figures circulate, and each answers a different question. Quoting one as though it were another is the most common error in this literature.

**Manufacture only: about 0.9% to 1.8% of global greenhouse-gas emissions.** The International Fertilizer Association attributed 0.47 Gt CO₂-equivalent to nitrogen fertiliser production in 2007, about 0.93% of the anthropogenic total (reported in Menegat et al. 2022). Process studies put ammonia manufacture at between 1% and 1.8% (Tunå et al. 2014; Tock et al. 2015; Demirhan et al. 2019).

**The synthetic-nitrogen supply chain: about 2.1%.** Menegat et al. (2022) add transport and field emissions and arrive at 1.13 Gt CO₂-equivalent for 2018. That is 10.6% of agriculture's emissions and 2.1% of the global total. Manufacture is 38.8% of it, transport 2.6%, and emissions from the field 58.6%.

**The whole nitrogen-fertiliser life cycle, manure included: about 5%.** Gao and Cabrera Serrenho (2023) find that roughly two-thirds of these emissions occur after the fertiliser is applied and one-third during production. Manure performs worst per unit of nitrogen, at about 23 t CO₂-equivalent per tonne, 1.9 times an average synthetic fertiliser, because so much of its nitrogen is lost. By their accounting an average synthetic fertiliser therefore carries something like 12 t CO₂-equivalent per tonne of nitrogen over its life, several times the factory figure.

The two broad studies agree on the point that matters for what follows. Most of fertiliser's climate cost arises in the soil, after application, and Gao and Cabrera Serrenho name nitrogen-use efficiency, the share of applied nitrogen that ends up in the crop, as the single most effective lever for cutting it.

## Can soil microbes stand in for the bag?

If soil biology supplied some of the crop's nitrogen, less would need to be made. The evidence divides cleanly. Where farmers deliberately add a nitrogen-fixing symbiosis, the saving is real and measured. Where the claim rests on the soil's resident microbes simply cycling nitrogen better, the support is indirect.

### Legumes and rhizobia: a real substitute, with limits

Legumes host rhizobia, bacteria that fix atmospheric nitrogen inside root nodules. They are the largest biological nitrogen input to cropping systems, and their effect on fertiliser demand is documented (Ladha et al. 2022). In one organic, legume-based rotation in Europe, fixation supplied 87% of all nitrogen inputs. The rotation matched conventional productivity with a nitrogen surplus of only 21 kg per hectare per year, against 40 to 100 kg for fertilised systems (Anglade et al. 2015). The fixed nitrogen feeds the legume directly and partly enriches the crop that follows.

Two things bound the saving. The share of a legume's nitrogen that comes from fixation ranges from a few percent to nearly all of it, depending on species, mineral nitrogen already in the soil, and competition (Anglade et al. 2015; Ladha et al. 2022). And fertiliser switches fixation off. In one system reviewed by Ladha et al. (2022), applying 125 to 250 kg of fertiliser nitrogen per hectare cut fixation by 81% to 86%. Fixation and fertiliser substitute for each other rather than adding up, which caps what fixation can save.

### The resident community: real activity, unproven savings

Three lines of evidence bear on the broader idea that an efficient resident microbial community lowers fertiliser need. None isolates the microbes' contribution.

*Free-living fixers.* Bacteria that fix nitrogen without a host plant are everywhere and do contribute, but the measured amounts in cereal systems are modest, rarely enough to displace a defined fertiliser rate at the same yield. Engineering cereals to fix their own nitrogen has not yet been achieved (Ladha et al. 2022).

*Inoculants.* The largest meta-analysis of microbial inoculants, covering 171 field studies, found average yield gains of 8.5% in continental climates, 10.0% in oceanic, 14.9% in tropical and 20.0% in dry climates, with effects often inconsistent and dependent on soil phosphorus and climate (Schütz et al. 2018). These trials ask how much more a crop yields at a given fertiliser rate. They do not ask how much fertiliser can be withheld at the same yield, which is the question here.

*Mineralisation.* Soil microbes release nitrogen from organic matter, but only about 1% to 4% of soil organic nitrogen is mineralised in a season. That is seldom enough for modern high-yielding cultivars without added nitrogen (St. Luce et al. 2011). Measuring how much nitrogen a given soil will supply can trim fertiliser rates. The lever there is knowing the soil's supply, though, not any demonstrated property of the community doing the supplying.

Behind all three sits an attribution problem. Soils that need less fertiliser also tend to differ in organic matter, water and rotation history. None of the studies here separates microbial efficiency from those factors as the cause of a lower requirement at constant yield.

## What this leaves

Legume fixation, a management choice, reduces synthetic input. The resident community supplements fertiliser and has not been shown to replace it.

Substitution may be the wrong place to look for the biggest gain anyway. If most of fertiliser's emissions occur after application, the larger question is how much of the applied nitrogen the crop captures and how much is lost, and whether soil microbial communities govern that fraction. The evidence here establishes the weight of the use phase. It does not test whether microbes control it.
