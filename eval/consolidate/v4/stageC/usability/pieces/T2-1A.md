# Ghost nodes: what graph diffusion does with nodes that have no features

Picture a map of a research literature built from two kinds of evidence at once. Each paper carries a vector of 768 numbers from SPECTER2, a model that embeds scientific documents so that papers on similar topics get similar vectors. The papers are also joined by citations. A graph-diffusion method combines the two by letting each paper's vector spread along its links, so papers that cite each other, or lean on the same sources, drift together. The blended vectors are then projected into a low-dimensional map with UMAP and grouped into clusters with HDBSCAN.

Many nodes in such a graph have no vector at all. They sit in the network because of their links but have nothing of their own to spread. In the literature-mapping pipeline discussed here, about 70% of nodes are like this when the graph keeps every node with at least two links; one test graph had 3,392 featureless nodes against 1,405 with features. Call them ghosts.

## Where ghosts break the diffusion

The method is APPNP, a form of personalised PageRank. In each round, every node replaces its vector with a mix: 70% the average of its neighbours' current vectors, 30% its own original vector. That 30% share works as an anchor. Without it, repeated averaging blurs every node towards the same constant vector, a failure called oversmoothing.

A ghost has nothing to be anchored to, so something must go in that slot. The anchor can be set to zero. It can be filled with an imputed vector, typically a diffusion-style average of the ghost's neighbours, which is what the pipeline currently does. Or the ghost can have no anchor at all, staying in the network to pass signal along while contributing nothing of its own. Call that a conduit.

## Does filling in a ghost preserve the link it carries?

Many ghosts matter because they connect two real papers. If papers A and B both link to the same ghost, they share a third work, which co-citation and bibliographic-coupling analysis treat as evidence that they are related. The diffusion does carry this. Personalised PageRank is a random walk with restarts, and walks from A to B through the shared node add to the similarity of their final vectors. Network-embedding theory (the "second-order proximity" of the LINE method) agrees that nodes with shared neighbours should land close together.

So the bridge is in the arithmetic. Whether it reaches the final clusters is another matter. Um and colleagues (ICML 2025) proved that diffusion-based imputation produces channels whose values are nearly identical across nodes, and that such low-variance channels contribute very little downstream. A ghost whose anchor is a neighbourhood average is exactly that kind of signal, so what it passes between A and B may be close to constant. Oversmoothing then erodes what remains, and the ghost lacks the real anchor that protects other nodes. Finally, UMAP preserves local neighbourhoods and HDBSCAN can treat a thin connection as noise, so a weak pull between A and B can vanish at either step. No study covers an intermediary whose own vector is fabricated, at 70% ghosts, feeding this chain. The bridge is encoded; nobody has shown it survives.

The fill-in choice also affects the real papers around a ghost. A zero anchor feeds zeros into every neighbour's average, shrinking those vectors towards the origin. Yi and colleagues (ICLR 2020) proved the general form: with zero-filling, a model's output scales with the fraction of the input actually observed. So the ghost distorts papers whose own features are perfectly good.

The conduit option has precedent. "Virtual nodes" in graph neural networks route information without features of their own, and masked propagation lets only observed features enter the anchor term. The strongest argument comes from Feature Propagation (Rossi and colleagues, 2022), which resets known features at every step and lets unknown ones be set entirely by their neighbours. Its end state does not depend on the starting guess for the missing values, and that is why it holds up where zero-filling fails. APPNP with a fixed ghost anchor does the opposite, re-injecting the fabricated vector every round. Dropping ghosts from the anchor term while keeping them in the averaging would be a small change. No one has yet tested imputed, zero and conduit ghosts against each other in a pipeline like this.

## How much does the share of ghosts matter?

The field's most reassuring number is Feature Propagation's: a 4.12% relative drop in classification accuracy with 99% of features missing. It comes with conditions. Individual entries were hidden at random, so most nodes kept some information. Features were sparse word-count vectors. The graphs were homophilous, meaning linked nodes tend to be alike, and the method weakens as that tendency fades. Under these conditions most methods did well up to 50% missing, and they began to separate from about 60%.

Two newer results suggest ghosts fare worse. Ferrini and colleagues (2026) argue that sparse features hide damage: when most entries are already empty, removing more barely changes what the features say. Methods that looked robust on word counts failed on dense, low-dimensional features, and SPECTER2 vectors are dense. Yun and colleagues found Feature Propagation degrades faster when whole nodes are missing rather than scattered entries, which is exactly what a ghost is. At 70%, the pipeline already sits past the published crossover, under conditions that should pull the crossover lower. The 50 to 60% figure is an optimistic ceiling.

Raising the link threshold to three, five or ten links shrinks the ghost share. It also discards nodes and edges, including the bridges the ghosts were kept for. Nobody has measured that trade-off for citation graphs.

## The denoising step

Before diffusion, the pipeline compresses the real papers' vectors to 100 dimensions with principal component analysis (PCA), discarding low-variance directions as noise. Ghosts have nothing to compress, so they get their anchors afterwards, in the reduced space.

Whether this worsens ghost distortion is unknown. Each remaining dimension carries more relative weight after PCA, so a ghost's pull could distort more; but the vectors are also smaller in absolute terms, so it could distort less. A zero-anchored ghost might even push energy back into directions PCA removed. Denoising first is plausibly the safer order, since diffusion is itself a smoothing filter and the reverse order would smooth imputed values twice, but that is reasoning rather than a result. The nearest evidence sits outside this regime. PCA before a graph network on complete data left accuracy roughly unchanged (80.98% against 80.82% on one benchmark), and in ordinary statistics, treating missing entries as zero before PCA biases its spectrum downwards. No study has put these pieces together.

## What has to be measured

None of the four questions is settled, so the next steps are measurements. First, check the existing pipeline: compare the variance of ghost vectors with real ones, and see whether real pairs bridged by a ghost cluster together more often than pairs joined through randomly rewired ghosts. If ghost vectors prove near-constant, test the conduit variant, adopting it only if it moves real papers less without weakening the bridges. Then sweep the link threshold and plot reliability against the structure lost. Denoising experiments come last because prior work supports them least. Several key results are recent and little replicated, so even the parts that look established deserve some caution.
