## What the research found, by question

### CB3-AG-EMISSIONS

**D1. What share of global greenhouse gas emissions is attributed to agriculture, and how does that share change with the accounting boundary used: agricultural production alone, agriculture with land use and land-use change, the whole food system, or soil emissions alone? What is the separate share attributable to nitrous oxide from managed soils and from synthetic fertiliser?**

*Answer, as the research gave it; not checked by a second reader.* **9. Verdict: which boundary produces a figure near five per cent?**

**No boundary in the current authoritative literature produces exactly five per cent for agriculture, and none produces it for agricultural soils.** Three boundaries land close enough that a "roughly 5%" claim could have originated from any of them, and they are not interchangeable: 1. **Nitrous oxide from agricultural soils (inventory category 3.D-equivalent): 4.1%**, Climate Watch/WRI for 2016, as presented by Our World in Data (Climate and World n.d.). This is the closest match to a soils-specific claim, and the only widely circulated breakdown in which a soils category sits near 5%. It is also the weakest-supported number in this review: one secondary presentation, no uncertainty statement, not verified against the primary dataset. Independent reconstructions of the same quantity come in lower — ~3.5% from the Global N2O Budget, ~2.4% for "managed soils and pasture" from EDGAR via AR6 (Nabuurs et al. 2022), ~1.5% for cropland soils from SRCCL (IPCC 2019). 2. **All anthropogenic nitrous oxide, every sector: ~4.7%** of 2010–2019 global GHG. If a "5%" claim is really about N2O rather than about soils, this is what it matches — but it includes fossil fuel, industry, waste and biomass burning N2O, not just agriculture. 3. **Emissions outside the farm gate: 5–10%**, SRCCL (IPCC 2019); and **non-food agriculture plus other deforestation drivers: 5%**, Poore & Nemecek (Poore and Nemecek 2018). Both are exactly 5% at one end, and both are about something else entirely — supply chains and non-food crops. What five per cent is definitively **not**: it is not agricultural production (10.7–14.6%), not AFOLU (13–22%), not agriculture with land-use change for food (16–27%), and not the food system (21–42%). A figure near 5% is three to eight times smaller than any of those, and can only belong to a soils- or N2O-specific boundary. If a claim near 5% must be anchored, the honest statement of the evidence is: published estimates of N2O from agricultural soils as a share of global GHG emissions range from about **1.5% to 4.1%** depending on whether the category is cropland soils only, managed soils and pasture, or the full inventory "agricultural soils" line, and on the database and metric used. The 4.1% end rests on a single unverified source.

### accounting boundaries for agriculture's emissions

- The question of what share of global emissions is agriculture has no single answer, because four different accounting boundaries are in routine use, each defensible, each producing a number between roughly 2% and 42%. (the reviewer's synthesis)
  Holds for: the four accounting boundaries in routine use.

### emission metrics and gas coverage

- EDGAR-FOOD and FAOSTAT aggregate emissions with AR5 GWP100 (CH4 = 28, N2O = 265), while AR6 uses AR6 GWP100 with climate–carbon feedbacks (CH4 = 27, N2O = 273). (Nabuurs et al. 2022; Crippa et al. 2021; Tubiello et al. 2022)
  Holds for: the metric, one of the reasons the major sources differ.
- AR6 warns that "The choice of metric depends on the purpose of the analysis and all GHG emission metrics have limitations and uncertainties". (IPCC 2022)
  Holds for: GHG emission metrics in general.
- On gas coverage, SRCCL's 23% covers only CO2, CH4 and N2O, while EDGAR-FOOD and FAOSTAT include F-gases. (IPCC 2019; Crippa et al. 2021; Tubiello et al. 2022)
  Holds for: gas coverage, one of the reasons the major sources differ.
- Lynch et al. argue that GWP100 CO2-equivalent reporting misrepresents agriculture's warming contribution, because agriculture is dominated by CH4 and N2O rather than CO2: "conventional reporting of aggregated CO2-equivalent emission rates is highly ambiguous and does not straightforwardly reflect historical or anticipated contributions to global temperature change". (Lynch et al. 2021)
  Holds for: GWP100 CO2-equivalent reporting of agriculture's emissions.
- Lynch et al. do not offer a replacement share: their criticism attacks the aggregation, not the inventory. (Lynch et al. 2021)
  Holds for: the metric criticism by Lynch et al.
- The direction of the effect Lynch et al. describe is not uniformly downward: they also state that "the impact of increasing methane emissions at rates above ~1% per year will be understated by reporting using GWP100 CO2e". (Lynch et al. 2021)
  Holds for: methane emissions increasing at rates above ~1% per year, under GWP100 CO2e reporting.
