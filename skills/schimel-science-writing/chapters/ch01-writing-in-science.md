# Chapter 1: Writing in Science

## Core Idea
As a scientist, you are a professional writer: success comes not from publishing but from being *cited*, and citation depends on whether readers can follow your ideas — so writing is a core scientific tool to be studied and sharpened, not a post-hoc chore. This is a short, motivational framing chapter; it sets premises the rest of the book builds on rather than introducing analytic machinery.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **"It is the author's job to make the reader's job easy."** (the overriding principle of the whole book)
  - When to use: every decision about structure, paragraphs, sentences, and word choice — treat it as the tiebreaker.
  - How: assume the reader is busy, tired, and willing to quit; remove every obstacle that forces them to "fight" for meaning. If a choice makes your job easier but the reader's harder, reverse it.
  - Why it works / failure mode: readers' expectations of science writing are *low* and they want the information, so they will fight through clutter — but some will lose that fight, and "then you, the author, will be the greater loser." Your impact dies in the unread paragraph.
- **Writing is rewriting** (this book is "really a book about rewriting, not writing")
  - When to use: whenever drafting paralyzes you, or you try to make a first draft perfect.
  - How: get *something* down on screen as fast as possible just to have material to revise (Williams). Then iterate: rewrite earlier draft into something clearer → understand the ideas better → express them more clearly → understand them even better, looping until you run out of energy, interest, or time.
  - Why it works / failure mode: thinking about the book's principles *while drafting* will stop you drafting at all. First drafts are private and don't matter ("Shitty First Drafts" — Lamott); polish is where funded proposals and cited papers come from.

## Key Concepts
- **Publish or perish vs. cite or succeed**: publishing is about surviving; succeeding is about being cited and used by peers.
- **Impact Factor / H-factor**: citation-based metrics that reward influence; 100 uncited papers rate a flat zero on the H-factor.
- **Writing as the rate-limiting step**: because writing is the bridge to your audience, it can constrain the effectiveness of *all* your other (data-generating, analytic) tools.
- **Writer as identity, not activity**: being a writer is something a scientist *is*, not merely something a scientist *does*.
- **Clear writing → clear thinking**: the act of organizing and expressing ideas clarifies them, reversing the common maxim ("to write clearly you must first think clearly"); writing is how scientists discover the real form of their work (Montgomery).
- **Strategy over tactics**: "Good tactics never overcome bad strategy" — story structure (the big issues) precedes paragraphs, sentences, and words.

## Mental Models
- Think of writing as a **tool in your toolbox**, as complex and learnable as molecular biology or statistics — and the most important of the tools that communicate results.
- Use the **rate-limiting-step** model when prioritizing skill investment: the weakest link to your audience caps everything upstream of it.
- Treat yourself as a **professional/craftsman**: professionals study their craft, analyze peers' work, and experiment with new tools — "If you want your writing to be effective, become a writer."
- Reframe causality: don't wait to think clearly before writing — **write to think clearly**.

## Anti-patterns
- **"Writing up" the paper post hoc**: treating writing as an afterthought after the data is in means you never sharpen the tool and may not know you have a writing toolbox at all.
- **The maxim "to write clearly, you must first think clearly"**: Schimel calls this "monstrously prevalent" and "really rubbish"; writing is rebellious and full of unforeseen revelations, so clarity *emerges from* the struggle.
- **Chasing the perfect first draft**: paralyzing; no one sees your first drafts, so let them be bad.
- **Confusing publishing with writing**: most science-writing books teach manuscript logistics (figures vs. tables, dealing with editors) — that is publishing, not the craft of writing.

## Worked Example
Schimel's central worked argument is the H-factor illustration he uses to redefine "success." He contrasts three researchers: one with 10 papers each cited 10 times (H = 10); one with 30 papers each cited 30 times (H = 30); and one with 100 published papers, none cited — who "would rate a flat zero." The point lands the chapter's thesis concretely: raw page count is invisible to the metric that matters; only *used* work counts. He then chains the causal logic — papers get read and cited "because we made our points well enough that readers could follow," and proposals get funded "because we were able to make our ideas clear, compelling, and convincing to reviewers" — so success "comes from our ability to communicate our ideas as much as from their inherent quality." This is the evidence that writing is not cosmetic but the rate-limiting step on a career.

## Key Takeaways
1. Optimize for citation, not publication — measure your writing by whether peers can use it.
2. Make the reader's job easy; let that override every other writing decision.
3. Study writing as deliberately as any lab technique — it is a tool, not a chore.
4. Draft fast and ugly; invest your real effort in rewriting ("Polish, polish, polish!").
5. Don't apply this book's principles while drafting — apply them while *re*writing.
6. Write to clarify your thinking, not only to report finished thoughts.
7. Lead with strategy (story structure) before fixing tactics (sentences and words).
