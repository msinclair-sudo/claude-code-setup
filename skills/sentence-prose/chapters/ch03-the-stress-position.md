# Chapter 3: The Stress Position

## Core Idea
Readers naturally emphasize whatever arrives at the **moment of syntactic closure** — typically the end of a sentence or clause. Put the information you want stressed there, and reader and writer will agree on what matters.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The stress position**: The location of natural reader emphasis, coinciding with syntactic closure ("Save the best for last").
  - When to use: Deciding where the new, emphasis-worthy idea of a sentence should land.
  - How: Identify the single most important new idea; arrange the syntax so that idea occupies the slot just before a full stop, colon, or semicolon.
  - Why it works: Readers take a "mental breath" at a sentence's start and exhale as it closes; the exhalation produces emphasis. We also expect a reward for the effort of reading — "we do not start with strawberry shortcake and work our way up to the broccoli."
- **Secondary stress positions**: A properly used colon or semicolon creates an additional point of closure. The material before each must stand alone as a complete sentence — so one sentence can carry several stressed ideas.

## Key Concepts
- **Syntactic closure**: The point where the reader knows nothing remains in the clause but what is now being read; the stress position begins there.
- **Variable size**: A stress position can be a single word or several lines (even a numbered, indented list, if clearly announced as all that remains).
- **"Too long" redefined**: A sentence is too long when it has **more candidates for stress than there are stress positions available** — not when it crosses some word count (the mythical 29).

## Mental Models
- Think of the sentence end as a spotlight: whatever stands there gets the audience's attention whether or not it deserves it.
- Use colons/semicolons as you would extra spotlights — each demands a grammatically complete lead-in.

## Anti-patterns
- **Unworthy material in the stress position**: Forces the reader to hunt elsewhere for the point, with no structural clue to guide them.
- **Worse — an "imposter" in the stress position**: Material incapable of bearing emphasis lands there, the reader stresses it anyway, and the writer loses the chance to direct interpretation.
- **Trusting word-count readability formulas**: They mistake length for difficulty.

## Worked Example
The semicolon repair from the URF passage creates a second stress position to carry a second emphasis-worthy idea:

> The smallest of the URF's is **URFA6L**, a 207-nucleotide reading frame overlapping out of phase the NH2-terminal portion of the ATPase subunit 6 gene; it has been identified as the animal equivalent of the recently discovered **yeast H+-ATPase subunit 8 gene**.

The first clause closes on *URFA6L* (one emphasis); the semicolon opens a second closure ending on *subunit 8 gene* (the second). Two stress-worthy facts, two stress positions — no candidate is left fighting for an unavailable slot.

## Key Takeaways
1. Place the new information you want emphasized in the stress position (point of closure).
2. Closure is created by a period — and, mid-sentence, by a colon or semicolon whose lead-in stands alone.
3. A sentence is too long when stress candidates outnumber stress positions, not at any fixed word count.
4. Never let unworthy or unstressable material occupy the stress position.
5. Match the number of stress positions to the number of ideas that need stressing.
