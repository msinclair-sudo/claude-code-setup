# Chapter 6: Locating the Action

## Core Idea
Readers expect the **action of a sentence to live in its verb**. When the real actions are hidden (buried in nouns, or never stated) and the verbs are limp ("is", "are presumed to be"), readers can't tell what is happening and each invents their own interpretation.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Articulate the action in the verb**: The final core reader expectation.
  - When to use: Sentences whose verbs are weak forms of *to be* or vague auxiliaries, while the genuine action sits in a noun or is missing.
  - How: List the verbs of a passage. If the list doesn't tell the story, find the true actions, convert them into strong verbs, and pair them with a consistent topic-position actor.
  - Why it works: The verb is the reader's primary structural clue to "what is happening." Strip it of action and the reader has no secondary clue — interpretation goes uncontrolled.

## Key Concepts
- **The verb test**: Read only the verbs of a paragraph in sequence. If they fail to convey the action, the actions are misplaced.
- **Missing actions**: Sometimes the crucial verbs (e.g., "limit", "inhibit") never appear at all — the players are named but their relationships aren't.
- **Actor in the topic position**: Pair recovered verbs with the most frequently recurring old information placed up front, so the passage finally has a consistent protagonist.

## Mental Models
- Think of verbs as the load-bearing beams of a sentence. Nominalize the action into a noun and the beam becomes decoration while the roof sags.
- "Whose story + what they do": topic position answers *who*, the verb answers *does what*. Both must be present.

## Anti-patterns
- **Nominalized / hidden actions**: The real verb is trapped inside a noun; the sentence's stated verb is an empty *is/has*.
- **Too many competing protagonists**: Topic positions shift every sentence (egg extract, TFIIIA, oocyte extract, RNA polymerase, 5S RNA, transcription) so none dominates and no story emerges.

## Worked Example
The 5S-RNA passage's verbs, listed in order, carry almost no action:
> is / is...is / are presumed to be / are transcribed / has / is...can be alleviated / destabilizes

The genuine actions — *limit* and *inhibit* — never appear as the main verbs. Recovering them and fixing "egg extract" and "TFIIIA" as the recurring topic-position actors yields:

> In the egg extract, the availability of TFIIIA **limits** transcription of the 5S RNA genes. This is surprising because the same concentration of TFIIIA does not **limit** transcription in the oocyte nuclear extract. ... When added to the nuclear extract, the egg extract **affected** the efficiency of transcription in two ways. First, it **inhibited** transcription generally...Second, the egg extract **destabilized** transcription complexes...

With the actions in the verbs and a consistent actor up front, the passage finally tells a story — and reveals that the author never explained the connection between "limit" and "inhibit" (the buried hypotheses).

## Key Takeaways
1. Put the action of every clause or sentence in its verb.
2. Diagnose with the verb test: read the verbs alone — do they tell the story?
3. Weak *to be* verbs plus action-bearing nouns = misplaced action; convert the noun back to a verb.
4. Pair recovered verbs with a consistent topic-position actor so the passage has one protagonist.
5. Recovering hidden actions, like fixing old/new placement, exposes unstated reasoning.
