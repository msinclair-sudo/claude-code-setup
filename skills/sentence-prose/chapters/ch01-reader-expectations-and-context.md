# Chapter 1: Writing with the Reader in Mind — Expectation and Context

## Core Idea
Readers don't just read, they *interpret* — and they make their most important interpretive decisions from **structure**, not just content. Clarity comes from placing each piece of information where readers already expect to find it.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Reader-expectation methodology**: Base every structural choice on how readers actually process prose, not on what feels natural to write.
  - When to use: Any time prose is "correct" but still hard to follow.
  - How: Ask "where will a reader *expect* this information?" and put it there; reserve fixed structural slots for fixed functions.
  - Why it works: Drawn from rhetoric, linguistics, and cognitive psychology — readers expend energy unraveling structure *before* they can absorb content. Honor the expectation and that energy goes to meaning instead.
- **Substance ↔ structure interplay**: The same facts in different structures produce different meanings. Structure is not decoration; it directs interpretation.
- **Units of discourse**: Anything with a beginning and an end — clause, sentence, paragraph, section, article. Every unit carries reader expectations about *where* particular content belongs.

## Key Concepts
- **Reader expectation**: The relatively fixed assumption readers hold about where in a structure a given kind of information will appear.
- **Context-before-content**: Readers want orientation before they are asked to process something new.
- **"Shape"**: What prose has when a writer intuitively satisfies reader expectations.
- **Communication vs. presentation**: Success is not converting data into sentences; it is a large majority of readers perceiving what the author meant.

## Mental Models
- Think of every sentence as having reserved seats: certain positions are *expected* to hold certain kinds of information. Seat the wrong guest and the reader gets confused.
- Use "improving the writing improves the thinking" as a working belief — structural revision repeatedly exposes gaps in the underlying science (see [ch05](ch05-perceiving-logical-gaps.md)).

## Anti-patterns
- **Blaming the reader**: When a passage is hard, writers assume complexity or the reader's inattention. Usually the fault is structural.
- **Writing to unburden yourself**: Recording information in the order *you* thought of it rather than the order the reader needs it.

## Worked Example
The same temperature/time data, two structures. Most readers find the second far easier:

```
t=15', T=32q; t=0', T=25q; t=6', T=29q; t=3', T=27q; t=12', T=32q; t=9', T=31q
```
versus
```
time (min)    temperature (°C)
   0               25
   3               27
   6               29
   9               31
  12               32
  15               32
```
The table works because **context (time) sits on the left** in a predictable, regular pattern, and the **interesting result (temperature) sits on the right**, where the reader looks for the payoff. Reverse the columns (temperature left, time right) and it becomes hard to read again — the same data, wrong placement. Principle: *context left, new/important information right* — the same left-to-right logic that governs sentences (topic → stress).

## Key Takeaways
1. Readers interpret from structural clues; control the clues and you control the interpretation.
2. Identical information in a different structure means something different — placement is meaning.
3. Put context where readers expect context (early/left) and the point where they expect the point (late/right).
4. When prose fails, suspect the structure before the vocabulary or the reader.
5. Good writers satisfy expectations intuitively; the rest of us can do it consciously.
