# Chapter 21: Resolution

## Core Idea
This is the book's own resolution: the closing of the circle Schimel opened in Chapter 1 with "as a scientist, you are a professional writer." Every tool in the book grows from two roots — stickiness (SUCCES) and story structure (OCAR) — and the career payoff is not publishing but being cited, because you succeed by writing papers worth reading, not by writing many.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **OCAR / SUCCES as the two unifying principles**: All chapter-level guidelines were just applications of these two cores — story structure (OCAR: Opening, Challenge, Action, Resolution) and stickiness (SUCCES).
  - When to use: As the lens for everything from whole papers and proposals down to single sentences.
  - How: Target your audience; focus on the critical story elements; remember science is about knowledge and understanding, not just data. Apply the writer's tools to identify the valuable core of a flawed dataset, facet it, and polish it.
  - Why it works / failure mode: Writing skill cannot "turn a lump of coal into a diamond" — it cannot rescue fundamentally weak research. It can only reveal and present the valuable core that is already there.
- **Success strategies over survival strategies**: Aim at success; survival takes care of itself ("clear the bar of success, you will clear the bar of survival without noticing it was there").
  - When to use: Throughout a scientific career, against the "publish or perish" mindset that prizes quantity.
  - How: Write for your *real peers* — the people in your field who actually read your papers — not for administrators counting your CV. Real peers see through "flash, splash, and numbers" and cite work that contributes.
  - Why it works: In the U.S. system, the decisive part of a promotion package is letters from peers, who care whether your work was creative and moved the field — not the raw paper count.
- **Quality begets quantity**: Peers send strong scientists their students and postdocs (who do the work and write the papers), so doing quality work indirectly generates quantity too.

## Key Concepts
- **Commencement / graduation duality**: Every ending is also a beginning; finishing the book is both an accomplishment and a new starting point — learning to write is "a road without end."
- **Real peers**: The scientists worldwide who actually read your work; the audience whose citations define academic success.
- **Citation as success metric**: You succeed not by getting papers published but by getting them cited (echoes Ch 1).
- **Writing-up vs. writing**: Writing skills won't make you a writer until you take the tools and develop expertise through practice.
- **Toolbox**: The chapters and exercises expand your tool box, but tools alone aren't mastery.
- **Communication generally**: The lessons are about communication, not just writing — "Homer didn't write the Odyssey, he recited it," yet understood OCAR and SUCCES.

## Mental Models
- Use **"impress your real peers"** when tempted to game metrics — your dean reads citation history, invitations, and editorial-board service, all of which follow from peer respect.
- Use the **coal-vs-diamond test** when deciding what writing can fix: clever writing facets and polishes a real gem; it cannot manufacture one.
- Use **"remember who your real peers are"** when frustrated by politics or metrics — write for the readers in your field.

## Anti-patterns
- **"Publish or perish" as a success philosophy**: It is at best a survival rule; interpreting it as "quantity over quality" produces a forgettable CV in a crowded universe.
- **Writing to impress administrators / counting publications**: Administrators rarely know the field; chasing paper count instead of contribution misreads how promotion actually works.
- **Expecting tools to substitute for science**: Writing cannot strengthen fundamentally weak research.

## Worked Example
The appended revision answers (Appendix A) demonstrate the book's tools in miniature. For exercise 14.3A, the original sentence's only verbs were "increase" and "alter," while the *real* dramatic action — "ecological interactions," a euphemism for a nematode eating a bacterium — was hidden. Schimel's fix energizes the buried action into a verb: *"Increased mobility of predatory nematodes in soil would allow them to **consume** more bacteria and so alter bacterial population dynamics."* For 12.4A, the story's critical word "unclear" was buried mid-sentence, so he moves it to the stress position: *"...the crystalline nature of kryptonite remains **unclear**."* Each shows OCAR/stress-position principles applied at sentence scale.

## Key Takeaways
1. Treat OCAR and SUCCES as the source of every other rule — when a guideline feels arbitrary, trace it back to story structure or stickiness.
2. Use writing to find and polish the valuable core of your data, but never expect it to rescue weak science.
3. Aim for citation, not publication count; write papers worth reading and citing.
4. Write for your real peers — the readers in your field — and the deans and committees will follow.
5. Finishing the book only fills the toolbox; build mastery by actually using the tools and deepening your insight over a career.
6. Let quality drive quantity: strong work attracts the students and collaborators who produce more work.
