# Chapter 3: Making a Story Sticky

## Core Idea
Memorable, often-cited papers are "sticky" — and stickiness is engineered, not lucky. Schimel imports the Heath brothers' **SUCCES** checklist (from *Made to Stick*) and translates each element into science-writing terms, with the *simple story* and the *knowledge gap* as the two highest-leverage moves.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **SUCCES** (Simple, Unexpected, Concrete, Credible, Emotional, Stories): the six factors that make an idea stick.
  - When to use: *before* drafting — decide how each element will be woven in, especially the simple story.
  - How: (1) **Simple** — find the *core* of the problem and express it in one compact idea (Darwin's natural selection, "it's the economy, stupid"); (2) **Unexpected** — frame a *knowledge gap*, not a data dump; (3) **Concrete** — ground abstractions in data or familiar images; (4) **Credible** — build an unbroken chain from prior work → methods → data → conclusions; (5) **Emotional** — engage *curiosity* (the one acceptable scientific emotion); (6) **Stories** — assemble the paper from modular sub-stories.
  - Why it works / failure mode: simple ≠ simplistic. Simplistic *dodges* the core ("you pay too much in taxes"); simple *targets* it. A break anywhere in the credibility chain forfeits the whole.
- **Knowledge-gap theory of curiosity** (from Loewenstein via Heath & Heath): curiosity comes from realizing you *lack* a specific piece of knowledge.
  - When to use: building the introduction / framing the question.
  - How: use what *is* known to draw the boundaries of what is *unknown*; "frame a window" around the gap you will fill. Identifying the gap creates curiosity; filling it creates novelty.
  - Why it works / failure mode: showing off what you know does *not* create curiosity — "First… they must realize they need [the facts]." Junior authors over-display knowledge and kill engagement.
- **Schemas**: prior mental structures readers already hold; new ideas stick when attached to them ("tastes like chicken, but meatier").
  - How: learn which schemas your *target* audience holds, then build from them; redefine in more broadly held schemas to reach beyond a narrow subdiscipline.
- **Ladder of Abstraction** (Roy Peter Clark): a continuum from broad simple ideas (top) to physical data (bottom); the **middle rungs are the danger zone** — expert-only jargon (sexual selection, ribozymes, "multimedia modeling").
  - How: you can't avoid middle rungs, but ground them in a top-rung schema or a bottom-rung concrete detail.

## Key Concepts
- **Sticky / "papers with legs"**: work that retains impact and keeps accruing citations for years.
- **Simple vs. simplistic**: simple captures the essence; simplistic trivializes or dodges it.
- **Curiosity**: the fundamental scientific emotion; excitement (its cousin) is what wins a proposal an advocate.
- **Story modules**: a large story built from threaded smaller story units, each with its own characters.

## Mental Models
- Think of stickiness as **engineering, not talent** — run SUCCES like a checklist before you write.
- Use the **knowledge-gap** lens: lead with the *question* ("what's my question?"), not the answer ("what's my answer?").
- Use the **ladder of abstraction** to diagnose jargon: if a term sits on a middle rung, anchor it up or down.
- Treat **curiosity → excitement** as the path from "fundable" to "funded": you need one panelist excited enough to advocate for you.

## Anti-patterns
- **Simplistic messaging**: dumbing down or dodging the core instead of distilling it.
- **Data dumps / showing off knowledge**: facts before the reader feels the need for them — no curiosity, forgettable.
- **Living on the middle of the ladder**: writing in expert-only abstractions, accessible to a narrow clique.
- **Hype over substance**: "with that much lipstick, the proposal had to be a pig" — boldface and buzzwords destroy credibility because they aren't concrete.

## Worked Example
Schimel's freeze-thaw paper is his worked lesson in *simple vs. simplistic*. Initially, freeze-thaw cycles raised respiration in some soils and lowered it in others with "no pattern" — and that inconsistency was the story in the first submission. Reviewers called it simplistic and "said so in no uncertain terms. They were right." After weeks of head-banging, he found the genuinely simple story: in **rich soils freeze-thaw reduced respiration; in poor soils it enhanced it** — a coherent pattern suggesting testable mechanisms. That "what an idiot!" reframing turned a rejected, patternless result into a paper cited 100+ times. The moral he draws: tough reviewers who force you to find the simple story in complex data are doing you a favor — "the pain of an embarrassing review lasts a few days, the pain of an embarrassing paper lasts a lifetime."

## Key Takeaways
1. Run **SUCCES** as a pre-writing checklist; invest most in finding the *simple story*.
2. Distinguish simple (targets the core) from simplistic (dodges it).
3. Frame a **knowledge gap** to create curiosity — lead with the question, not the answer.
4. Build from your **audience's schemas**; redefine narrow terms in broadly held ones.
5. Keep concepts off the middle of the **ladder of abstraction** — anchor them up or down.
6. Maintain an unbroken **credibility chain**; concreteness *is* credibility.
7. Engineer curiosity → excitement; for proposals, you need an advocate on the panel.
