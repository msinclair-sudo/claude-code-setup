# Cheatsheet — Writing Science (Schimel)

## Decision rules

| When… | Do… | Because… |
|---|---|---|
| Writing for a **specialist journal** | Use full **OCAR** (resolution last) | Patient readers distrust front-loaded "proof" — they want to watch you *test* |
| Writing for **Nature/Science / generalist** | Use **LDR** (lead → develop → resolve) | The editor triages; you must hook fast but readers still finish |
| Writing a **proposal** | Use **ABDCE / front-load** | Reviewers decide by the end of page 2 — "if you haven't told them, you haven't told them" |
| Writing for the **public / a reporter** | **Message box** + LDR, lead from their schema | Impatient, no shared jargon; payoff is *application*, not insight |
| A reader could read **only O, C, R** | Make sure those three alone convey your message | Readers *do* read just the power positions |
| Stating your question | "**To learn X, we did Y**" (question before objectives) | Objectives-first reads as a chore list and buries the stake |
| Reporting a number | Lead with **effect size**, append p-value | A p-value is credibility, not a finding |
| You hit a **real limitation** | "**But, yes**" — raise it early, end on the science | "Yes, but" makes "more research is needed" your lasting message |
| A method can't answer the question | **Revise the question** in the intro | Better to fill a small gap than to claim a big one you can't |
| Editing a draft | **SCFL**: Story → Clear → Fluent → Language, top-down, cyclic | Polishing words you'll cut is wasted effort |
| A setup sentence is weak | **Cut the pawn, launch the queen** | The strong sentence should hold the power position |

## Sentence-level placement (topic / stress)

- **Topic position (start)** → old/familiar info, the character you already introduced. *Never* put a new technical term here.
- **Stress position (end of main clause)** → the new idea, the key term, the main message.
- **Flow** → the **stress of sentence N feeds the topic of N+1** ("reach back and grab"). Stress→topic = story; topic→topic = list.
- **2-3-1 emphasis** (Clark): strongest last (1), next first (2), weakest in the middle (3).

## Verb tells & smells (energizing — Ch 14)

| Smell | Symptom | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| **Passive voice** | "was measured by us" | actor-action-acted-on: "we measured" |
| **Fuzzy verb** | is / makes / performs / conducts | name the real action verb |
| **Nominalization** | "adjustment," "responsiveness," "the regulation of" | turn the noun back into a verb |
| Action hidden in an **adjective** | "plants are responsive" | "plants respond" |

## The two roots (everything reduces to these)
- **OCAR** = story structure (Opening, Challenge, Action, Resolution) — honor at every scale.
- **SUCCES** = stickiness (Simple, Unexpected, Concrete, Credible, Emotional, Stories).
- Every other rule in the book is an application of one of these two.

## Simple vs. simplistic
- **Simple** = finds and states the *core* of the problem. ✓
- **Simplistic** = dumbs down, trivializes, or *dodges* the core. ✗
- Test: does it target the hard question, or avoid it?

## Knowledge gap (curiosity engine)
- Frame the **unknown inside the known** ("frame a window").
- Identifying the gap → curiosity. Filling it → novelty.
- Lead with the **question**, not the answer. Showing off facts kills curiosity.

## Ladder of abstraction
- Top = broad simple ideas (natural selection) — everyone gets it.
- Bottom = concrete data — everyone gets it.
- **Middle = danger zone** = expert-only jargon. Anchor middle-rung terms *up* (to a schema) or *down* (to a concrete detail).

## Condensing — the delete-key targets (Ch 16)
Empty amplifiers (very, really, quite) · redundancies · throat-clearing ("It is important to note that…") · dead weight. **Prune, then shake.** Watch for noun trains when over-trimming.

## Final check
Read it **aloud, standing up, from a clean printout** — your ear catches what your eye skates over.
