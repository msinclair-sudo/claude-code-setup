# Chapter 16: Condensing

## Core Idea
Tight, sleek writing isn't optional for scientists — it's survival, because proposals have hard page limits and bloated papers get rejected. Real condensing comes from selection, not formatting tricks: prune ideas you don't need, repair story arcs to kill repeats, then attack leftover filler with the delete key.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Prune, then shake** (Roy Peter Clark's "Prune the big limbs, then shake out the dead leaves"): a two-stage strategy where "brevity comes from selection, not compression."
  - When to use: any over-length draft (Schimel's NSF proposals start at 18 pages, must hit 15).
  - How: (1) Prune the big limbs — figure out what you don't need to say, then don't say it; this grows directly from SUCCES and your simple story. (2) Shake the dead leaves — fix broken story arcs to eliminate repeats and transition words, then cut filler word-by-word.
  - Why it works: lifting whole blocks (selection) recovers far more than squeezing sentences (compression). Broken arcs are inefficient — three complete arcs need 2 internal transitions; fragmented, they need 13.

- **The four (five) targets for the delete key**: a checklist of word-types to hunt — Redundancies, Obvious, Modifiers (adjectives/adverbs), Metadiscourse, Verbosity.
  - When to use: line-editing pass after structure is fixed.
  - How: ask of each word — does it add content, clarify meaning, or provide coherence? If not, it's a "slacker." Redundancy duplicates info within a passage; Obvious is well-known/implied so needn't be said anywhere; Metadiscourse discusses the discussion; Verbosity is the sum of all filler.
  - Failure mode: skipping this feels like saving time but squanders it via rejection.

- **Empty amplifiers**: adjectives/adverbs that try to intensify their referent but add no meaning (certainly, dramatically, entirely, highly, quite, rather, really, simply, substantially, very). Delete them and the important thought remains. Worse, modifiers can *hide* empty thoughts — strip them and find no substance underneath.

- **Good modifiers**: words that don't just reinforce but clarify, define, or *alter* meaning ("first phase," "preliminary result," "she smiled sadly"). These you cannot delete.

## Key Concepts
- **Selection vs. compression**: cut whole blocks of unneeded ideas rather than merely squeezing surviving sentences.
- **Formatting tricks**: shrinking fonts/figures to force words onto the page — produces a dense unreadable mass; a hurdle to enlisting the reader as your advocate.
- **Redundancy**: several words doing the work of one, or the same idea repeated across sentences.
- **Obvious**: information so well known or implied it needs saying nowhere ("There is evidence that," "in this study").
- **Metadiscourse**: discussing the discussion ("We found that," "We argue that") — some is needed for flow, much is deletable.
- **Empty amplifier**: an intensifier carrying no concrete meaning ("dramatically increased").
- **Verbosity / obese words**: rambling sentences from insecurity or inability to separate one's mental process from the story.
- **Second draft = first draft – 10%** (Stephen King): Schimel trims 20%.
- **Literary trash compactor**: Schimel's self-description; his game — cut a paragraph's dangling bottom line back into the body, since killing one word can save a whole line.

## Mental Models
- Use **"prune, then shake"** when a draft is over length — select what to cut before compressing what stays.
- Use the **delete-key test** ("does this word add content, clarify, or cohere?") on every word; if none, cut it.
- Use the **modifier-stripping test** when prose feels padded — if removing the adjective/adverb leaves a hole, the missing thing is concrete substance you should have supplied, not the modifier.
- Use **active verbs to condense**: converting passives, fuzzies, and nominalizations to verbs makes sentences shorter *and* stronger; eliminating metadiscourse also sidesteps the active/passive-voice argument entirely.

## Anti-patterns
- **Formatting tricks instead of editing**: smaller fonts, unbulleted hypotheses, shrunken figures — forces words onto 15 pages but signals you lacked the skill or inclination to condense.
- **"I have too much to say"**: Schimel's verdict — "Rubbish." He's never seen dense writing that couldn't be streamlined without cutting substance.
- **Decoration replacing content**: "highly effective... efficiently discriminates" hypes instead of telling us *what* the mechanism is — an empty pawn push where a queen launch with intellectual meat belongs.
- **Caveat-by-adverb**: "dramatically" or "There is evidence that" — vague hedges; the word "can" already supplies the caveat, and a factor of 42 already proves the increase is dramatic.

## Worked Example
The 260-word tundra-N paragraph (Ex. 16.18), condensed sentence by sentence:
- "A central dogma of ecology has long been..." → "Ecological dogma has been..." (dogma is always central and implies long duration; "plants to take up" → "plants," since plants do nothing else with N — obvious).
- "several lines of evidence have forced us to question the importance of microbial decomposition and inorganic-N uptake by plants" → "several lines of evidence challenge this" (cut metadiscourse; encapsulate the prior concept in "this").
- Two weak microbial-process sentences (growing-season uptake vs. total annual release) collapse into one strong one: "Microbes release only half the inorganic N required to support measured plant growth" — taking "plant growth" from the first sentence, the core message from the second.
- "have been shown in lab studies to be able to take up..." → "can take up and grow on amino acids" (cut "have been shown"; collapse explanation into "can").
- Closing sentence: "we must reevaluate our basic view of the central role..." — "our basic view" is implied (nature doesn't change, only our perception) and "central" is empty.

The point: none of the originals was *bad*, yet the paragraph still halved without losing meaning.

## Key Takeaways
1. Condense by selection first (prune whole ideas via your SUCCES story), then by compression — never reach for smaller fonts.
2. Fix story arcs before line-editing; complete arcs erase the repeats and transitions that fragmented arcs multiply.
3. Run every draft through the five targets: redundancies, obvious, modifiers, metadiscourse, verbosity.
4. Delete empty amplifiers (very, dramatically, highly); if the sentence then feels empty, supply concrete numbers/specifics instead — that hole is the real content.
5. Keep modifiers that clarify or alter meaning ("preliminary," "first"); cut ones that merely reinforce.
6. Cut metadiscourse ("We found that," "in this study") — it's usually obvious and dodges the active/passive debate.
7. Budget a 10–20% cut from every first draft; treat it as buying acceptance, not spending time.
